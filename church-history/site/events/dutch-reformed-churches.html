Dutch Reformed Churches: Beginnings and Splits

1561–2022 · Reformation

How Calvinist churches took root in the Netherlands under Spanish persecution, became the public church of the Dutch Republic, spread to North America and South Africa, and divided repeatedly over doctrine, church order, race, and sexuality.

Began
1561[1][6]
Ended
2021–2022[6]
Place
The Netherlands, North America, and South Africa

Background

  • Calvinist teaching spread in the Low Countries from the 1540s, in congregations meeting secretly or in exile under the Habsburg rulers' heresy laws.[1][6]

What happened

  • The Belgic Confession (1561), the Heidelberg Catechism, and later the Canons of Dort became the 'Three Forms of Unity', the doctrinal standards of Dutch Reformed churches worldwide.[1][6]
  • After the revolt against Spain, the Reformed church became the public church of the Dutch Republic, though Catholics, Mennonites, and others were tolerated in varying degrees.[1]
  • Dutch trade and settlement carried the church to New Netherland (later the Reformed Church in America) and the Cape (the Dutch Reformed Church in South Africa).[3][5]
  • From the 19th century the Dutch churches divided again and again over orthodoxy against liberal theology, state control, and church order; in South Africa the church divided by race and supported apartheid.[2][3][5]

Key participants

  • John Calvin Reformer whose theology the churches follow
  • Guido de Brès Author of the Belgic Confession; executed 1567
  • Abraham Kuyper Leader of the Doleantie and later prime minister
  • Klaas Schilder Leader of the 1944 Liberation

Outcomes and significance

  • Dutch Reformed churches number many millions worldwide, but are divided into dozens of denominations in the Netherlands, North America, and southern Africa.[6]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Dutch Calvinists were persecuted under Habsburg rule, and later persecuted dissenters within their own tradition.

1550s–1570s

Habsburg persecution of the Reformed in the Netherlands

Under Charles V's and Philip II's heresy edicts, Protestants were burned, beheaded, or drowned. Guido de Brès was hanged at Valenciennes in May 1567 after the town's surrender, and the Duke of Alba's Council of Troubles (1567–76) condemned some 9,000 people, executing over 1,000.[1][6]
