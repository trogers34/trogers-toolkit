Nicholas of Myra

c. 270–c. 343 · Early Church

Also known as Saint Nicholas; Nicholas the Wonderworker; the origin of Santa Claus

Bishop of Myra in Lycia

Fourth-century bishop of Myra about whom very little is known for certain, but whose legends of secret generosity and miracles made him one of the most popular saints in both East and West, and eventually the model for Santa Claus.

Born
c. 270 (traditional; not recorded) · Patara, Lycia, Asia Minor (modern Turkey), according to tradition[2][4]
Died
6 December, c. 343 (traditional) · Myra, Lycia (modern Demre, Turkey)[2][4]

What they did

  • Served as bishop of Myra in Lycia in the early 4th century. Almost nothing about his life is documented by contemporaries; the earliest detailed accounts were written centuries later.[2][3][4]
  • Is remembered, in the best-known legend, for secretly giving three bags of gold as dowries so that three poor sisters would not be sold into prostitution, the origin of the custom of secret gift-giving in his name.[1][2]
  • Is credited in an early story (the Praxis de stratelatis, c. 5th–6th century) with saving three innocent men from execution and rescuing three imperial officers who had been unjustly condemned.[1][2]
  • Became one of the most widely venerated saints of the Middle Ages, patron of sailors, children, merchants, and many cities. His relics were taken from Myra to Bari, Italy, by Italian sailors in 1087, where they remain.[3][4]
  • His feast day, 6 December, and the Dutch figure of Sinterklaas developed in America into Santa Claus.[3][4]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No contemporary record shows Nicholas persecuting anyone. Later legends credit him with two hostile acts: slapping or striking Arius at the Council of Nicaea (325), and destroying pagan temples in his region, including the temple of Artemis at Myra. Historians treat both stories as legend rather than history.

Striking Arius at Nicaea (legend)

Carried it out

Against: Arius

A late legend, not found in early accounts of the Council of Nicaea, says Nicholas grew so angry at Arius during the debates that he struck him, and that the council removed him from office and jailed him until a vision restored him. Historians regard the story as legend; Nicholas's presence at Nicaea is itself uncertain.[2][3]

Outcome: In the legend, Nicholas was briefly deposed and imprisoned, then restored.

early 4th century

Destroying pagan temples (legend)

Carried it out

Against: Pagan worshippers of Lycia; the temple of Artemis at Myra

The Life of Nicholas by Michael the Archimandrite (9th century) says he tore down pagan temples and shrines in and around Myra, chief among them the temple of Artemis. This is a later hagiographical account with no contemporary support, and historians treat it as legend.[1][2]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

The record for Nicholas rests on later tradition, not contemporary documents. Tradition says he was imprisoned during the Great Persecution under Diocletian and released when Constantine's policy of toleration took effect. A late legend says that he struck the heretic Arius at the Council of Nicaea and was briefly stripped of his office and jailed by the council, but historians regard this story as legend.

Excommunications

No excommunication of Nicholas is recorded. The legend that the Council of Nicaea removed him from office for striking Arius is not historical.

c. 303–311 (according to tradition)

Imprisoned during the Great Persecution

According to his traditional biographies, Nicholas was imprisoned during the empire-wide persecution of Christians launched by Emperor Diocletian in 303, and freed when the persecution ended. No contemporary record confirms this.[2][1]
