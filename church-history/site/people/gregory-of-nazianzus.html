Gregory of Nazianzus

c. 329–c. 390 · Early Church

Also known as Gregory the Theologian; Gregory Nazianzen; one of the Cappadocian Fathers

Theologian, poet, and briefly bishop of Constantinople; friend of Basil the Great

The orator of the Cappadocian Fathers, whose Five Theological Orations (380), preached in Constantinople, gave classic form to the doctrine of the Trinity and earned him the title 'the Theologian' in the Eastern church. A reluctant churchman who loved solitude and literature, he was ordained against his will, attacked by opponents in Constantinople, made bishop there with imperial backing, and forced to resign during the Council of Constantinople in 381.

What they did

  • Defined the doctrine of the Trinity in the Five Theological Orations (380), defending the full divinity of the Son and especially of the Holy Spirit; the Eastern church calls him 'the Theologian' for them.[1][4]
  • Argued against Apollinarius that Christ had a full human mind and soul, in the principle that 'what has not been assumed has not been healed'.[1][3]
  • Rebuilt the Nicene community in Constantinople from a house chapel, the Anastasia, before the city's churches were handed over to the Nicenes by Theodosius.[3][5]
  • Wrote some 17,000 lines of poetry, including the autobiographical On His Own Life, and many letters; he is one of the great Greek stylists of late antiquity.[2][4]
  • Compiled with Basil the Philokalia, an anthology of Origen's writings.[3][5]
  • Preached on care for the poor and the sick, especially lepers, in his oration On Love for the Poor (Oration 14).[1][4]
  • Wrote two bitter invectives against the emperor Julian after his death (Orations 4–5).[1][3]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Gregory did not order violence, but his installation as bishop of Constantinople was part of Theodosius's expulsion of the anti-Nicene clergy from the city's churches, and he later asked the authorities to stop the Apollinarians from holding meetings. He also wrote fierce invectives against Julian and against Arian opponents.

November 380

Installed in the cathedral after the expulsion of the Homoian clergy

Took part

Against: Bishop Demophilus and the Homoian (anti-Nicene) clergy and congregations of Constantinople

When Theodosius ordered the Homoian bishop Demophilus to accept the Nicene creed or leave, Demophilus left, and Theodosius led Gregory into the Church of the Holy Apostles under armed guard. Gregory describes the tense scene in On His Own Life.[2][3]

Outcome: The Homoians lost their churches in Constantinople.

c. 383

Called for restraints on the Apollinarians

Called for it

Against: Followers of Apollinarius of Laodicea

In a letter to Nectarius, his successor at Constantinople (Letter 202), he complained that the Apollinarians were being allowed to hold assemblies and urged that this be stopped.[1][3]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

In Constantinople Gregory faced violence from opponents, an attempt to replace him with a rival bishop, and a campaign that forced his resignation.

Excommunications

Gregory was never excommunicated or condemned. He resigned his see in 381 under pressure, but no council deposed him, and he is honoured as a saint and Doctor of the Church.

Easter 379

Attack on the Anastasia

During the Easter vigil, a crowd of his anti-Nicene opponents, reportedly including monks and poor people incited by Homoian clergy, broke into the Anastasia and threw stones; one of his followers was killed or badly hurt, and Gregory was later accused in court of causing the disturbance.[2][3]

380

Attempted replacement by Maximus the Cynic

Maximus, a philosopher Gregory had befriended, was secretly consecrated bishop of Constantinople by Egyptian bishops sent by Peter of Alexandria, in an attempt to take over the see. The plot failed, but it humiliated Gregory.[2][3]

381

Forced resignation at the Council of Constantinople

Egyptian and Macedonian bishops argued that his move from Sasima to Constantinople broke the canon against bishops changing sees, and others resented his policies at the council. He resigned rather than continue the dispute.[1][3]
