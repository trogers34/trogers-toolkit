Benedictus Deus

1331–1336 · Medieval

Also known as Constitution of Pope Benedict XII on the beatific vision, 1336

Pope Benedict XII's definition that the souls of the saved see God face to face immediately after death (or after purgatory), settling a controversy started by his predecessor John XXII's own sermons.

Began
1 November 1331 (John XXII's first sermon)[3][2]
Ended
29 January 1336[1][4]
Place
Avignon
Agreed
Dogma: the souls of the saved see God face to face immediately after death or purification, before the resurrection; the damned go to hell at once.

Background

  • In sermons from November 1331, Pope John XXII taught that the souls of the saints do not see the divine essence until the Last Judgment, seeing only Christ's humanity until then, and that the damned do not go to hell until then either.[3][2]
  • Theologians at Paris and elsewhere objected, and John's enemies, including the dissident Franciscans Michael of Cesena and William of Ockham in Munich, used the sermons to call him a heretic.[2][3]

What happened

  • In December 1333 a meeting of theologians at Vincennes, called by King Philip VI of France, rejected the pope's view.[2]
  • On 3 December 1334, the day before he died, John XXII retracted, saying the purified souls see God's essence 'as far as their state and condition allow'.[2][3]
  • His successor Benedict XII, a former inquisitor who had written against John's view, defined in Benedictus Deus that the souls of the saved, once purified, see the divine essence immediately, before the resurrection of the body, and that the souls of those who die in mortal sin go immediately to hell.[1][3]

Key participants

  • John XXII Pope whose sermons started the controversy
  • Benedict XII (Jacques Fournier) Pope who issued the definition
  • William of Ockham Franciscan philosopher who, from exile in Munich, denounced John XXII as a heretic, partly over these sermons
  • Thomas Waleys English Dominican imprisoned for preaching against the pope's view

Outcomes and significance

  • The case is often cited in debates over papal infallibility, since a pope publicly taught a view his successor then defined as contrary to the faith; Catholic theologians note that John taught it as a private opinion and retracted.[3][4]
  • Eastern Orthodox teaching, which speaks of a fuller glory only after the resurrection, does not accept the definition in the same form, and the question arose again at the Council of Florence.[2][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The one well-documented punishment in the controversy fell on a critic of the pope.

January 1333 – c. 1342

Imprisonment of Thomas Waleys

The English Dominican Thomas Waleys preached against John XXII's view in Avignon in January 1333. He was arrested, tried by the papal inquisitor, and imprisoned; he remained in custody for years, into Benedict XII's reign, and was released only around 1342.[2]
