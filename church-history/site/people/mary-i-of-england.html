Mary I of England

1516–1558 · Reformation

Also known as Mary Tudor; Bloody Mary

Queen of England and Ireland (1553–1558), the first queen regnant crowned in England; wife of Philip II of Spain

Daughter of Henry VIII and Catherine of Aragon who, after being declared illegitimate and pressed to renounce her faith under her father and brother, became queen in 1553, reunited the English church with Rome, and revived the heresy laws under which about 280 Protestants were burned, earning her the later name "Bloody Mary."

What they did

  • Won the throne in July 1553 against the attempt by John Dudley, Duke of Northumberland, to crown the Protestant Lady Jane Grey, and became the first woman crowned queen regnant of England.[3][4][8]
  • Under Mary the swing went back to Rome: Edward VI's English Prayer Book was abolished, the Latin Mass, altars, and images returned, married priests were removed from their parishes, and papal authority was restored, until her half-sister Elizabeth I reversed it again in 1559.[5][6]
  • Restored Catholic worship. Parliament repealed Edward VI's religious laws in 1553, the Latin Mass returned, and married clergy were removed from their posts.[5][3][6]
  • Reunited the Church of England with Rome. On 30 November 1554 the papal legate Cardinal Reginald Pole absolved the kingdom, and the Second Act of Repeal (1555) undid Henry VIII's anti-papal laws, though former monastic lands were left with their new owners.[5][2][3]
  • Revived the medieval heresy laws (1555). Between February 1555 and November 1558 about 280 Protestants were burned, among them Archbishop Thomas Cranmer and Bishops Hugh Latimer, Nicholas Ridley, and John Hooper, and many ordinary men and women; others died in prison and some 800 fled abroad.[5][1][8]
  • Supported a program of Catholic renewal under Pole: new preaching and catechetical books such as Bishop Edmund Bonner's homilies, a legatine synod (1555–1556) with plans for diocesan seminaries, and the refounding of some religious houses such as Westminster Abbey.[5][6]
  • Married Philip of Spain (later Philip II) in 1554, a match that provoked Sir Thomas Wyatt's rebellion. After the rising was crushed, Lady Jane Grey, her husband, and her father were executed, along with about 90 rebels.[3][4]
  • Took England into Spain's war with France (1557), which led to the loss of Calais in January 1558. Her two apparent pregnancies (1554–1555 and 1557–1558) produced no child, and the crown passed to her Protestant half-sister Elizabeth.[3][8]
  • Became known as "Bloody Mary" through the Protestant memory of the burnings, shaped above all by John Foxe's Acts and Monuments (1563). Recent historians such as Eamon Duffy and Linda Porter have reassessed her reign, while acknowledging that the burnings were exceptional in English history.[1][5][4]

  • "Bloody Mary" Later Protestant nickname[5][8]

    Not her words. The name comes from later Protestant tradition built on Foxe's account of the burnings; it is also used for an unrelated cocktail and folklore figure.

  • "Mary, Mary, quite contrary" English nursery rhyme, first printed in the 18th century[7]

    Often said to be about Mary I and her executions, but there is no evidence connecting the rhyme to her; the link is a later theory.

Notable people executed on Mary I's authority

About 280 people (Eamon Duffy counts 284) were burned for heresy in Mary's reign, the great majority ordinary laypeople in London, Kent, Essex, and East Anglia; others died in prison. In addition, Lady Jane Grey and about 90 rebels were executed after Wyatt's rebellion in 1554. Heresy cases were tried by bishops and papal commissioners and the burnings carried out by the secular authorities under the revived heresy laws, with the queen's warrant. Historians agree that Mary supported the burnings and refused to stop them; how far she, Cardinal Pole, Bishop Stephen Gardiner, Bishop Edmund Bonner, and the council each drove them is debated. Persecution, arrests, and executions

Before she became queen, Mary was declared illegitimate, separated from her mother, and pressed under threat of treason charges to accept her father's supremacy over the church, which she did in 1536. Under her Protestant brother Edward VI she was pressured to give up the Mass, and her household officers were jailed. Edward's will tried to exclude her from the throne. She was never imprisoned or excommunicated.

Excommunications

Mary was never excommunicated. She shared in the schism of her father's church after her forced submission of 1536, but the realm, including her, was formally absolved and reconciled to Rome by Cardinal Pole on 30 November 1554. She died in communion with the Roman Catholic Church.

1533–1536

Declared illegitimate and separated from her mother

After Cranmer declared Henry's marriage to Catherine of Aragon void (1533), Mary lost the title of princess and was declared illegitimate by the Act of Succession (1534). Her household was broken up, she was made to serve in the household of the infant Elizabeth, and she was not allowed to see her mother, who died in 1536.[3][4]

June 1536

Forced submission to her father's supremacy

After Anne Boleyn's fall, Mary still refused to accept Henry as head of the church or her own illegitimacy. A council delegation led by the Duke of Norfolk threatened her, judges advised that she could be proceeded against for treason, and her supporters were questioned. Advised by the imperial ambassador Eustace Chapuys to give way, she signed the submission.[3][4]

1549–1551

Pressure to abandon the Mass under Edward VI

After the Act of Uniformity (1549) made the Book of Common Prayer compulsory, Mary continued to have Mass said in her household. In 1550 she considered fleeing to the Continent. In March 1551 Edward VI and his council confronted her; in August 1551 her chief officers, Robert Rochester, Edward Waldegrave, and Francis Englefield, were sent to the Tower for refusing to stop the Masses in her house.[3][4]

June–July 1553

Excluded from the throne by Edward VI's "Devise"

Edward VI, with the Duke of Northumberland, set aside both his half-sisters in favor of Lady Jane Grey, in large part to keep a Catholic from the throne. After Edward's death on 6 July, Jane was proclaimed queen and forces were sent to capture Mary.[3][8]
