James VI and I

1566–1625 · Reformation

Also known as James Charles Stuart; James VI of Scotland; James I of England and Ireland

King of Scots (1567–1625) and King of England and Ireland (1603–1625); Supreme Governor of the Church of England

Son of Mary, Queen of Scots and Lord Darnley, baptized a Catholic but raised a Protestant, who became King of Scots as an infant in 1567, struggled for decades with Andrew Melville and the Presbyterian ministers over control of the Kirk and restored bishops in Scotland, and in 1603 succeeded Elizabeth I as King of England. A writer on divine-right kingship and on witchcraft, he personally examined accused witches in the North Berwick trials, survived the Catholic Gunpowder Plot, imposed an Oath of Allegiance on Catholics, commissioned the King James Bible, and allowed the last burnings for heresy in England (1612).

What they did

  • The religious swings of his life ran differently from the Tudors'. He was baptized in the Catholic rite at Stirling Castle in December 1566 by his Catholic mother, but after her deposition (1567) he was raised Protestant under the regents and his tutor George Buchanan. Scotland's Kirk, Protestant since 1560 under his mother's reluctant rule, swung between Presbyterian government by ministers and elders and government by bishops: he upheld bishops in 1584 (Black Acts), conceded Presbyterianism in 1592 (Golden Act), and then restored bishops step by step (1600–1610). In England, where he followed Elizabeth I in 1603, the church stayed Protestant and episcopal; he refused the Puritans' main demands but also kept the laws against Catholics. He was followed in both kingdoms by his son Charles I.[8][11][9][20]
  • Changed parish worship in Scotland through the Five Articles of Perth (1618, ratified by Parliament 1621): kneeling to receive communion, private communion for the sick, private baptism, confirmation by bishops, and keeping Christmas, Good Friday, Easter, Ascension, and Pentecost. Many ministers and congregations resisted, and some who refused were deprived or punished.[11][12]
  • Restored bishops in Scotland: ministers were made bishops to sit in Parliament from 1600, a General Assembly at Glasgow (1610) accepted their authority over church courts, and three of them were consecrated in England in 1610. The Presbyterian courts (kirk sessions, presbyteries, synods) continued under the bishops.[11][12][19]
  • Set out a theory of divine-right kingship in The True Law of Free Monarchies (1598) and Basilikon Doron (1599, a book of advice to his son Prince Henry), arguing that kings answer to God alone and that subjects may not resist even a bad king; Basilikon Doron also attacked the Presbyterian ministers he called "Puritans" as "very pests in the Church and Commonweal".[1][9]
  • Took a personal part in the North Berwick witch trials (1590–1591), questioning accused witches such as Agnes Sampson at Holyroodhouse, where she was said to have told him words he had spoken to his bride on their wedding night. He defended the reality of witchcraft and the duty to punish it in Daemonologie (1597). Later in life he exposed several false accusations, including the Leicester case of 1616, in which nine people had already been hanged on a boy's testimony.[3][4][2][16]
  • At the Hampton Court Conference (January 1604) heard Puritan requests from the Millenary Petition, made minor changes to the Prayer Book, rejected Presbyterian government ("No bishop, no king"), and accepted John Rainolds's proposal for a new translation of the Bible. Archbishop Richard Bancroft then enforced the Canons of 1604, and some 80 or more ministers who refused to conform were deprived.[5][13][9]
  • Sponsored the Authorized Version of the Bible (1611), translated by about fifty scholars in six companies under rules drawn up by Bancroft that kept traditional church words and forbade partisan marginal notes; it became the standard English Bible for some three centuries.[15][20]
  • After the Gunpowder Plot (1605), approved the Oath of Allegiance (1606), which required Catholics to deny that the pope could depose kings. Pope Paul V forbade Catholics to take it (1606 and 1607), and James defended it in print against Cardinal Robert Bellarmine in Triplici nodo, triplex cuneus, or An Apology for the Oath of Allegiance (1607–1609).[1][10][14]
  • Took part in Reformed theology abroad: he pressed the Dutch to dismiss the theologian Conrad Vorstius from Leiden (1611–1612) and had his books burned in England, and sent a delegation of British divines (George Carleton, John Davenant, Samuel Ward, Joseph Hall, later replaced by Thomas Goad, and the Scot Walter Balcanqual) to the Synod of Dort (1618–1619). He also hoped for a general council to reunite Christendom.[10][9]
  • Issued the Declaration of Sports (1617 for Lancashire, 1618 for all England), allowing dancing, archery, and other recreations after Sunday service, against Puritan Sabbath views. His close attachments to male favourites, notably Esmé Stewart, Robert Carr, and George Villiers, drew comment then and since; historians differ on whether the relationships were sexual.[9][8]

Persecution they supported or carried out

James took a personal part in witch trials in Scotland, questioning accused people at the North Berwick trials, and wrote a defence of witch-hunting, though he later grew sceptical and exposed several false accusations. He allowed the last two burnings for heresy in England (1612), kept and added to the laws against Catholics, especially after the Gunpowder Plot, and imprisoned or exiled Presbyterian ministers who opposed his church policy in Scotland. He was also milder than many contemporaries in some respects: fewer Catholics were executed in his reign than under Elizabeth I, and he often protected the Catholic Scottish earls and preferred fines and banishment to death.

1590–1597

Scottish witch hunts

Took part

Against: People accused of witchcraft in East Lothian, Edinburgh, and elsewhere in Scotland

Besides questioning the North Berwick accused, James had the jurors who acquitted Barbara Napier in 1591 prosecuted for wilful error and spoke to them in the Tolbooth on the duty to punish witches. In 1597 standing royal commissions to try witches led to a second wave of trials, until the Privy Council revoked the commissions later that year after abuses. Others responsible included the Privy Council, local magistrates, and the Kirk's ministers and sessions, which pressed for prosecutions. In England the Witchcraft Act of 1604, passed by Parliament early in his reign, widened the penalties of the 1563 act.[4][16][17][2]

Outcome: James grew more sceptical in later life and intervened in several English cases of fraudulent possession and accusation (Anne Gunter, 1605; the Leicester boy, 1616).

March–April 1612

Last heresy burnings in England

Ordered or signed it

Against: Bartholomew Legate and Edward Wightman, anti-Trinitarians

The king issued writs for both burnings after their condemnation by the bishops of London (John King) and Coventry and Lichfield (Richard Neile), with the support of Archbishop George Abbot. At least one other anti-Trinitarian condemned at about the same time was kept in prison instead of burned, reportedly because the burnings had aroused public sympathy.[18][7]

Outcome: No one was burned for heresy in England after 1612.

1604–1625

Laws and penalties against Catholics

Ordered or signed it

Against: English and Scottish Catholics, priests, and Jesuits

After a brief easing of recusancy fines in 1603, James ordered Jesuits and seminary priests out of England (proclamation of 1604) and the fines were renewed. After the Gunpowder Plot the Popish Recusants Act (1606) imposed the Oath of Allegiance and new penalties, and the 1610 act extended the oath. Around two dozen Catholics, most of them priests, were executed in his reign. Parliament, the Privy Council, Robert Cecil, Earl of Salisbury, and the bishops shared responsibility.[14][10][9]

1584–1611

Imprisonment and exile of Presbyterian ministers

Ordered or signed it

Against: Andrew Melville, his nephew James Melville, John Welsh, John Forbes, and other Scottish ministers

The Black Acts (1584) led Andrew Melville and other ministers to flee to England. In 1606 six ministers, including John Welsh and John Forbes, were convicted of treason at Linlithgow for holding a General Assembly at Aberdeen (1605) against the king's prohibition and were banished. The same year Andrew and James Melville and other ministers were summoned to London; Andrew Melville was sent to the Tower of London (1607–1611) over a Latin epigram mocking worship in the Chapel Royal and then exiled to Sedan in France, where he died in 1622, and James Melville was confined in the north of England. The Privy Council of Scotland and the archbishops of St Andrews and Glasgow (George Gladstanes, John Spottiswoode) carried out the policy.[6][11][12]

1604–1609

Deprivation of Puritan ministers in England

Approved or defended it

Against: English ministers who refused to subscribe to the Canons of 1604

After the Hampton Court Conference, Archbishop Richard Bancroft required all clergy to subscribe to the Prayer Book and the Thirty-nine Articles; some 80 or more were deprived of their livings, with the king's backing.[13][5]

1611–1612

Campaign against Conrad Vorstius

Called for it

Against: Conrad Vorstius, German theologian appointed at Leiden, and his books

James condemned Vorstius as a heretic, had his books burned at St Paul's, Oxford, and Cambridge, and pressed the States of Holland, through his ambassador Ralph Winwood, to remove him; James wrote that he deserved to be burned.[10][9]

Outcome: Vorstius was kept from teaching and later, after the Synod of Dort, banished from the Dutch Republic.

1617–1625

Enforcement of the Five Articles of Perth

Ordered or signed it

Against: Scottish ministers and laypeople who refused kneeling at communion and other articles

Ministers who refused to conform were summoned before the Court of High Commission, and some were deprived, confined, or banished (David Calderwood was banished in 1617 for protesting the king's church policy); Archbishop John Spottiswoode enforced the articles.[11][12]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

As a child king James was a prize in the struggles between noble factions: he was held captive for ten months by the Ruthven raiders (1582–1583), was the target of raids by his cousin Francis Stewart, Earl of Bothwell, whom the North Berwick witches were said to have served, and of the disputed Gowrie conspiracy (1600). In England he was the intended victim of the Bye and Main plots (1603) and the Gunpowder Plot (1605). Presbyterian ministers denounced him from the pulpit, which he resented, but this fell short of persecution.

Excommunications

James was never excommunicated. Pope Paul V condemned the Oath of Allegiance in briefs of 1606 and 1607 and forbade Catholics to take it, which was a ruling on the oath, not a sentence against the king. Earlier popes had hoped he would convert, and his Catholic queen, Anne of Denmark, privately practised Catholicism. In Scotland, the Kirk excommunicated some of his Catholic nobles and his opponent Bothwell, but never the king.

August 1582 – June 1583

Ruthven Raid: held captive by Protestant lords

To break the influence of his favourite Esmé Stewart, Duke of Lennox, whom they suspected of Catholic sympathies, a group of Protestant nobles seized the sixteen-year-old king at Ruthven Castle and held him for about ten months. The General Assembly of the Kirk approved the raid.[8][12]

5 August 1600

Gowrie conspiracy

At Gowrie House in Perth, John Ruthven, 3rd Earl of Gowrie, and his brother Alexander Ruthven were killed by the king's attendants after, by James's account, Alexander tried to seize or kill him. Several ministers doubted the king's version, and some historians have suspected that the plot was the king's own; what happened remains disputed.[8][9]

July 1603

Bye and Main plots

The Bye Plot, led by the Catholic priests William Watson and William Clark with others, planned to seize the king to force toleration for Catholics; the related Main Plot, attributed to Lord Cobham and implicating Sir Walter Raleigh, allegedly aimed to put Arbella Stuart on the throne with Spanish help.[9][14]

5 November 1605

Gunpowder Plot

A group of English Catholics led by Robert Catesby placed gunpowder under the House of Lords to kill the king, his heirs, and Parliament at the State Opening. The plot was revealed by an anonymous letter to Lord Monteagle, and Guy Fawkes was arrested guarding the powder.[14][9]
