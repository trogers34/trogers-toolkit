Third Council of Constantinople

680–681 · Medieval

Also known as Sixth Ecumenical Council

The council that condemned monothelitism, the teaching that Christ had only one will, and anathematized past patriarchs and Pope Honorius I.

Began
7 November 680[1][5]
Ended
16 September 681[1][5]
Place
Constantinople (the domed hall, or Trullo, of the imperial palace)

Background

  • In the 630s Patriarch Sergius of Constantinople and Emperor Heraclius promoted the formula of one 'energy' and then one will in Christ to reconcile the opponents of Chalcedon. Heraclius's Ekthesis (638) imposed it, and Pope Honorius I had written approvingly of 'one will'.[3][2]
  • Constans II's Typos (648) forbade all discussion of the issue. Pope Martin I and Maximus the Confessor condemned monothelitism at the Lateran Synod of 649, and both were then arrested and punished by the emperor.[2][3]
  • Constantine IV, wanting peace with Rome after the Arab conquests had taken the provinces monothelitism was meant to win over, asked Pope Agatho for delegates and summoned a council.[5][4]

What happened

  • Constantine IV presided over many of the 18 sessions in person; attendance grew from fewer than 50 to about 150 bishops.[1][5]
  • The council accepted Pope Agatho's letter, which taught two wills in Christ, as it had accepted Leo's Tome at Chalcedon.[1][4]
  • Macarius of Antioch defended one will, refused to give way, and was deposed during the council.[1][5]
  • It defined that Christ has two natural wills and two natural operations, divine and human, the human will following and subject to the divine.[1]

Key participants

  • Constantine IV Roman emperor; summoned and presided
  • Agatho Bishop of Rome; his letter set out the teaching adopted
  • George I of Constantinople Patriarch; accepted two wills
  • Macarius of Antioch Patriarch; defended monothelitism and was deposed

Outcomes and significance

  • Pope Leo II confirmed the council in 682, including the condemnation of Honorius, whom he described as having allowed the faith to be stained by not suppressing heresy.[1][5]
  • The condemnation of a pope became a central argument in the debate over papal infallibility at the First Vatican Council (1870).[5]
  • Monothelitism was briefly revived by Emperor Philippikos (711–713) and lasted longer among the Maronites of Lebanon, who later entered communion with Rome.[5][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The council itself deposed Macarius and sent him to Rome for confinement. The harshest persecution in the controversy came before it, under the monothelite emperor Constans II.

681

Deposition and confinement of Macarius of Antioch

After his deposition, the emperor sent Macarius and his followers to Rome, where Pope Leo II placed them in monasteries.[5][4]

653–655

Arrest and exile of Pope Martin I (before the council)

On Constans II's orders the exarch Theodore Calliopas arrested Martin in Rome in June 653. He was taken to Constantinople, tried for treason, publicly humiliated, and exiled to Cherson in Crimea.[2][3]

653–662

Trials and mutilation of Maximus the Confessor (before the council)

Maximus was arrested with Martin, tried and exiled, and tried again in 662 by a synod in Constantinople. His tongue and right hand were cut off so that he could no longer speak or write against monothelitism, and he was exiled to Lazica in the Caucasus.[2][3]
