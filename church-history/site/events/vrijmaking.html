The Vrijmaking (Liberation of 1944)

1942–1944 · Modern

The split in the Gereformeerde Kerken in Nederland during the German occupation, when the synod deposed the theologian Klaas Schilder over binding doctrinal statements on baptism and covenant, and his supporters formed the 'Liberated' churches.

Began
1942[1][2]
Ended
11 August 1944[1][2]
Place
The Netherlands (under German occupation)

Background

  • The Gereformeerde Kerken were divided over Abraham Kuyper's teaching of 'presumptive regeneration', that baptized children of believers may be presumed to be born again; Klaas Schilder, professor at Kampen, rejected binding it on the churches.[1][2]
  • Schilder had been arrested by the Germans in 1940 for articles against Nazism and was in hiding during much of the dispute.[1]

What happened

  • A long-running synod (1939–46) adopted doctrinal declarations in 1942 and required office-bearers to submit to them.[1][2]
  • In March 1944 the synod suspended and then deposed Schilder; in August his supporters signed an 'Act of Liberation or Return', freeing themselves from the synod's decisions.[1][2]
  • About a tenth of the membership left to form the Gereformeerde Kerken (vrijgemaakt).[2]

Key participants

  • Klaas Schilder Kampen theologian deposed by the synod; leader of the Liberated churches
  • Synod of the Gereformeerde Kerken (1939–1946) Imposed the doctrinal declarations and deposed Schilder

Outcomes and significance

  • The synod withdrew the binding force of its declarations in 1946, but the split was not healed. Emigrants formed the Canadian and American Reformed Churches (from 1950).[2]
  • The Liberated churches themselves later split; most merged with the Netherlands Reformed Churches (NGK) in 2023.[2]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The split itself involved no state persecution, but it took place under the Nazi occupation, which had imprisoned Schilder.

August–December 1940

Imprisonment of Klaas Schilder by the Germans

The German occupation authorities arrested Schilder for anti-Nazi articles in his journal De Reformatie and held him for four months; afterwards he lived largely in hiding.[1]
