Second Vatican Council

1962–1965 · Modern

Also known as Vatican II; twenty-first ecumenical council (Catholic count)

The council called by John XXIII that defined no new dogma but reshaped Catholic worship, teaching on the church, and relations with other Christians, Jews, and other religions.

Began
11 October 1962[1][2]
Ended
8 December 1965[1][2]
Place
St Peter's Basilica, Vatican

Background

  • John XXIII announced the council on 25 January 1959, speaking of the church's need for aggiornamento (bringing up to date).[2][3]

What happened

  • About 2,400 bishops attended its four sessions, along with Protestant and Orthodox observers.[2][3]
  • Sacrosanctum Concilium (1963) allowed the Mass and sacraments in local languages and called for full and active participation of the laity.[1][2]
  • Lumen Gentium (1964) taught that the church of Christ 'subsists in' the Catholic Church, stressed the collegiality of bishops, and described Mary within its teaching on the church.[1]
  • Nostra Aetate (1965) stated that the death of Christ cannot be blamed on all Jews then living or on Jews today, deplored antisemitism, and spoke with respect of Muslims, Hindus, and Buddhists; Dignitatis Humanae (1965) affirmed religious liberty as a civil right.[1][2]
  • The council defined no new dogmas and pronounced no anathemas.[2][3]

Key participants

  • John XXIII Pope who called the council; died in June 1963
  • Paul VI Pope who continued and closed it
  • Marcel Lefebvre Archbishop who led the conservative minority and later rejected parts of the council
  • Josyf Slipyj Ukrainian Greek Catholic archbishop freed from Soviet prison camps in 1963 and brought to the council
  • John Henry Newman Often called the council's 'absent father' for the influence of his ideas on development of doctrine and the laity

Outcomes and significance

  • Catholic worship changed dramatically with the new order of Mass (1969–70) in local languages.[2][3]
  • Catholic ecumenical dialogues with Protestant and Orthodox churches followed, including the lifting of the 1054 excommunications and later agreements such as the 1999 Joint Declaration on justification.[2][3]
  • How to interpret the council remains disputed: as a break with the past, or as reform in continuity, as Benedict XVI argued in 2005.[2]

The council itself condemned no one. The main formal break connected with it came later.

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Several bishops from communist countries could not attend or came only after release from prison.

1945–1963

Imprisonment of Josyf Slipyj

Archbishop Josyf Slipyj of Lviv, head of the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church, was arrested by the Soviet authorities in 1945 and spent 18 years in prisons and labor camps after his church was forcibly merged into the Russian Orthodox Church. John XXIII's diplomacy with Nikita Khrushchev won his release in February 1963.[2][3]
