Melkite Schism of 1724

1724–1729 · Modern

Also known as Split of the Patriarchate of Antioch

The division of the Orthodox Patriarchate of Antioch into the Greek Orthodox Church and the Melkite Greek Catholic Church, after rival patriarchs were chosen in 1724.

Began
September 1724[1][3]
Ended
1729 (Rome recognizes Cyril VI)[1][3]
Place
Damascus and Aleppo, Ottoman Syria

Background

  • Catholic missionaries, especially Jesuits and Capuchins, had been active in Aleppo and Damascus since the 17th century, and a pro-Catholic party had grown among the Arabic-speaking Orthodox of the patriarchate.[1][2]

What happened

  • When Patriarch Athanasios III Dabbas died in 1724, the clergy and people of Damascus elected Cyril VI Tanas, who favored union with Rome.[1][3]
  • The Patriarch of Constantinople and his synod instead appointed Sylvester, a Greek monk from Cyprus, as patriarch of Antioch, and obtained Ottoman recognition for him.[1][2]
  • Cyril was excommunicated by Constantinople and fled to the Shuwayr monastery in Lebanon; Pope Benedict XIII recognized him in 1729.[1][3]

Key participants

  • Cyril VI Tanas First patriarch of the Melkite Greek Catholic Church
  • Sylvester of Antioch Greek Orthodox patriarch appointed from Constantinople
  • Jeremias III Patriarch of Constantinople who backed Sylvester
  • Benedict XIII Pope who recognized Cyril

Outcomes and significance

  • Two churches claim the patriarchate of Antioch to this day: the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Antioch and the Melkite Greek Catholic Patriarchate.[3][1]
  • The Ottoman government recognized the Melkite Catholics as a separate community (millet) only in 1848.[2][3]
  • The Greek-dominated hierarchy of the Orthodox patriarchate was replaced by Arab hierarchs in 1899.[1]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

With Ottoman backing, the Orthodox patriarch's party pressed hard against the Catholic Melkites for over a century.

1724–1830s

Pressure on the Catholic Melkites

Patriarch Sylvester used Ottoman decrees to have Catholic clergy arrested, churches taken, and Catholics forced to accept Orthodox clergy, especially in Aleppo; Catholic Melkites could not legally have their own churches in many cities until the 19th century.[1][2]
