Heinrich Bullinger

1504–1575 · Reformation

Also known as Henry Bullinger

Swiss Reformer, Zwingli's successor as chief minister (Antistes) of the Zürich church

Leader of the Zürich church for over forty years after Zwingli's death, author of the Second Helvetic Confession and the Decades, and one of the most widely read and best-connected Reformed theologians of the sixteenth century.

What they did

  • Led the Zürich church as Antistes from 1531 until his death, steadying it after the defeat at Kappel and negotiating with the city council a settlement that kept the clergy's freedom to preach while limiting their role in politics.[4][3]
  • Wrote the Second Helvetic Confession, first drafted as a personal statement of faith in 1561 and published in 1566; it became one of the most widely accepted Reformed confessions, adopted in Switzerland, Hungary, Poland, and elsewhere.[1][3]
  • Agreed with Calvin the Consensus Tigurinus (1549), a common statement on the sacraments that drew the Zürich and Genevan churches together and marked the Reformed tradition off from the Lutheran.[4][3]
  • Published the Decades (1549–51), fifty sermons on Christian doctrine; in English translation they were widely used in the Elizabethan church, and Archbishop John Whitgift ordered less-educated clergy to read them (1586).[2][3]
  • Kept up a correspondence of about 12,000 surviving letters with reformers, rulers, and churches across Europe, and advised the churches of England, Hungary, and Poland.[3][6]
  • Gave refuge and support to Protestant exiles, including English Protestants fleeing Mary I and the Protestants expelled from Locarno in 1555.[4][3]
  • Developed an early theology of the covenant in On the One and Eternal Testament or Covenant of God (1534), influential in later Reformed covenant theology.[3][6]
  • Wrote against the Anabaptists, including a history tracing them to the radicals of the 1520s (1560), and supported the Zürich council's use of fines, imprisonment, and banishment against them, defending the magistrate's right to punish obstinate heretics.[4][3]
  • Endorsed the condemnation of Michael Servetus, advising Geneva in 1553 that he should be punished and approving his execution afterwards.[4][5]
  • Wrote a history of the Reformation in the Swiss Confederation, an important if partisan source for Zwingli's career and death.[3][4]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Bullinger supported the punishment of religious dissenters by the civil authorities: he advised that Michael Servetus be punished and approved his burning, defended Zürich's fines, imprisonment, and banishment of Anabaptists, and backed the banishment of Bernardino Ochino. Zürich's executions of Anabaptists by drowning (1527–1532) mostly came before he succeeded Zwingli in December 1531, and he held no judicial office himself.

1553

Advice for the punishment of Michael Servetus

Called for it

Against: Michael Servetus, anti-Trinitarian physician tried in Geneva

When Geneva consulted the Swiss Protestant churches, the Zürich ministers led by Bullinger replied that Servetus should be punished so that his heresy could not spread, and Bullinger privately expected and approved a death sentence. Servetus was burned on 27 October 1553; the Geneva council passed the sentence and John Calvin led the prosecution.[5][4]

1531–1575

Measures against Anabaptists in Zürich

Approved or defended it

Against: Anabaptists in Zürich and its territory

Bullinger supported the Zürich council's continuing mandates against Anabaptists, which punished them with fines, imprisonment, and banishment, and in his writings, including a history of the movement (1560), defended the magistrate's right to punish obstinate heretics and the earlier executions as punishment for sedition.[4][3][2]

1563

Banishment of Bernardino Ochino

Took part

Against: Bernardino Ochino, Italian refugee preacher to Zürich's Italian congregation

After Ochino published his Thirty Dialogues, which were read as excusing polygamy and questioning the Trinity, the Zürich ministers led by Bullinger reported against the book, and the council expelled him and his children in winter 1563. Ochino, then in his mid-seventies, died in Moravia the next year.[4][3]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Bullinger was never arrested, tried, or imprisoned. He and his father were driven from Bremgarten in 1531 when the Catholic cantons restored the Mass there after their victory at Kappel. As a leading Protestant his works were later placed on the Catholic Index. He was himself on the side of the authorities in the coercion of Anabaptists and in the case of Servetus, described among his accomplishments above.

Excommunications

No papal excommunication of Bullinger by name is recorded. As a Protestant minister he was outside the Catholic Church, and all his works were forbidden to Catholics.

November 1531

Flight from Bremgarten

After the Catholic cantons defeated Zürich at Kappel (11 October 1531), Bremgarten, in the jointly ruled Freie Ämter, was forced back to Catholicism. Bullinger, his father, and the Reformed party fled to Zürich.[4][3]
