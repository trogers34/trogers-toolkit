Anglican Churches: Beginnings and Splits

1534–2023 · Reformation

How the Church of England broke from Rome under Henry VIII, took shape under Edward VI and Elizabeth I, spread as a worldwide communion, and divided over Puritanism, Methodism, churchmanship, and sexuality.

Began
November 1534[4][5]
Ended
February 2023[5]
Place
England, then worldwide

Background

  • Henry VIII broke with Rome after Pope Clement VII refused to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon; the break was political at first, with Catholic doctrine largely kept until Henry's death.[1][5]

What happened

  • Under Edward VI, Archbishop Thomas Cranmer introduced Protestant worship in English through the Book of Common Prayer; Mary I restored Catholicism (1553–58); Elizabeth I settled a Protestant church with bishops and traditional forms.[1][4]
  • Puritans who wanted further reform were pushed out after the Restoration (1662) and formed the Nonconformist churches; Methodists separated after Wesley's death.[3][4]
  • The church spread with the British Empire and missions, forming the Anglican Communion of independent provinces linked to the Archbishop of Canterbury.[4][5]
  • From the 1970s disputes over women's ordination and sexuality led to breakaway churches and a split between conservative provinces, mostly in the Global South, and Western provinces.[4][5]

Key participants

  • Henry VIII King who broke with Rome (1534) but kept Catholic doctrine
  • Edward VI King under whom the church became Protestant (1547–53)
  • Mary I Queen who restored Catholicism and union with Rome (1553–58)
  • Elizabeth I Queen who restored Protestantism in the 1559 settlement
  • Thomas Cranmer Archbishop who wrote the Book of Common Prayer
  • Thomas More Executed for refusing to accept the royal supremacy
  • Richard Hooker Theologian of the Elizabethan church
  • John Wesley Anglican priest whose movement became Methodism
  • John Henry Newman Leader of the Oxford Movement
  • C. S. Lewis Anglican layman and writer

Outcomes and significance

  • The Anglican Communion has tens of millions of members, the largest share in Africa, but is divided over authority and sexuality.[4][5]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The English Reformation brought executions on all sides as the official religion changed with each monarch.

1535–1540s

Executions under Henry VIII

Those who refused the oath acknowledging the royal supremacy were executed, including Bishop John Fisher, Thomas More, and Carthusian monks of London (1535); Henry also burned Protestants who denied Catholic doctrines.[1][5]

1555–1558

Burnings under Mary I

About 280 Protestants, including Archbishop Cranmer and Bishops Latimer and Ridley, were burned for heresy.[2][5]

1570s–1603

Executions of Catholics under Elizabeth I

After the papal excommunication of Elizabeth, Catholic priests were treated as traitors; about 180–200 Catholics, most of them priests such as Edmund Campion (1581), were executed.[2][5]
