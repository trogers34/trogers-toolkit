Charles Haddon Spurgeon

1834–1892 · Modern

Also known as C. H. Spurgeon; "the Prince of Preachers"

Baptist preacher, pastor of the Metropolitan Tabernacle, London

English Baptist preacher who drew vast crowds in Victorian London, published a sermon every week for nearly forty years, and founded a college, an orphanage, and other ministries, while defending Calvinist evangelical doctrine to the end of his life.

What they did

  • Preached to crowds of thousands from the age of 19, first at New Park Street Chapel and the Surrey Gardens Music Hall, then for three decades at the Metropolitan Tabernacle, one of the largest churches of its day.[1][7]
  • Published a sermon every week from 1855 until his death and beyond. The New Park Street and Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit series fill 63 volumes, sold widely, and were translated into many languages.[2][4]
  • Founded the Pastors' College (1856, now Spurgeon's College) to train ministers, many of them without formal education.[1][6]
  • Founded the Stockwell Orphanage (1867, with a girls' wing from 1879), along with almshouses and a society of colporteurs who sold Christian literature door to door.[1][4]
  • Wrote The Treasury of David, a seven-volume commentary on the Psalms, Lectures to My Students, the devotional Morning and Evening, and the popular John Ploughman's Talk.[4][6]
  • Edited the monthly magazine The Sword and the Trowel from 1865.[3][6]
  • Spoke and wrote strongly against slavery, which cost him readers and sales in the American South.[4][6]
  • In the Down-Grade Controversy (1887), warned that Baptist and other Nonconformist ministers were abandoning core evangelical doctrines, and withdrew from the Baptist Union when it would not adopt a firm doctrinal statement.[3][5]
  • Suffered for decades from gout, kidney disease, and periods of deep depression, which he spoke about openly in his preaching.[4][6]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Spurgeon supporting or carrying out persecution of others. A Baptist and Nonconformist, he argued for religious liberty and against the established Church of England's privileges; his conflicts, such as the Down-Grade Controversy (1887–88), were doctrinal disputes conducted in print, not persecution.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Spurgeon was never arrested or imprisoned. Early in his ministry he was mocked in the press and in cartoons as a vulgar showman. The incidents below are the more serious ones: the deadly false alarm at the Surrey Gardens Music Hall, the burning of his sermons in the American South over his opposition to slavery, and the Baptist Union's formal censure during the Down-Grade Controversy.

Excommunications

Spurgeon was never excommunicated. The Baptist Union's Council passed a formal vote of censure against him in January 1888, after he had already resigned from the Union, but that was a rebuke, not an expulsion. He remained pastor of the Metropolitan Tabernacle until his death.

19 October 1856

False "Fire!" alarm at the Surrey Gardens Music Hall

During his first service at the Surrey Gardens Music Hall, before a crowd of about 10,000 or more, people in the audience shouted "Fire!" and that the galleries were collapsing. Neither was true, but the panic caused a crush in which seven people died and many were injured. Spurgeon and his supporters believed the alarm was raised deliberately to disrupt the service. Parts of the press blamed Spurgeon himself.[1][4]

c. 1860

Sermons burned in the American South over his opposition to slavery

After Spurgeon's denunciations of slavery became known in the United States, his sermons and books were publicly burned in parts of the slaveholding South, and booksellers there stopped selling them. He also received threats.[4][6]

October 1887 – April 1888

Censured by the Baptist Union in the Down-Grade Controversy

Spurgeon accused some Baptist ministers of abandoning core doctrines and resigned from the Baptist Union in October 1887. In January 1888 the Union's Council formally censured him for making these charges without naming the ministers concerned. The Union then adopted a doctrinal statement that Spurgeon considered too vague.[5][3][4]
