Prussian Union and the Old Lutherans

1817–1845 · Modern

King Frederick William III's union of the Lutheran and Reformed churches of Prussia, and his government's persecution of the 'Old Lutherans' who refused it, many of whom emigrated to America and Australia.

Began
27 September 1817[1][2]
Ended
23 July 1845[1][2]
Place
Kingdom of Prussia

Background

  • The Hohenzollern kings were Reformed while most of their subjects were Lutheran; Frederick William III wanted one Protestant church and personally wrote much of its new liturgy.[1][2]

What happened

  • The union began as voluntary in 1817, but the king's common liturgy (1822) was pressed on congregations, and from 1830 refusing it became a punishable offense.[1]
  • Led by Johann Gottfried Scheibel of Breslau, 'Old Lutherans' who insisted on the Lutheran confessions and liturgy refused; their pastors were suspended, fined, and jailed, and services were held in secret.[1][2]
  • Thousands emigrated: Pastor August Kavel led groups to South Australia from 1838, and Pastor J. A. A. Grabau led others to Buffalo, New York, in 1839.[2][3]

Key participants

  • Frederick William III King of Prussia who imposed the union
  • Frederick William IV King who ended the persecution (1840–45)
  • Johann Gottfried Scheibel Breslau theologian who led the resistance
  • J. A. A. Grabau Pastor imprisoned for resistance; founded the Buffalo Synod
  • August Kavel Pastor who led emigrants to South Australia

Outcomes and significance

  • The emigrants helped found confessional Lutheran churches in America and Australia, and the controversy strengthened confessional Lutheranism, including the Missouri Synod.[3][2]
  • The Evangelical Church of the Prussian Union lasted, in changing forms, until the 2000s; the Old Lutherans in Germany are part of today's Independent Evangelical-Lutheran Church (SELK).[2][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

1830–1840

Suppression of the Old Lutherans

Pastors who refused the union were removed, fined, and imprisoned; Grabau was jailed in 1837–38. In December 1834 soldiers forced open the church at Hönigern in Silesia and installed a union pastor; worshippers who resisted were beaten and soldiers quartered on them. Lay people who held secret services were fined and had property seized.[1][2]
