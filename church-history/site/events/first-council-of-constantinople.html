First Council of Constantinople

381 · Early Church

Also known as Second Ecumenical Council

The council called by Emperor Theodosius I that ended the Arian controversy in the eastern empire, affirmed the full divinity of the Holy Spirit, and is traditionally credited with the expanded Nicene Creed.

Began
May 381[1][3]
Ended
9 July 381[1][3]
Place
Constantinople

Background

  • After Nicaea (325), emperors such as Constantius II and Valens supported anti-Nicene bishops, and Constantinople had been held by non-Nicene bishops for about 40 years.[3][4]
  • On 28 February 380 Theodosius I issued the edict Cunctos populos, ordering all subjects to hold the faith of the bishops of Rome and Alexandria and calling others 'demented and insane' heretics. In November 380 he expelled the non-Nicene bishop Demophilus from Constantinople's churches.[2][3]
  • A group called the Pneumatomachians ('fighters against the Spirit', also called Macedonians) accepted the Son's divinity but denied that the Holy Spirit was fully God.[3][4]

What happened

  • About 150 bishops attended, all from the eastern empire; the bishop of Rome, Damasus I, was neither present nor represented. A further 36 Pneumatomachian bishops came but left rather than accept the Nicene faith.[1][3]
  • Meletius of Antioch presided until he died during the council. Gregory of Nazianzus, confirmed as bishop of Constantinople, then presided, but resigned amid disputes over the succession at Antioch and objections to his transfer from another see.[5][3]
  • Nectarius, an unbaptized senator, was chosen as bishop of Constantinople in Gregory's place.[5][7]
  • Canon 1 reaffirmed the faith of Nicaea and anathematized the Eunomians, Arians, Semi-Arians or Pneumatomachians, Sabellians, Marcellians, Photinians, and Apollinarians.[1]
  • Canon 3 gave the bishop of Constantinople precedence of honor after the bishop of Rome, 'because it is New Rome'.[1]

Key participants

  • Theodosius I Roman emperor (East); summoned the council
  • Meletius of Antioch First president; died during the council
  • Gregory of Nazianzus Bishop of Constantinople and president after Meletius; resigned
  • Gregory of Nyssa Cappadocian bishop and theologian; took part
  • Nectarius Elected bishop of Constantinople

Outcomes and significance

  • The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed, used today in most churches, is traditionally attributed to this council; it expands the article on the Holy Spirit, 'the Lord and giver of life, who proceeds from the Father'. Its text is first recorded in the acts of Chalcedon (451), and scholars debate how it was issued in 381.[1][6][4]
  • Together with Theodosius's laws, the council ended official support for Arianism in the eastern empire; Arian Christianity continued for centuries among the Goths and other Germanic peoples.[3][7]
  • Rome did not accept canon 3, and the council was recognized as ecumenical in the West only after Chalcedon (451).[1][7]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The council itself condemned teachings, not named people. The penalties came from Theodosius I's laws, which took churches from non-Nicene Christians and banned their meetings.

380–381

Imperial laws against non-Nicene Christians

Theodosius's edict of 380 threatened heretics with divine and imperial punishment. In January 381 he ordered all churches handed to Nicene bishops and banned heretical meetings inside towns; on 30 July 381, after the council, he ordered churches given only to bishops in communion with named orthodox bishops.[2][3]
