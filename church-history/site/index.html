Events

  • First Council of Nicaea325 · Council · Early Church

    The first council of bishops from across the Roman Empire, called by Emperor Constantine, which condemned Arius and issued the original Nicene Creed.

  • First Council of Constantinople381 · Council · Early Church

    The council called by Emperor Theodosius I that ended the Arian controversy in the eastern empire, affirmed the full divinity of the Holy Spirit, and is traditionally credited with the expanded Nicene Creed.

  • Council of Ephesus431 · Council · Early Church

    The council at which Cyril of Alexandria had Nestorius of Constantinople deposed and Mary affirmed as Theotokos ('God-bearer'), held amid rival assemblies, imperial arrests, and bribery of the court.

  • Marian Dogmas of the Catholic Church431–1950 · Document · Medieval

    The four teachings about Mary that the Catholic Church has defined as dogma, binding on all Catholics: that she is the Mother of God, that she remained a virgin, that she was conceived without original sin, and that she was taken body and soul into heaven.

  • Council of Chalcedon451 · Council · Early Church

    The council that defined Christ as one person in two natures, divine and human, using Pope Leo I's Tome, and that split the eastern churches.

  • Second Council of Constantinople553 · Council · Early Church

    The council called by Emperor Justinian I to condemn the 'Three Chapters', held over the refusal of Pope Vigilius, who was in the city under imperial pressure.

  • Third Council of Constantinople680–681 · Council · Medieval

    The council that condemned monothelitism, the teaching that Christ had only one will, and anathematized past patriarchs and Pope Honorius I.

  • Second Council of Nicaea787 · Council · Medieval

    The council called by Empress Irene that ended the first period of Byzantine iconoclasm and approved the veneration of icons.

  • Great Schism of 10541054 · Schism · Medieval

    The mutual excommunications of 1054 between papal legates and the patriarch of Constantinople, traditionally dated as the split between the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches.

  • The Crusades1095–1291 · War · Medieval

    A series of wars called by popes, which promised spiritual rewards to those who fought: above all the campaigns to capture and hold Jerusalem and the Holy Land, from Urban II's call in 1095 to the fall of Acre in 1291.

  • Fourth Lateran Council1215 · Council · Medieval

    Pope Innocent III's great reforming council, which first officially used the word 'transubstantiation', required yearly confession, organized the suppression of heresy, and imposed distinctive dress on Jews and Muslims.

  • Arsenite Schismc. 1262–1310 · Schism · Medieval

    A split in the Byzantine church after Patriarch Arsenios excommunicated Emperor Michael VIII for blinding the boy emperor John IV, and was deposed for it; his followers refused communion with the official patriarchs for over 40 years.

  • Second Council of Lyon1274 · Council · Medieval

    Pope Gregory X's council that defined the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father and the Son and proclaimed a short-lived reunion with the Byzantine church.

  • Unam Sanctam1302–1303 · Document · Medieval

    Pope Boniface VIII's bull, issued in his struggle with King Philip IV of France, declaring that it is necessary for salvation for every human being to be subject to the Roman pontiff.

  • Council of Vienne1311–1312 · Council · Medieval

    Pope Clement V's council, held under pressure from Philip IV of France, which suppressed the Knights Templar and defined that the rational soul is the form of the human body.

  • Benedictus Deus1331–1336 · Document · Medieval

    Pope Benedict XII's definition that the souls of the saved see God face to face immediately after death (or after purgatory), settling a controversy started by his predecessor John XXII's own sermons.

  • Council of Constance1414–1418 · Council · Medieval

    The council that ended the Western Schism of three rival popes, condemned John Wycliffe, and burned Jan Hus and Jerome of Prague.

  • Council of Florence1438–c. 1445 · Council · Medieval

    The council at which the Byzantine emperor and Greek bishops agreed to reunion with Rome on the filioque, purgatory, and papal primacy, a union their church soon rejected; it also issued decrees on the sacraments and on salvation outside the church.

  • Fifth Lateran Council1512–1517 · Council · Reformation

    The last council before the Reformation, called by Julius II against a rival council at Pisa, which defined the individual immortality of the soul and issued reform decrees that were largely not carried out.

  • Ninety-five Theses (the Wittenberg door)1517 · Document · Reformation

    Martin Luther's 95 Latin propositions against the sale of indulgences, sent to the archbishop of Mainz on 31 October 1517 and, by a later account, nailed to the door of Wittenberg's Castle Church. The date is traditionally taken as the start of the Protestant Reformation.

  • Diet of Worms1521 · Imperial assembly · Reformation

    The imperial assembly at which Martin Luther refused before Emperor Charles V to recant his writings and was then declared an outlaw.

  • Council of Trent1545–1563 · Council · Reformation

    The Catholic Church's council in response to the Protestant Reformation, which defined Catholic teaching against Protestant doctrines and launched wide-ranging reforms.

  • St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre1572 · Massacre · Reformation

    The killing of Huguenot (French Protestant) leaders in Paris on royal orders, which turned into mass killings of Protestants by Catholic crowds in Paris and other French cities.

  • Union of Brest1595–1596 · Schism · Reformation

    The union of most Orthodox bishops of the Kyivan Metropolitanate in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth with Rome, keeping their Byzantine rite, which split the Ruthenian church and created what is now the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church.

  • Old Believers Schism1652–1667 · Schism · Modern

    The split in the Russian Orthodox Church over Patriarch Nikon's reforms of worship, which the church and state enforced with exile, torture, and burning, and which led thousands of Old Believers to burn themselves to death.

  • Melkite Schism of 17241724–1729 · Schism · Modern

    The division of the Orthodox Patriarchate of Antioch into the Greek Orthodox Church and the Melkite Greek Catholic Church, after rival patriarchs were chosen in 1724.

  • First Vatican Council1869–1870 · Council · Modern

    Pope Pius IX's council, which defined papal infallibility and the pope's universal jurisdiction, and was cut short when Italian troops took Rome.

  • Bulgarian Schism1872–1945 · Schism · Modern

    The break between the Patriarchate of Constantinople and the Bulgarian Exarchate, which Constantinople declared schismatic in 1872 for organizing the church by nationality ('ethnophyletism').

  • Old Calendarist Schism1924–1935 · Schism · Modern

    The split that followed the adoption of the Revised Julian calendar by the Church of Greece and other Orthodox churches in 1924, when many faithful refused to change and formed separate Old Calendarist churches.

  • Russian Church Abroad Split1927–2007 · Schism · Modern

    The 80-year separation between the Russian Orthodox Church in the Soviet Union and the émigré Russian Church Abroad, which broke with Moscow over its loyalty to the Soviet state and reunited in 2007.

  • Second Vatican Council1962–1965 · Council · Modern

    The council called by John XXIII that defined no new dogma but reshaped Catholic worship, teaching on the church, and relations with other Christians, Jews, and other religions.

  • Macedonian Orthodox Church Schism1967–2022 · Schism · Modern

    The 55-year break after the Macedonian Orthodox Church declared itself independent of the Serbian Orthodox Church in 1967, ended by its recognition in 2022.

  • Moscow–Constantinople Schism2018–2026 · Schism · Modern

    The break of communion by the Russian Orthodox Church with the Ecumenical Patriarchate in 2018, after Constantinople moved to grant independence to a new Orthodox Church of Ukraine; it remains unresolved.

People

  • Clement of Romec. 35–c. 99 · Early Church

    Leader of the church at Rome at the end of the 1st century and author of 1 Clement, a letter to the church at Corinth that is one of the earliest Christian writings outside the New Testament. He is one of the "Apostolic Fathers," the earliest Christian writers after the New Testament (c. 90–150).

  • Ignatius of Antiochc. 35–c. 110 · Early Church

    Bishop of Antioch who, while being taken under guard to Rome for execution, wrote seven letters that are the earliest evidence for the church being led by a single bishop with presbyters and deacons, and the first known use of the phrase "the catholic church." He is one of the "Apostolic Fathers," the earliest Christian writers after the New Testament (c. 90–150).

  • Papias of Hierapolisc. 60–c. 130 · Early Church

    Early 2nd-century bishop of Hierapolis who gathered oral traditions from people who had known Jesus' followers. His lost work survives only in quotations, including the earliest statements about how the Gospels of Mark and Matthew were written. He is one of the "Apostolic Fathers," the earliest Christian writers after the New Testament (c. 90–150).

  • Polycarp of Smyrnac. 69–c. 155 · Early Church

    Bishop of Smyrna, said by Irenaeus to have been taught by the apostle John, whose execution in old age is recorded in the Martyrdom of Polycarp, the earliest surviving account of a Christian martyrdom after the New Testament. He is one of the "Apostolic Fathers," the earliest Christian writers after the New Testament (c. 90–150).

  • Hermasc. 70–c. 150 · Early Church

    Roman Christian, by his own account a former slave, who wrote the Shepherd, a book of visions and moral instruction that many early churches read as Scripture. He is one of the "Apostolic Fathers," the earliest Christian writers after the New Testament (c. 90–150).

  • Pseudo-Barnabasc. 70–c. 140 · Early Church

    The unknown author of the Epistle of Barnabas, a treatise traditionally credited to Paul's companion Barnabas but almost certainly written by someone else between about 96 and 135. The dates shown on the timeline are placeholders for an unknown life. The author is one of the "Apostolic Fathers," the earliest Christian writers after the New Testament (c. 90–150).

  • Quadratus of Athensc. 70–c. 130 · Early Church

    Author of the earliest known Christian apology, a written defense of the faith presented to Emperor Hadrian around 125. Only a single sentence survives. He is counted with both the Apostolic Fathers and the later Apologists.

  • Nicholas of Myrac. 270–c. 343 · Early Church

    Fourth-century bishop of Myra about whom very little is known for certain, but whose legends of secret generosity and miracles made him one of the most popular saints in both East and West, and eventually the model for Santa Claus.

  • Athanasius of Alexandriac. 296–373 · Early Church

    Bishop of Alexandria who spent nearly half a century defending the Nicene teaching that the Son is fully God against Arianism, enduring five exiles under four emperors.

  • Monicac. 331–387 · Early Church

    North African Christian whose years of prayers and tears for her son Augustine, recorded in his Confessions, made her the model of the praying Christian mother.

  • John Chrysostomc. 349–407 · Early Church

    The greatest preacher of the early Greek church, whose sermons earned him the name "golden-mouthed." As Archbishop of Constantinople, he was deposed and exiled after clashing with the imperial court and a rival archbishop, and died on a forced march into exile.

  • Augustine of Hippo354–430 · Early Church

    North African bishop whose writings on grace, sin, the Church, and the two cities shaped Western theology more than any other author after the apostle Paul.

  • Leo the Greatc. 400–461 · Early Church

    Pope whose letter on the two natures of Christ, the Tome, became the basis of the Council of Chalcedon's definition, who strongly asserted the authority of the bishop of Rome, and who met Attila the Hun to turn him away from Italy.

  • Gregory the Greatc. 540–604 · Early Church

    Roman aristocrat turned monk who, as pope, kept Rome fed and defended during plague and Lombard invasions, sent the mission that converted the English, and wrote works on pastoral care and spirituality that shaped the medieval church.

  • Anselm of Canterburyc. 1033–1109 · Medieval

    Monk and archbishop of Canterbury, often called the founder of scholasticism, known for the ontological argument for God's existence and the satisfaction theory of the atonement.

  • Thomas Aquinasc. 1225–1274 · Medieval

    Dominican friar who joined Aristotle's philosophy to Christian theology in a systematic body of work, above all the Summa Theologiae, and became the most influential theologian of the Catholic Church.

  • William of Ockhamc. 1287–1347 · Medieval

    English Franciscan friar and one of the most influential philosophers of the Middle Ages, known for nominalism and the principle of parsimony later called "Ockham's razor." After he accused Pope John XXII of heresy, he lived his last twenty years as an excommunicated exile under the emperor's protection.

  • John Wycliffec. 1330–1384 · Medieval

    Oxford theologian who argued that Scripture is the supreme authority for Christians, attacked the wealth and power of the clergy and papacy, and inspired the first complete English Bible and the Lollard movement.

  • Jan Husc. 1372–1415 · Medieval

    Czech priest and university master who preached church reform in Prague, drawing on Wycliffe's ideas. Despite a promise of safe conduct, he was arrested at the Council of Constance and burned as a heretic, and his death set off the Hussite movement.

  • John Oldcastlec. 1378–1417 · Medieval

    English knight and friend of Henry V who protected Wycliffe's Lollard followers. He was condemned as a heretic, escaped from the Tower of London, led a failed Lollard uprising, and after four years as a fugitive was hanged and burned.

  • Desiderius Erasmusc. 1466–1536 · Reformation

    The most famous scholar of the Renaissance, whose printed Greek New Testament became the basis for the Reformation's Bible translations. He criticized church abuses but stayed Catholic and broke publicly with Luther, so he was attacked from both sides.

  • Thomas More1478–1535 · Reformation

    English lawyer, humanist, and author of Utopia who, as Lord Chancellor, vigorously pursued Protestant "heretics" and their books. He was himself executed by Henry VIII for refusing to accept the king as head of the Church of England, and was canonized by the Catholic Church in 1935.

  • Martin Luther1483–1546 · Reformation

    German friar and professor whose challenge to the sale of indulgences in 1517 sparked the Protestant Reformation and whose German Bible shaped both the church and the German language.

  • Huldrych Zwingli1484–1531 · Reformation

    Leader of the Reformation in Zürich, founder of the Reformed tradition, and opponent of Luther on the Lord's Supper, who was killed in battle against the Catholic Swiss cantons.

  • Thomas Cranmer1489–1556 · Reformation

    Archbishop of Canterbury under Henry VIII and Edward VI who annulled Henry's first marriage, led the English Reformation's changes to worship, and wrote the Book of Common Prayer. Under Mary I he was tried for heresy, recanted, then withdrew his recantations and was burned.

  • Martin Bucer1491–1551 · Reformation

    Reformer of Strasbourg who worked for unity among Protestants and with Catholics, mentored John Calvin, and ended his life in English exile. Under Mary I his body was dug up and burned as a heretic.

  • William Tyndalec. 1494–1536 · Reformation

    English scholar who made the first printed New Testament in English, translated directly from the Greek, and whose wording carried through into the King James Bible; he was executed for heresy in 1536.

  • John Calvin1509–1564 · Reformation

    French theologian whose Institutes of the Christian Religion and work in Geneva shaped the Reformed and Presbyterian churches.

  • John Knoxc. 1514–1572 · Reformation

    Scottish preacher who survived the French galleys and exile to lead the Scottish Reformation, helping bring about the 1560 break with Rome and shaping the Presbyterian Church of Scotland.

  • Martin Chemnitz1522–1586 · Reformation

    Leading Lutheran theologian of the generation after Luther, whose Examination of the Council of Trent was the major Protestant response to Trent, and who led the work on the Formula of Concord that united most German Lutherans.

  • Richard Hookerc. 1554–1600 · Reformation

    Elizabethan priest whose Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity defended the Church of England against its Puritan critics and became the classic statement of Anglican theology.

  • John Wesley1703–1791 · Modern

    Anglican priest whose itinerant preaching and network of societies launched the Methodist movement in Britain and America.

  • Jonathan Edwards1703–1758 · Modern

    New England pastor and theologian whose preaching helped spark the Great Awakening and whose writings made him the most influential theologian of colonial America.

  • Charles Wesley1707–1788 · Modern

    Younger brother of John Wesley and co-founder of Methodism, who wrote thousands of hymns, many still sung across the world's churches.

  • George Whitefield1714–1770 · Modern

    English evangelist whose open-air preaching on both sides of the Atlantic made him the most famous preacher of the 18th century and a central figure in the Great Awakening.

  • John Henry Newman1801–1890 · Modern

    Leader of the Oxford Movement in the Church of England who became a Catholic in 1845, wrote the Apologia Pro Vita Sua and The Idea of a University, was made a cardinal, and was canonized in 2019.

  • Charles Haddon Spurgeon1834–1892 · Modern

    English Baptist preacher who drew vast crowds in Victorian London, published a sermon every week for nearly forty years, and founded a college, an orphanage, and other ministries, while defending Calvinist evangelical doctrine to the end of his life.

  • G. K. Chesterton1874–1936 · Modern

    Prolific English writer whose books Orthodoxy and The Everlasting Man, and his Father Brown detective stories, made him one of the best-known Christian apologists of the 20th century.

  • J. Gresham Machen1881–1937 · Modern

    Princeton New Testament scholar who led the conservative side in the Presbyterian fundamentalist–modernist controversy, was suspended from the ministry by his church, and founded a new seminary and denomination.

  • C. S. Lewis1898–1963 · Modern

    Oxford and Cambridge scholar of medieval and Renaissance literature who returned from atheism to Christianity and became the 20th century's most widely read Christian apologist, through works such as Mere Christianity and The Screwtape Letters, and the author of The Chronicles of Narnia.

  • Billy Graham1918–2018 · Modern

    American evangelist who preached in person to more people than anyone before him through large "crusades" around the world, advised U.S. presidents from Truman to Obama, and became the best-known figure of 20th-century evangelical Protestantism.

  • Chuck Smith1927–2013 · Modern

    California pastor who opened his small church to the hippies of the Jesus Movement in the late 1960s, building Calvary Chapel into a worldwide network of churches known for verse-by-verse Bible teaching and contemporary worship music.

  • R. C. Sproul1939–2017 · Modern

    American Reformed theologian who made classic Calvinist theology accessible to lay audiences through teaching, books, radio, and Ligonier Ministries, and who became a leading defender of biblical inerrancy and justification by faith alone.
