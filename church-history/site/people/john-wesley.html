John Wesley

1703–1791 · Modern

Anglican priest, evangelist, founder of Methodism

Anglican priest whose itinerant preaching and network of societies launched the Methodist movement in Britain and America.

Born
17 June 1703 (Old Style; 28 June New Style) · Epworth, Lincolnshire, England[2][3]
Died

What they did

  • With his brother Charles led a small Oxford group, mocked as the "Holy Club" and "Methodists," devoted to prayer, study, and visiting prisoners.[2][3]
  • Recorded in his Journal his experience at a meeting in Aldersgate Street on 24 May 1738, where he felt assured of salvation by faith in Christ.[1][2]
  • Took up open-air preaching in 1739 at George Whitefield's urging and traveled an estimated 250,000 miles on horseback over five decades.[1][3]
  • Organized converts into societies, classes, and bands, with lay preachers and an annual Conference beginning in 1744.[2][3]
  • Taught Christian perfection: that believers can be made perfect in love in this life.[2]
  • Published sermons, tracts, and a Christian Library for ordinary readers, and with Charles produced hymnbooks for the movement.[2][3]
  • Ordained Thomas Coke as superintendent for American Methodists in 1784, opening the way for the Methodist Episcopal Church.[2][3]
  • Spoke against the slave trade; his Thoughts upon Slavery (1774) and his final known letter (1791, to William Wilberforce) urged its abolition.[2][3]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Wesley opposed the persecution of Methodists and usually urged love toward Catholics (his 1749 Letter to a Roman Catholic), but in 1779–1780 he opposed the Catholic Relief Act and publicly defended the Protestant Association's call to keep the legal restrictions on Catholics. He did not support the Gordon Riots of June 1780, which followed the Association's petition.

1779–1780

Defended keeping the penal laws against Catholics

Called for it

Against: English Roman Catholics

In a letter printed in the Public Advertiser in January 1780, and in Popery Calmly Considered (1779), Wesley argued that Catholics should not be granted toleration because, he claimed, their church taught that faith need not be kept with heretics and that the pope could absolve them from allegiance. He said he wished no Catholic to be persecuted for conscience, only that they not be given the means to do harm. Lord George Gordon's Protestant Association used such arguments in the campaign that ended in the Gordon Riots; Wesley visited Gordon in the Tower in December 1780.[2][4]

Outcome: The Relief Act stood; the riots in London killed several hundred people and destroyed Catholic chapels and homes. Wesley was not involved in the violence.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Wesley was never imprisoned or executed. He faced a lawsuit and a grand jury indictment in colonial Georgia, was shut out of many Church of England pulpits, and was attacked by mobs during the early years of Methodism.

Excommunications

Wesley was never excommunicated. Many clergy closed their pulpits to him, but the Church of England never took formal action against him, and he died a priest of that church. The Methodists separated from the Church of England only after his death.

1737

Indicted in Savannah, Georgia

Wesley refused Holy Communion to Sophia Williamson (née Hopkey), whom he had courted before her marriage. Her husband, William Williamson, sued him for defaming her, and a warrant was issued for Wesley. A grand jury in Savannah, encouraged by her uncle Thomas Causton, the town's chief magistrate, indicted him on ten counts, most about how he ran the church. Wesley argued that a civil court had no authority over church matters.[1][2]

1739

Shut out of church pulpits; told to leave the Diocese of Bristol

As his preaching became controversial, many Church of England clergy refused to let him preach in their churches, which pushed him into preaching in the open air. In August 1739 Joseph Butler, Bishop of Bristol, told Wesley that he had no business preaching in the diocese and advised him to leave.[1][2]

20 October 1743

Seized by a mob at Wednesbury

Methodists around Wednesbury, Staffordshire, had been attacked by mobs for months. On the night of 20 October 1743, a mob seized Wesley and took him before two local justices of the peace, Justice Lane of Bentley and Justice Persehouse of Walsall, both of whom refused to deal with him. A Walsall mob then dragged him through the streets and struck him. Wesley, and later historians, accused some local clergy of encouraging the mobs.[1][2]
