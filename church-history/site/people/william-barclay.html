William Barclay

1907–1978 · Modern

Scottish New Testament scholar, broadcaster, and Church of Scotland minister; author of the Daily Study Bible

A Church of Scotland minister and University of Glasgow professor whose Daily Study Bible commentaries on the New Testament, written for ordinary readers, sold in the millions and are still widely used by preachers. A gifted popularizer and broadcaster, he called himself a 'liberal evangelical'. His doubts about the virgin birth, his natural explanations of some miracles, and his open universalism made him controversial among conservative Christians.

What they did

  • Wrote the Daily Study Bible, a 17-volume commentary on the whole New Testament for lay readers, combining his own translation with background on Greek words and the ancient world; it has sold millions of copies and been widely translated.[1][3]
  • Taught New Testament at the University of Glasgow for nearly three decades, as lecturer and then Professor of Divinity and Biblical Criticism.[3][6]
  • Became one of Britain's best-known religious broadcasters on BBC radio and television, known for plain, warm teaching.[3][4]
  • Wrote popular word studies such as New Testament Words and translated the New Testament himself (1968–1969).[3]
  • Worked despite severe deafness, which he said helped him concentrate, and kept writing into his last years with Parkinson's disease.[4][3]
  • Openly described himself as a universalist, believing all people would finally be saved, and doubted the virgin birth; he explained the feeding of the five thousand as the crowd being moved to share their food. Conservative critics charged him with denying core doctrines.[2][5]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Barclay was never arrested, tried, or disciplined. He received sharp criticism from conservative Christians for his views; in his memoir he describes an anonymous letter claiming that God had drowned his daughter to save her from her father's heresies. That was hostility, not persecution.

Excommunications

Barclay was never excommunicated, tried for heresy, or disciplined by the Church of Scotland, despite criticism of his views.
