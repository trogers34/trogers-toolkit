Fourth Lateran Council

1215 · Medieval

Also known as Lateran IV; twelfth ecumenical council (Catholic count)

Pope Innocent III's great reforming council, which first officially used the word 'transubstantiation', required yearly confession, organized the suppression of heresy, and imposed distinctive dress on Jews and Muslims.

Began
11 November 1215[1][4]
Ended
30 November 1215[1][4]
Place
Lateran Basilica, Rome
Agreed
Dogma: transubstantiation; 'no salvation outside the church'; yearly confession and Easter communion required; measures against heretics; dress rules for Jews and Muslims.

Background

  • Innocent III summoned the council in 1213 to reform the church, fight heresy (especially the Cathars of southern France, already the target of the Albigensian Crusade), and launch a new crusade.[3][4]

What happened

  • About 400 bishops and 800 abbots and priors attended, with envoys of kings and the Eastern Latin patriarchs, making it the largest council of the Middle Ages; it issued 71 constitutions in three sessions.[1][3]
  • Canon 1 set out a profession of faith declaring that there is one universal church 'outside which no one at all is saved', and that in the Eucharist the bread and wine are 'transubstantiated' into Christ's body and blood.[1]
  • Canon 21 (Omnis utriusque sexus) required every Catholic who had reached the age of discretion to confess to their own priest and receive Communion at least once a year, at Easter.[1][4]
  • Canon 3 required secular rulers to expel heretics from their lands on pain of excommunication and loss of their territory, and gave those who fought heretics the same indulgence as crusaders to the Holy Land.[1]
  • Canons 68 and 69 required Jews and Muslims ('Saracens') to wear clothing that distinguished them from Christians, forbade Jews to appear in public during Holy Week, and barred them from public office.[1][2]
  • Canon 18 forbade clergy to bless judicial ordeals, which helped end trial by ordeal in Western Europe.[1][4]
  • The council planned a new crusade (the Fifth Crusade, launched 1217), confirmed Frederick II as emperor, and handed most lands of Count Raymond VI of Toulouse to the crusade leader Simon de Montfort.[3][1]

Key participants

  • Innocent III Pope who called and led the council
  • Raymond VI of Toulouse Count whose lands were given to Simon de Montfort for failing to suppress the Cathars

Outcomes and significance

  • Its canons on confession, preaching, and clerical life shaped Catholic parish practice for centuries, and its profession of faith made transubstantiation the official term for the Eucharist.[3][4]
  • The dress rules led to compulsory badges for Jews in England (1218), France, and elsewhere; historians see the council as a turning point in the legal segregation of Jews in Christian Europe.[2][4]
  • Its framework for suppressing heresy fed into the inquisitions established in the 1230s.[3][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The council's decrees set rules that later bodies enforced. It named no individual for punishment but laid down how heretics and religious minorities were to be treated.

1215 onward

Measures against heretics

Canon 3 ordered that condemned heretics be handed to the secular power for punishment, that suspects who could not prove their innocence be excommunicated, and that rulers who failed to purge heretics lose their lands to Catholics who would.[1][3]

1215 onward

Distinctive dress and restrictions for Jews and Muslims

Canons 68–69 required Jews and Muslims to dress differently from Christians, kept Jews off the streets in Holy Week, and barred them from public office; canon 67 restricted Jewish moneylending.[2][1]
