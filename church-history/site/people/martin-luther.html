Martin Luther

1483–1546 · Reformation

Augustinian friar, professor of theology, reformer

German friar and professor whose challenge to the sale of indulgences in 1517 sparked the Protestant Reformation and whose German Bible shaped both the church and the German language.

Born
10 November 1483 · Eisleben, County of Mansfeld, Holy Roman Empire[2][4]
Died

What they did

  • Wrote the Ninety-five Theses (1517) disputing the sale of indulgences, which spread rapidly by printing and started the Reformation.[1][2]
  • Taught that sinners are justified before God by grace through faith alone, not by works or purchased merit.[3][4]
  • In 1520 published To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation, The Babylonian Captivity of the Church, and The Freedom of a Christian.[1][2]
  • Refused to recant his writings before Emperor Charles V at the Diet of Worms (April 1521) unless convinced by Scripture and plain reason.[2][4]
  • Translated the New Testament into German while hidden at the Wartburg castle (published 1522), and with colleagues completed the whole Bible by 1534.[2][4]
  • Married the former nun Katharina von Bora (1525), modeling a married Protestant clergy.[2][3]
  • Wrote The Bondage of the Will (1525) in reply to Erasmus on free will and grace.[1][3]
  • Wrote the Small and Large Catechisms (1529) for teaching families and pastors, and many hymns including "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God."[1][4]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Luther was condemned as a heretic by the pope and declared an outlaw by the emperor, but he was never arrested or executed. Princes who protected him kept him out of reach, and he died of natural causes in 1546. Some of his followers were executed.

Excommunications

Luther was excommunicated once, by Pope Leo X in 1521, after he refused to recant within the 60 days the pope had given him in 1520. The sentence was never lifted.

1518

Heresy proceedings in Rome; examined at Augsburg

Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz, whose indulgence campaign Luther had attacked, sent the Ninety-five Theses to Rome, and the papal court opened a heresy case. Luther was summoned to Rome. Instead, at the request of his prince, he was examined at Augsburg in October 1518 by the papal legate, Cardinal Cajetan, who demanded that he recant. Luther refused and left the city secretly at night.[2][3][4]

1520

Condemned by the papal bull Exsurge Domine; books burned

In June 1520 Pope Leo X issued the bull Exsurge Domine. It condemned 41 statements from Luther's writings, ordered his books burned, and gave him 60 days to recant or face excommunication. Johann Eck, who had helped prepare it, and the papal nuncio Girolamo Aleander published it in Germany, and Luther's books were burned at Louvain, Cologne, and Mainz.[2][1][4]

May 1521

Declared an outlaw by the Edict of Worms

After Luther refused to recant at the Diet of Worms, Emperor Charles V issued the Edict of Worms, drafted by the papal nuncio Aleander. It placed Luther under the imperial ban, forbade anyone to shelter him, ordered his arrest, and banned his writings. Frederick the Wise had him taken to the Wartburg castle in a staged kidnapping, to hide him.[2][3][4]

1522

His New Testament banned in Catholic territories

Catholic rulers banned Luther's German New Testament in their lands. In neighboring Ducal (Albertine) Saxony, Duke George ordered copies surrendered and punished people who kept them.[2]

1 July 1523

First of his followers burned: Voes and van den Esschen

Hendrik Voes and Johann van den Esschen, Augustinian friars from Antwerp who held Luther's teachings, were burned for heresy in Brussels. They were the first people executed as Lutherans. Luther wrote his first hymn, "A New Song Here Shall Be Begun," about their deaths.[2][1]
