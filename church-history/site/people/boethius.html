Boethius

c. 477–c. 524 · Early Church

Also known as Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius; Saint Severinus Boethius

Roman senator, philosopher, and Christian theologian

Roman aristocrat whose translations of Aristotle shaped medieval logic, who wrote short theological treatises on the Trinity, and who composed The Consolation of Philosophy in prison before his execution by the Ostrogothic king Theodoric.

Born
c. 475–480 · Rome, Italy[3][5]
Died
c. 524 (some date it 525–526) · Near Pavia (Ticinum), Ostrogothic Italy[2][3]
Executed on King Theodoric's orders for alleged treason; a near-contemporary account says he was tortured and clubbed to death

What they did

  • Planned to translate all of Plato and Aristotle into Latin; his translations of and commentaries on Aristotle's logical works and Porphyry's Isagoge were the main source of logic in the Latin West for some 600 years.[3][4]
  • Wrote short theological treatises (Opuscula sacra) applying logic to the Trinity and the two natures of Christ, which later scholastics commented on.[3][4]
  • Wrote The Consolation of Philosophy in prison, a dialogue in prose and verse in which Lady Philosophy consoles him on fortune, providence, and free will; it was one of the most widely read books of the Middle Ages, translated by Alfred the Great, Chaucer, and Elizabeth I.[1][5]
  • Wrote textbooks on arithmetic and music that were standard in medieval schools.[3]
  • Served as consul (510) and master of offices (522) under the Arian Ostrogothic king Theodoric.[3][5]
  • Is venerated as a martyr at Pavia, as Saint Severinus Boethius, on the view that he died as a Catholic at the hands of an Arian king; historians generally see his death as political.[3][5]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Boethius supporting or carrying out persecution of others. In the Consolation of Philosophy he says that as an official he opposed oppression of the provincials and defended people, such as the senator Albinus, against accusations at Theodoric's court, which led to his own arrest and execution.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Boethius was arrested, condemned, and executed by the Ostrogothic regime. The Consolation gives his own account of the charges.

Excommunications

Boethius was never excommunicated or condemned by any church; he died a Catholic under an Arian king.

c. 523–524

Arrest, condemnation, and execution

When the senator Albinus was accused of treasonable correspondence with the Byzantine emperor Justin, Boethius defended him and was himself accused, on evidence he called forged, of treason and of sacrilege (practicing magic). He was imprisoned, condemned by the Senate without being heard, and executed; his father-in-law Symmachus was executed soon after.[1][2][3]
