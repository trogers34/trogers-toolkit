William Tyndale

c. 1494–1536 · Reformation

Scholar, Bible translator, reformer

English scholar who made the first printed New Testament in English, translated directly from the Greek, and whose wording carried through into the King James Bible; he was executed for heresy in 1536.

Born
c. 1494 (exact date unknown) · Gloucestershire, England, probably near Dursley[4][7]
Died
6 October 1536 (traditional date) · Vilvoorde, near Brussels (Habsburg Netherlands); strangled and burned as a heretic[3][4]

What they did

  • Translated the New Testament into English directly from the Greek. It was the first New Testament printed in English (Worms, 1526); earlier Wycliffite versions were hand-copied translations from the Latin Vulgate.[1][4][7]
  • Aimed to put Scripture in the hands of ordinary people. Foxe reports him telling a learned opponent that, if God spared his life, he would cause "a boy that driveth the plough" to know more of the Scripture than he did.[3][4]
  • Worked in exile after failing to get a bishop's backing in England. His New Testaments were smuggled into England, where church authorities bought up and burned copies, including a public burning at St Paul's in 1526.[4][5]
  • Translated the first five books of the Old Testament (1530) and Jonah (1531) from Hebrew, the first English translations of those books made from the Hebrew.[4][7]
  • Wrote The Obedience of a Christian Man (1528), arguing for the authority of Scripture and for the obedience of subjects to their king rather than to the pope.[2][4]
  • Opposed Henry VIII's plan to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon in The Practice of Prelates (1530), costing himself the king's favor.[4][7]
  • Defended his translation choices and Reformation teaching in a printed debate with Sir Thomas More, including An Answer unto Sir Thomas More's Dialogue (1531).[4][7]
  • Introduced English words such as "Passover" and "scapegoat," along with many phrases still in common use.[4][7]
  • Laid the foundation of later English Bibles: his work was the basis of Matthew's Bible (1537) and the Great Bible (1539). One study estimates that about 83% of the King James New Testament (1611) keeps his wording.[6][4]
  • Was condemned as a heretic and executed at Vilvoorde in 1536. Foxe records his last words as a prayer: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes."[3][4]
