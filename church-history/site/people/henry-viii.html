Henry VIII

1491–1547 · Reformation

Also known as Henry VIII of England; Defender of the Faith (Fidei Defensor)

King of England (1509–1547) and lord, then king, of Ireland; first Supreme Head of the Church of England

King of England who, after the pope would not annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, broke the English church from Rome, made himself its Supreme Head, and dissolved the monasteries. He kept most Catholic doctrine, and his government executed both Catholics who refused his supremacy (including Thomas More and John Fisher) and Protestants who denied traditional teaching.

What they did

  • Broke the English church from papal authority. After Pope Clement VII would not annul his first marriage, a series of statutes (1532–1534) ended appeals and payments to Rome, and the Act of Supremacy (1534) declared the king Supreme Head of the Church of England.[2][4][5]
  • In parish churches the Latin Mass, the seven sacraments, and clerical celibacy remained under Henry, but the pope's name was struck from service books, shrines and monasteries were destroyed, and an English Bible was ordered placed in every church (1538); his son Edward VI then moved the church decisively to Protestantism.[7][6]
  • Dissolved every monastery, priory, convent, and friary in England and Wales (1536–1540), over 800 houses. Their lands and wealth passed to the crown and were largely sold to the nobility and gentry; shrines, including Thomas Becket's at Canterbury (1538), were destroyed.[5][7][10]
  • Wrote, with help from scholars, the Assertio Septem Sacramentorum (1521) defending the seven sacraments and papal authority against Martin Luther, for which Pope Leo X gave him the title "Defender of the Faith," still used by English monarchs.[1][4][10]
  • Authorized the English Bible in churches: royal injunctions of 1538 ordered a Bible in English in every parish, and the Great Bible (1539) was issued with his image on the title page. In 1543 he restricted Bible reading by women and the lower classes.[8][6][5]
  • Kept most traditional Catholic doctrine. The Act of Six Articles (1539) made denial of transubstantiation punishable by burning and upheld clerical celibacy and private Masses; Protestants were burned under it, including Anne Askew (1546).[2][6][3]
  • Had those who refused to accept his supremacy executed for treason, among them Bishop John Fisher, Sir Thomas More, Carthusian monks of the London Charterhouse, and Bridgettine and Franciscan friars (see the list below).[4][5][10]
  • Married six times. He had his marriages to Catherine of Aragon and Anne of Cleves annulled and two wives, Anne Boleyn (1536) and Catherine Howard (1542), beheaded on charges of adultery and treason that many historians regard as doubtful in Anne Boleyn's case.[9][4][10]
  • Crushed the Pilgrimage of Grace (1536–1537), a northern rising that demanded the restoration of the monasteries and the old religion; after a promised pardon, around 200 people were executed, including its leader Robert Aske.[5][6][4]
  • Pursued a shifting middle course. His chief minister Thomas Cromwell and Archbishop Thomas Cranmer advanced reforming policies, while conservative bishops such as Stephen Gardiner shaped the Six Articles and the King's Book (1543); on 30 July 1540 three Protestants were burned and three Catholics hanged together.[8][5][3]

    Not coined by Henry, but granted to him; Parliament later confirmed it as a royal title after the break with Rome, and it still appears on British coins as "F.D." or "Fid. Def."

Misattributed 2

  • "Greensleeves" English ballad tune, first registered in 1580[10]

    Popularly said to have been written by Henry for Anne Boleyn. Its musical style is later than his reign, and there is no contemporary evidence that he wrote it.

  • "Will no one rid me of this turbulent priest?" Often wrongly placed in Henry VIII's mouth[10]

    This later paraphrase belongs to Henry II and Thomas Becket (1170), not Henry VIII, who destroyed Becket's shrine in 1538.

Persecution they supported or carried out

Henry VIII ordered or authorized the deaths of many people for religious and political dissent: Catholics who refused his royal supremacy, rebels of the Pilgrimage of Grace, and Protestants and Anabaptists burned under his heresy laws. He punished both sides, sometimes on the same day, as in July 1540 when three evangelicals were burned and three Catholics hanged together at Smithfield.

See the list of executed people above. Those sentences ran through treason trials, acts of attainder, and the heresy laws, all under the king's authority; ministers such as Thomas Cromwell and later Thomas Wriothesley and Stephen Gardiner carried out much of the work, and Henry's personal part varied from case to case.[2][4][5]

1537

Reprisals after the Pilgrimage of Grace

Ordered or signed it

Against: Northern rebels, including monks and leaders such as Robert Aske, Lord Darcy, and Sir Robert Constable

After the rising of 1536 had dispersed on a promise of pardon, renewed unrest in early 1537 gave Henry grounds to act. He instructed the Duke of Norfolk to impose martial law and make a terrible example, including hanging monks who had resisted the dissolution; around 200 people were executed, the leaders after trials in London.[4][6][10]

Outcome: Resistance in the North was broken and the dissolution of the monasteries continued.

1535–1546

Burnings for heresy under his laws

Ordered or signed it

Against: Dutch Anabaptists (1535), the sacramentarian John Lambert (1538), the evangelicals Robert Barnes, Thomas Garrett, and William Jerome (1540), Anne Askew and others (1546)

Henry kept the late medieval laws against heresy and added the Act of Six Articles (1539), which made denial of transubstantiation punishable by burning. In November 1538 he presided in person over John Lambert's heresy trial in Westminster Hall, disputing with him and pronouncing sentence; Lambert was burned at Smithfield. Bishops such as Stephen Gardiner and Edmund Bonner and the council handled most other cases.[2][3][8][6]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Henry was never arrested, tried, or imprisoned. The main threats he faced over religion were papal: Pope Clement VII threatened excommunication in 1533, and Pope Paul III's bull of excommunication and deposition (drafted 1535, published 1538) called on Catholic rulers to act against him, which none did. The Pilgrimage of Grace (1536) was a large armed rising against his religious policies, but it was aimed at his ministers and policies rather than at deposing him, and he suppressed it.

Excommunications

Henry was excommunicated by Pope Paul III. Clement VII had already threatened him with excommunication in 1533. The sentence was never lifted, and Henry died without reconciling with Rome.

December 1538 – 1539

Papal sentence of deposition and the effort to enforce it

After the destruction of Becket's shrine, Pope Paul III published the bull of excommunication drafted in 1535, declaring Henry deposed and releasing his subjects from obedience. Cardinal Reginald Pole, an English exile, was sent to urge Emperor Charles V and King Francis I of France to enforce it. Henry's government executed Pole's relatives in England (his brother Henry Pole, Lord Montagu, in 1539 and his mother Margaret Pole, Countess of Salisbury, in 1541).[4][5]
