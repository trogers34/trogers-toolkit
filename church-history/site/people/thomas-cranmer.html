Thomas Cranmer

1489–1556 · Reformation

Archbishop of Canterbury (1533–1556), architect of the English Book of Common Prayer

Archbishop of Canterbury under Henry VIII and Edward VI who annulled Henry's first marriage, led the English Reformation's changes to worship, and wrote the Book of Common Prayer. Under Mary I he was tried for heresy, recanted, then withdrew his recantations and was burned.

Born
2 July 1489 · Aslockton, Nottinghamshire, England[3][4]
Died
21 March 1556 · Oxford, England; burned at the stake for heresy[2][3]

What they did

  • Wrote and compiled the Book of Common Prayer (1549, revised 1552), putting all public worship into English. Its language has shaped English-speaking Christianity and the English language ever since.[1][3][4]
  • As Archbishop of Canterbury, declared Henry VIII's marriage to Catherine of Aragon void (1533) and later presided over the annulments of his marriages to Anne Boleyn (1536) and Anne of Cleves (1540).[3][4]
  • Promoted the English Bible: he wrote the preface to the Great Bible (1540) and supported placing an English Bible in every parish church.[3][4]
  • Drafted the Forty-two Articles (1553), the basis of the Church of England's Thirty-nine Articles, and contributed to the Book of Homilies (1547).[3][4]
  • Invited Continental reformers such as Martin Bucer and Peter Martyr Vermigli to England.[3]
  • Took part in heresy proceedings himself as archbishop, including the condemnation of John Frith (burned 1533) and of the Anabaptist Joan Bocher (burned 1550).[3][2]
  • At his execution, withdrew the recantations he had signed and held his right hand, which had signed them, in the fire first.[2][3]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

When the Catholic Mary I became queen in 1553, Cranmer was imprisoned, convicted of treason, and then tried for heresy under papal authority. He signed a series of recantations, but was condemned to be burned anyway. At the stake he renounced the recantations.

Excommunications

Cranmer was condemned by Pope Paul IV in 1555, deprived of his archbishopric, and handed over for execution after being degraded from his orders. The sentence was never lifted by Rome. He is commemorated as a reformer and martyr in the Church of England and the Anglican Communion.

September–November 1553

Imprisoned in the Tower and convicted of treason

Cranmer had signed Edward VI's plan to pass the crown to Lady Jane Grey. After Mary I took the throne, he was sent to the Tower of London, partly after he publicly denounced the restoration of the Mass, and was tried and condemned for treason with Lady Jane Grey.[3][4]

March 1554 – March 1556

Heresy disputation, trial, and degradation at Oxford

Cranmer was moved to Oxford with Nicholas Ridley and Hugh Latimer, held in the Bocardo prison, and made to defend his views on the Eucharist in a public disputation (April 1554). After England was reconciled to Rome, he was tried for heresy in September 1555 before James Brooks, Bishop of Gloucester, acting as papal commissioner. He was made to watch from his prison as Ridley and Latimer were burned (October 1555). Pope Paul IV condemned him in December 1555, and in February 1556 he was formally degraded from his orders by Bishops Edmund Bonner and Thomas Thirlby.[2][3]

21 March 1556

Recantations, then burning

In prison Cranmer signed several recantations accepting papal authority and Catholic doctrine. Under the law, a repentant heretic might be spared, but Mary refused. Brought to the University Church to read a final recantation, he instead renounced all his recantations and rejected the pope. He was taken to the place outside Balliol College where Ridley and Latimer had died and burned, putting his right hand into the flames first.[2][3][4]
