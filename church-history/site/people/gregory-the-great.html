Gregory the Great

c. 540–604 · Early Church

Also known as Pope Gregory I; Saint Gregory the Great; Gregory the Dialogist (in the Eastern churches)

Prefect of Rome, monk, bishop of Rome (pope), 590–604

Roman aristocrat turned monk who, as pope, kept Rome fed and defended during plague and Lombard invasions, sent the mission that converted the English, and wrote works on pastoral care and spirituality that shaped the medieval church.

What they did

  • Sent the mission of Augustine (later first Archbishop of Canterbury) to England in 596, which baptized King Æthelberht of Kent and began the conversion of the Anglo-Saxons.[2][3][5]
  • Wrote the Book of Pastoral Rule (c. 591), a guide for bishops that became the standard handbook for clergy in the medieval West.[1][3]
  • Wrote the Moralia on Job, homilies on the Gospels and Ezekiel, and the Dialogues, which include the main early account of Saint Benedict.[3][4]
  • Organized grain supplies, relief for refugees and the poor, and the defense of Rome when the imperial government could not, effectively acting as the city's ruler.[3][5]
  • Left over 850 letters, a key source for the history of the period.[3]
  • Promoted monasticism and was the first monk to become pope.[3][5]
  • Is honored as a saint in Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, and Lutheran churches, and as a Doctor of the Church; with Leo I, he is one of two popes called "the Great."[5]

Words and sayings

Popularized 2

Already in use before them, but their use made it widely known.

  • "Servant of the servants of God" (servus servorum Dei) His preferred papal title[3][5]

    The phrase was used before him, but Gregory adopted it as his regular title, and popes have used it ever since.

  • The seven deadly sins Moralia on Job[4][5]

    Gregory revised the earlier list of eight evil thoughts from the desert monks Evagrius and Cassian into a list of seven principal vices, the form that medieval Christianity adopted.

Misattributed 2

Commonly credited to them, but not found in their writings.

  • Gregorian chant Named after him, but not his work[5]

    The chant tradition was attributed to him from the 9th century, but it took its familiar form in the Frankish lands of the 8th and 9th centuries, long after his death.

  • "Not Angles, but angels" (Non Angli sed angeli) A story told about him[2]

    Bede reports, as a tradition handed down, that Gregory said this on seeing English slave boys in Rome. It may be legend.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Gregory was never arrested, exiled, or persecuted. As pope he faced Lombard armies besieging Rome (593) and disputes with the emperor and the Patriarch of Constantinople, but no one acted against him personally.

Excommunications

Gregory was never excommunicated. He was pope, and he protested, without any formal break, against the Patriarch of Constantinople's use of the title "ecumenical patriarch."
