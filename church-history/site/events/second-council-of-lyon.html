Second Council of Lyon

1274 · Medieval

Also known as Lyon II; fourteenth ecumenical council (Catholic count)

Pope Gregory X's council that defined the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father and the Son and proclaimed a short-lived reunion with the Byzantine church.

Began
7 May 1274[1][4]
Ended
17 July 1274[1][4]
Place
Lyon, France

Background

  • The Byzantine emperor Michael VIII Palaiologos, who had retaken Constantinople from the Latins in 1261, sought union with Rome to stop Charles of Anjou from launching a Western attack on his empire.[2][3]
  • Gregory X, elected after a vacancy of almost three years, wanted church union, a new crusade, and reform.[1][4]

What happened

  • Thomas Aquinas, summoned to the council, died on the way at Fossanova on 7 March 1274; Bonaventure took a leading part and died during the council on 15 July.[4][3]
  • On 6 July 1274 Michael VIII's envoys accepted the Roman creed, including the filioque, purgatory, seven sacraments, and papal primacy, and the union of the churches was proclaimed.[2][1]
  • The council defined that the Holy Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son 'as from one principle' and condemned those who denied it.[1]
  • The decree Ubi periculum required cardinals to be locked in conclave to elect a pope, with their food reduced if they delayed.[1][4]
  • The council approved a six-year tithe on church income for a crusade that never took place.[1]

Key participants

  • Gregory X Pope who called and led the council
  • Michael VIII Palaiologos Byzantine emperor who sought the union
  • Bonaventure Franciscan cardinal; died during the council
  • Thomas Aquinas Summoned to the council, but died on the way

Outcomes and significance

  • Most Byzantine clergy and people rejected the union. After Michael VIII died in 1282, his son Andronikos II repudiated it.[2][3]
  • The failure left the filioque and papal primacy as central points of division, revisited at the Council of Florence.[3][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The union was enforced in Byzantium by force, against strong opposition.

1275–1282

Michael VIII's enforcement of the union

Michael VIII deposed Patriarch Joseph I, who opposed the union, installed the pro-union John Bekkos, and punished opponents, including members of his own family, with imprisonment, confiscation, exile, and in some cases blinding or mutilation. Later Orthodox tradition also tells of monks on Mount Athos being killed by unionist forces, accounts that historians treat with caution.[2][3]
