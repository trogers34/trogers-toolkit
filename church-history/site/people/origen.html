Origen

c. 185–c. 253 · Early Church

Also known as Origen of Alexandria; Origen Adamantius

Teacher, biblical scholar, and theologian of Alexandria and Caesarea

The most learned and prolific Christian scholar of the early church: founder of systematic textual criticism of the Bible (the Hexapla), author of the first Christian systematic theology (On First Principles), and defender of Christianity against the philosopher Celsus. Tortured in the Decian persecution, he was deprived of his priesthood by his own bishop, and some of his speculative ideas were condemned as heresy three centuries after his death.

What they did

  • Compiled the Hexapla, setting the Hebrew Old Testament, a Greek transliteration, and four Greek translations side by side; it was the first large work of biblical textual criticism and was later used by Jerome.[1][5]
  • Wrote On First Principles, the first attempt at a systematic Christian theology, covering God, the Trinity, rational beings, free will, and how to interpret Scripture; it survives mainly in Rufinus's softened Latin translation.[2][4]
  • Developed a method of reading Scripture on several levels (literal, moral, and spiritual), which shaped Christian biblical interpretation for over a thousand years.[2][5]
  • Wrote Against Celsus (c. 248), a point-by-point answer to the pagan philosopher Celsus's attack on Christianity and the most important early Christian apologetic work.[3][7]
  • Produced an enormous body of commentaries and sermons on almost every book of the Bible; Jerome said he wrote more than most people could read. Much is lost, and much survives only in Latin translation.[1][4]
  • Ran a school at Caesarea where he taught philosophy and Scripture together; his pupils included Gregory Thaumaturgus, whose farewell address describes his teaching.[1][5]
  • Taught some speculative ideas that later generations judged heretical: the pre-existence of souls, a hierarchy within the Trinity, and the hope that all rational beings, possibly even the devil, might finally be restored to God (apokatastasis); he presented some of these as exploration rather than settled doctrine.[4][6]
  • According to Eusebius, he castrated himself as a young man, taking Matthew 19:12 literally; some modern scholars doubt the story, which his opponents later used against him.[1][5]
  • Shaped later theology deeply even after his condemnation: Athanasius, the Cappadocian Fathers, and Ambrose drew on him, and his spiritual reading of the Song of Songs influenced medieval mystics.[4][7]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Origen supporting or carrying out persecution of anyone. He argued against pagan critics and against teachers he judged heretical (such as the Valentinians and Marcionites) in writing and debate only, for example in the Dialogue with Heraclides, and in Against Celsus he defended Christians' refusal to take up arms.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Origen lost his father to persecution, was tortured in the Decian persecution, was expelled and deprived of his priesthood by his own bishop, and was condemned as a heretic long after his death. His friend and patron Ambrose was also imprisoned under Maximinus Thrax (235).

Excommunications

Condemned as a heretic by name about three centuries after his death. He is not venerated as a saint in the Catholic or Orthodox churches, though he is widely honoured as a church father in a broad sense.

Martyrdom of his father Leonides

His father was beheaded in the persecution under Septimius Severus and the family's property was confiscated. Eusebius says Origen wanted to give himself up and was stopped only because his mother hid his clothes.[1][5]

c. 231–232

Expulsion and deprivation by Bishop Demetrius

After bishops Theoctistus of Caesarea and Alexander of Jerusalem ordained him presbyter without the consent of his own bishop, Demetrius of Alexandria called two synods: the first banished him from Alexandria, the second deprived him of the priesthood. Demetrius reportedly also raised his self-castration as a bar to ordination and circulated the decision to other churches, and Rome under Pontian concurred.[1][5][4]

250–251

Imprisonment and torture under Decius

In the persecution ordered by Emperor Decius, he was imprisoned, put in an iron collar, and had his legs stretched in the stocks for many days, threatened with fire; his torturers tried to make him deny Christ without killing him. Eusebius says he survived and wrote letters afterwards.[1][4]

393–404 (after his death)

The first Origenist controversy

Epiphanius of Salamis attacked Origen's ideas; Theophilus of Alexandria, after first supporting Origenist monks, condemned Origen at a synod in 400 and drove Origenist monks, including the 'Tall Brothers', out of the Egyptian desert. Jerome turned against Origen and quarrelled bitterly with his old friend Rufinus, who had translated him.[6][4]
