Thomas Aquinas

c. 1225–1274 · Medieval

Also known as Saint Thomas Aquinas; the "Angelic Doctor"

Dominican friar, theologian, and philosopher

Dominican friar who joined Aristotle's philosophy to Christian theology in a systematic body of work, above all the Summa Theologiae, and became the most influential theologian of the Catholic Church.

Born
c. 1225 · Roccasecca, Kingdom of Sicily (central Italy)[3][6]
Died
7 March 1274 · Fossanova Abbey, Italy, on his way to the Second Council of Lyon[3][6]

What they did

  • Wrote the Summa Theologiae (c. 1265–1273), a vast, systematic account of Christian theology that became the standard textbook of Catholic theology.[1][3][6]
  • Wrote the Summa contra Gentiles (c. 1259–1265), setting out the reasons for Christian belief for readers who did not accept the Bible's authority.[3][6]
  • Combined the newly recovered philosophy of Aristotle with Christian theology, arguing that faith and reason cannot truly contradict each other.[5][6]
  • Set out the "Five Ways," five arguments for the existence of God from motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection, and purpose in nature.[1][5]
  • Wrote extensive commentaries on Scripture and on Aristotle, along with hundreds of disputed questions.[3][4]
  • Composed hymns for the feast of Corpus Christi that are still sung, including Pange lingua (whose last verses are the Tantum ergo) and Panis angelicus.[3][6]
  • Was canonized in 1323, declared a Doctor of the Church in 1567, and named by Pope Leo XIII (1879) as the model for Catholic philosophy and theology.[3][6]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Aquinas was never arrested or tried. As a young man, his own family held him captive for about a year to stop him becoming a Dominican friar. As a friar teaching in Paris, he was caught up in the hostility of the university's secular masters toward the mendicant orders. After his death, some of his teachings were among those condemned at Paris and Oxford in 1277, until Paris withdrew the condemnation as it touched him in 1325.

Excommunications

Aquinas was never excommunicated. Some of his teachings were caught up in the 1277 condemnations of propositions at Paris and Oxford, after his death. Those condemnations targeted propositions, not him by name, and Paris revoked its condemnation as it applied to him in 1325.

1244–1245

Held captive by his own family

Thomas's noble family wanted him to become a Benedictine and, eventually, the powerful abbot of Monte Cassino. When he joined the new, begging Dominican order, his mother had his brothers seize him on the road and hold him at the family's castles. According to his early biographer, his brothers even sent a woman to tempt him, and he drove her out with a burning stick from the fire.[2][3]

1255–1257

Opposed as a friar by the Paris secular masters

During a bitter dispute over the place of the mendicant friars in the University of Paris, the secular masters, led by William of Saint-Amour, refused to admit Thomas to their guild as a master, and friars in Paris faced hostility from parts of the university and the city.[3][4]

1277 (three years after his death)

Some of his teachings condemned at Paris and Oxford

In March 1277 Étienne Tempier, Bishop of Paris, condemned 219 propositions taught in the arts faculty, some of which matched positions held by Thomas. Days later Robert Kilwardby, Archbishop of Canterbury and himself a Dominican, condemned propositions at Oxford that included Thomas's teaching that a human being has only one substantial form. His Franciscan successor John Pecham renewed the Oxford condemnation in 1284.[3][5]
