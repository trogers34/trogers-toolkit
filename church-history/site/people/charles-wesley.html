Charles Wesley

1707–1788 · Modern

Anglican priest, Methodist preacher, and hymn writer

Younger brother of John Wesley and co-founder of Methodism, who wrote thousands of hymns, many still sung across the world's churches.

Born
18 December 1707 (Old Style) · Epworth, Lincolnshire, England[2][4]
Died
29 March 1788 · London, England[2][4]
Natural causes in old age

What they did

  • Wrote more than 6,000 hymns and sacred poems, by most counts the largest body of hymns by any English writer.[2][4]
  • Wrote hymns still widely sung, including "Hark! the Herald Angels Sing", "Christ the Lord Is Risen Today", "O for a Thousand Tongues to Sing", "And Can It Be", "Jesus, Lover of My Soul", and "Love Divine, All Loves Excelling".[2][4]
  • Started the small Oxford group of students devoted to prayer, study, and charity that was mocked as the "Holy Club" and "Methodists".[2][4]
  • Preached across England, Wales, and Ireland as one of the first Methodist field preachers from 1739 until the mid-1750s.[1][2]
  • Used hymns to teach Methodist doctrine, especially salvation by grace through faith, assurance, and holiness; John called the 1780 Collection of Hymns 'a little body of experimental and practical divinity'.[2][3]
  • Insisted that Methodists stay within the Church of England, opposing lay preachers giving communion and John's 1784 ordinations; he wrote a satirical verse asking, of John and Thomas Coke, 'But who laid hands on him?'[3][4]
  • In 1749 rode to intervene when John planned to marry the widow Grace Murray, and hurried her marriage to the preacher John Bennet; the episode caused a lasting rift between the brothers.[2][3]
  • Remained a priest of the Church of England and asked to be buried in his parish churchyard at Marylebone rather than in John's Methodist chapel.[3][4]

    Usually credited to Charles Wesley alone, but the opening as now sung is George Whitefield's 1753 revision of Charles's 'Hark how all the welkin rings'. The tune by Felix Mendelssohn was added in the 1850s.

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Charles Wesley supporting or carrying out persecution of others. Some of his hymns for the 1745 Jacobite rising use anti-Catholic language, but he did not call for action against Catholics, and he wrote verses condemning the rioters of the 1780 Gordon Riots.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Charles Wesley was never imprisoned or executed. Like other early Methodist preachers he was shut out of many Church of England pulpits, attacked by mobs, and once accused of disloyalty to the crown.

Excommunications

Charles Wesley was never excommunicated or formally disciplined by the Church of England, and he died a priest of that church.

March 1744

Accused of praying for the Stuart pretender

During the Jacobite invasion scare of 1744, witnesses claimed that Charles had prayed in Yorkshire for the return of the exiled Stuarts, after he prayed for the Lord to 'call home his banished ones'. He was summoned before the magistrates at Wakefield, explained that he meant sinners, not the Stuarts, and was cleared.[1][2]

February 1747

Mob attack at Devizes

When Charles came to preach in Devizes, Wiltshire, a mob besieged the house where he was staying, sprayed it with water from the town fire engine, and attacked his horses. His journal says the local curate and some leading townsmen encouraged the mob.[1][2]
