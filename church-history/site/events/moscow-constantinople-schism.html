Moscow–Constantinople Schism

2018–2026 · Modern

Also known as The 2018 schism over Ukrainian autocephaly

The break of communion by the Russian Orthodox Church with the Ecumenical Patriarchate in 2018, after Constantinople moved to grant independence to a new Orthodox Church of Ukraine; it remains unresolved.

Began
15 October 2018 (Moscow breaks communion)[2][3]
Ended
Unresolved (as of 2026)[4]
Place
Constantinople (Istanbul), Moscow, and Kyiv

Background

  • Since 1686 the Kyiv metropolis had been under Moscow. After Ukraine's independence in 1991, Ukrainian Orthodoxy split between a church linked to Moscow and two unrecognized churches, one led by Filaret Denysenko, whom Moscow anathematized in 1997.[3][4]
  • After Russia's annexation of Crimea (2014) and the war in eastern Ukraine, the Ukrainian government and many clergy asked Constantinople for an independent church.[3][4]

What happened

  • On 11 October 2018 the synod of the Ecumenical Patriarchate decided to grant autocephaly to Ukraine, revoked the 1686 letter giving Moscow authority over Kyiv, and restored Filaret and other unrecognized bishops to communion.[1][4]
  • On 15 October 2018 the Holy Synod of the Russian Orthodox Church, meeting in Minsk, broke communion with the Ecumenical Patriarchate.[2][4]
  • A unification council in Kyiv on 15 December 2018 formed the Orthodox Church of Ukraine and elected Metropolitan Epifaniy; Patriarch Bartholomew signed its tomos of autocephaly on 5 January 2019.[4]
  • Moscow also broke communion with the primates of Greece, Alexandria, and Cyprus when they recognized the new church, and in 2021 set up its own exarchate in Africa, in Alexandria's territory.[4]

Key participants

  • Bartholomew I Ecumenical Patriarch
  • Kirill Patriarch of Moscow
  • Epifaniy (Dumenko) Metropolitan of Kyiv of the Orthodox Church of Ukraine
  • Filaret (Denysenko) Former head of the Kyiv Patriarchate
  • Onufriy (Berezovsky) Metropolitan of the Moscow-linked Ukrainian Orthodox Church

Outcomes and significance

  • The world's largest Orthodox church is out of communion with the Ecumenical Patriarchate and several other churches, the deepest division in Orthodoxy since the 20th century.[4][3]
  • After Russia's 2022 invasion, the Moscow-linked Ukrainian Orthodox Church declared its 'full independence' from Moscow (May 2022), though both Kyiv and Moscow dispute what that means.[4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The church conflict is bound up with Russia's war on Ukraine, and both states have taken action against churches they see as tied to the other side.

2022–2024

Ukrainian measures against the Moscow-linked church

After Russia's 2022 invasion, Ukraine's security service (SBU) searched churches and monasteries of the Ukrainian Orthodox Church, including the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra; several of its clergy were charged with collaboration, and its lease on parts of the Lavra was ended. In August 2024 parliament passed a law allowing courts to ban religious bodies linked to the Russian Orthodox Church.[4]

2014–present

Pressure on the Orthodox Church of Ukraine in Russian-occupied territory

In Crimea and other areas occupied by Russia, parishes of the Kyiv Patriarchate and later the Orthodox Church of Ukraine lost their churches and clergy were pressured to leave.[4]
