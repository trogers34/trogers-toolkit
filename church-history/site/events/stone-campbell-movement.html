Stone-Campbell Movement

1804–1832 · Modern

Also known as Restoration Movement; American Restoration Movement

The American movement to restore New Testament Christianity without creeds, led by Barton W. Stone and Thomas and Alexander Campbell, which produced the Churches of Christ, the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ), and the independent Christian Churches.

Began
28 June 1804[1][3]
Ended
1 January 1832[3][4]
Place
Kentucky, Pennsylvania, Virginia (now West Virginia), and the American frontier

Background

  • Barton W. Stone, a Presbyterian minister, hosted the Cane Ridge revival (1801); he and others who preached free salvation for all were censured by the Synod of Kentucky and formed their own Springfield Presbytery (1803).[3][6]
  • Thomas Campbell, a Seceder Presbyterian minister from Ireland, was censured in Pennsylvania (1807) for giving communion to Presbyterians of other branches; he and his son Alexander called for Christian unity on the Bible alone.[3][4]

What happened

  • In The Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery (1804), Stone's group dissolved their presbytery 'to sink into union with the Body of Christ at large' and called themselves simply Christians.[1][3]
  • Thomas Campbell's Declaration and Address (1809) argued that 'the church of Christ upon earth is essentially, intentionally, and constitutionally one' and that nothing should be required of Christians that the Bible does not expressly teach.[2][3]
  • The Campbells adopted believers' baptism by immersion (1812), taught weekly communion and the independence of congregations, and Alexander Campbell became a leading debater and editor.[3][4]
  • Stone's and Campbell's followers united in 1832, sealed by a handshake between 'Raccoon' John Smith and Stone at Lexington, Kentucky.[3][4]

Key participants

  • Barton W. Stone Founder of the 'Christian' churches in Kentucky
  • Thomas Campbell Author of the Declaration and Address
  • Alexander Campbell Leading preacher, editor, and founder of Bethany College (1840)
  • Walter Scott Evangelist who taught a 'five-finger' plan of salvation
  • 'Raccoon' John Smith Preacher who helped unite the two groups

Outcomes and significance

  • Disputes over instrumental music in worship and missionary societies divided the movement; the U.S. religious census of 1906 listed the a cappella Churches of Christ separately from the Disciples of Christ.[5][3]
  • A second division followed in the 20th century, when congregations opposed to the Disciples' liberal theology and 1968 restructuring formed the independent Christian Churches and Churches of Christ.[3][7]
  • The three branches together have millions of members, mostly in the United States.[3][7]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

No members of the movement were arrested or executed for their faith; opposition took the form of church censures and exclusion.
