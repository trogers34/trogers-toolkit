Methodist Split of 1844

1844–1845 · Modern

The division of the Methodist Episcopal Church, then the largest American denomination, into Northern and Southern churches after its General Conference asked a slaveholding bishop to stop serving.

Began
May–June 1844[1][2]
Ended
May 1845[1][3]
Place
New York and Louisville, Kentucky

Background

  • Wesley and early American Methodists condemned slavery, but the church relaxed its rules to grow in the South; abolitionists who pressed the issue, such as Orange Scott, were disciplined and in 1843 left to form the Wesleyan Methodist Connection.[1][2]

What happened

  • Bishop James O. Andrew of Georgia had become a slaveholder through inheritance and marriage. The 1844 General Conference voted 111 to 69 that he 'desist from the exercise of this office so long as this impediment remains'.[1][2]
  • Southern delegates protested, and the conference adopted a Plan of Separation allowing the Southern conferences to form their own church.[1][3]
  • The Methodist Episcopal Church, South, was organized at Louisville in May 1845.[1][3]

Key participants

  • James O. Andrew Slaveholding bishop at the center of the dispute
  • Orange Scott Abolitionist who founded the Wesleyan Methodist Connection (1843)

Outcomes and significance

  • The Northern church repudiated the Plan of Separation in 1848, leading to a dispute over church property that the U.S. Supreme Court settled in the Southern church's favor in Smith v. Swormstedt (1854).[1][2]
  • The split foreshadowed the Civil War. The churches reunited only in 1939, with Black Methodists placed in a separate, segregated Central Jurisdiction until 1968.[2][3]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

No one was persecuted in the split itself; the issue was the enslavement of Black Americans, which Southern Methodists defended.
