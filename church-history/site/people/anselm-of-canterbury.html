Anselm of Canterbury

c. 1033–1109 · Medieval

Also known as Anselm of Aosta; Anselm of Bec

Benedictine monk, abbot of Bec, archbishop of Canterbury, and theologian

Monk and archbishop of Canterbury, often called the founder of scholasticism, known for the ontological argument for God's existence and the satisfaction theory of the atonement.

Born
c. 1033 · Aosta, Kingdom of Burgundy (modern Italy)[4][5]
Died
21 April 1109 · Canterbury, England[1][4]
Natural causes in old age, after a long illness

What they did

  • Set out in the Proslogion the argument that God is 'that than which nothing greater can be conceived' and therefore must exist in reality, later called the ontological argument; the monk Gaunilo of Marmoutiers replied in his own time, and philosophers have debated it ever since.[3][5]
  • Wrote Cur Deus Homo ('Why God Became Man'), arguing that Christ's death made satisfaction for the dishonor sin does to God, an account of the atonement that shaped Western theology.[3][4]
  • Described theology as 'faith seeking understanding' and used reason to explain Christian belief without appealing to authority, which led later writers to call him the father of scholasticism.[3][5]
  • Wrote the Monologion (1076) and devotional prayers and meditations that were widely copied in the Middle Ages.[3][4]
  • Led Bec as prior and abbot for 30 years, making it one of the leading schools of Europe.[1][4]
  • As archbishop, defended the rights of the church against William II and Henry I, insisting that the king could not appoint bishops by investing them with ring and staff; the 1107 compromise let the king receive homage while the church invested bishops.[2][4]
  • Presided over the Council of Westminster (1102), which condemned the 'wicked trade' of selling people like animals in England, and enforced celibacy on the English clergy, removing married priests from their churches.[2][4]
  • Was declared a Doctor of the Church by Pope Clement XI in 1720.[5]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Anselm supporting or carrying out persecution of others. He wrote against the teaching of Roscelin of Compiègne after Roscelin had been made to recant at Soissons in 1092, and as archbishop he presided over the Council of Westminster (1102), whose decrees removed married priests from office; both were matters of church discipline rather than physical persecution.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Anselm was never arrested. As archbishop he was twice forced into exile by English kings, who seized the revenues of Canterbury while he was away.

Excommunications

Anselm was never excommunicated. He himself threatened Henry I with excommunication in 1105 but did not pronounce it.

February–March 1095

Council of Rockingham

Anselm asked William II's permission to go to Rome to receive his pallium from Pope Urban II, whom the king had not recognized. At a council at Rockingham the king demanded that Anselm give up his obedience to the pope; most of the bishops, led by William of St-Calais, bishop of Durham, sided with the king and renounced obedience to Anselm, but the barons would not.[2][4]

October 1097 – 1100

First exile, under William II

After further quarrels over church reform and the quality of knights he supplied to the king, Anselm left England without the king's leave to appeal to the pope. William II seized the lands and revenues of Canterbury.[2][4]

1103–1106

Second exile, under Henry I

Anselm refused to do homage to Henry I or consecrate bishops the king had invested, following the papal ban on lay investiture. He went to Rome and was not allowed back on his own terms; Henry seized Canterbury's revenues. Anselm threatened to excommunicate the king.[2][4]
