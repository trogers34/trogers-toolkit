The Afscheiding (Secession of 1834)

1834–1847 · Modern

The secession of orthodox Calvinists, led by Hendrik de Cock, from the state-controlled Dutch Reformed Church, whose members were fined, quartered with soldiers, and jailed, and many of whom emigrated to America.

Began
13–14 October 1834[1][2]
Ended
1846–1847[2]
Place
Ulrum, Groningen, and across the Netherlands

Background

  • In 1816 King William I reorganized the national church under state-appointed boards; orthodox critics complained of tolerated liberal preaching and of new hymns replacing the Psalms.[1][3]
  • Hendrik de Cock, minister at Ulrum, was suspended in 1833 for attacking liberal colleagues and baptizing children from other parishes, and deposed in 1834.[1][2]

What happened

  • De Cock's congregation declared its secession and 'return' to the Reformed confessions in October 1834; other ministers, including Hendrik Scholte, Simon van Velzen, Anthony Brummelkamp, and Albertus van Raalte, joined the movement.[1][2]
  • The government treated the seceders' meetings as illegal under a law forbidding gatherings of more than 20 people without permission.[1]
  • The seceders were granted conditional toleration from 1839–40 under King William II, and formed what became the Christian Reformed Church in the Netherlands.[1][3]

Key participants

  • Hendrik de Cock Minister at Ulrum who led the secession
  • Hendrik Scholte Seceder minister who founded Pella, Iowa
  • Albertus van Raalte Seceder minister who founded Holland, Michigan
  • William I King whose government suppressed the seceders

Outcomes and significance

  • Thousands of seceders emigrated to the American Midwest, founding Dutch Reformed communities in Michigan and Iowa.[2]
  • Most seceder churches united with Kuyper's Doleantie churches in 1892; a minority continued as the Christian Reformed Churches in the Netherlands.[1][3]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

1834–1839

Fines, quartering, and imprisonment of the seceders

Authorities broke up seceder services, fined leaders and hosts heavily, and quartered soldiers in their homes; De Cock was imprisoned for three months in 1835, and other ministers were jailed or fined repeatedly.[1][2]
