Johann von Staupitz

c. 1460–1524 · Reformation

Also known as Johannes von Staupitz

Augustinian friar, theologian, vicar general of the German Observant Augustinians, Martin Luther's superior and confessor

Theologian and head of the reformed (Observant) Augustinian friars in Germany who guided the young Martin Luther, sent him to teach at Wittenberg, and comforted him in his spiritual anguish by pointing him to Christ's grace. He protected Luther in 1518 but never joined the Reformation, resigned his office in 1520, submitted to the pope, and died a Benedictine abbot in Salzburg.

What they did

  • Served as Luther's superior, confessor, and spiritual guide: when Luther was tormented by fear of God's judgement and predestination, Staupitz told him to look to the wounds of Christ, and Luther later credited him with first showing him the gospel.[1][3]
  • Helped found the theology faculty at the University of Wittenberg (1502) and placed Luther there, ordering him to take his doctorate (1512) and succeed him as professor of Bible.[4][5]
  • Led the reform-minded Observant Augustinians in Germany for seventeen years, and tried to unite them with the less strict Conventual houses (1510); the plan was resisted by seven Observant houses, and Luther's journey to Rome (1510–11) was connected with the dispute.[4][2]
  • Wrote and preached on grace, predestination, and the love of God (On the Love of God, 1518), in a late-medieval Augustinian theology that stressed God's mercy; scholars debate how much of Luther's thought came from him.[2][3]
  • Protected Luther in 1518: he arranged for him to present his theology at Heidelberg and, at Augsburg, released him from his vow of obedience so that the order would not be bound to hand him over to Cardinal Cajetan.[4][3]
  • Did not follow Luther out of the Catholic Church: he resigned his office (1520), submitted to the pope's judgement when pressed in 1520–21, and ended his life as a Benedictine abbot, while writing to Luther in 1524 that his love for him was unchanged.[3][4]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Staupitz supporting or carrying out persecution of anyone. In 1518 he was under orders from the head of his order to have Luther arrested and sent to Rome, and he did not carry them out.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Staupitz was never arrested or tried, but Rome and the leaders of his order pressed him to discipline Luther, and after Luther's condemnation he was pressed to distance himself from him.

Excommunications

Staupitz was never excommunicated. He died in communion with Rome as a Benedictine abbot.

1518

Ordered to arrest Luther

Following papal instructions, Gabriele della Volta, prior general of the Augustinian order, wrote ordering the German province to arrest Luther and send him to Rome. Staupitz did not act on it and instead released Luther from his obedience at Augsburg.[4][3]

1520–1521

Pressed to submit after Luther's condemnation

After the bull Exsurge Domine condemned Luther (1520), Rome, through Cardinal Matthäus Lang, archbishop of Salzburg, demanded that Staupitz renounce Luther's errors. He submitted his writings to the pope's judgement, which Luther regretted as a weakness.[3][4]
