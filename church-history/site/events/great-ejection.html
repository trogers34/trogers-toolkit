Great Ejection of 1662

1662 · Modern

The removal of about 2,000 Puritan ministers from the Church of England who would not accept the Act of Uniformity, which created English Nonconformity as a separate body of churches.

Began
19 May 1662[2][3]
Ended
24 August 1662 (St Bartholomew's Day)[2][1]
Place
England and Wales

Background

  • After the Restoration of Charles II (1660), the Cavalier Parliament set out to restore the Church of England with bishops and the Prayer Book after two decades of Puritan rule.[2][3]

What happened

  • The Act required every minister to be ordained by a bishop, to declare 'unfeigned assent and consent' to everything in the revised 1662 Book of Common Prayer, and to renounce the Solemn League and Covenant, by St Bartholomew's Day 1662.[2][3]
  • Including those removed since 1660, about 2,000 ministers, roughly a fifth of the clergy, lost their livings, among them Richard Baxter and Edmund Calamy.[1][2]
  • The 'Clarendon Code' followed: the Conventicle Act (1664) banned unauthorized worship of more than five people, and the Five Mile Act (1665) barred ejected ministers from coming within five miles of their former parishes or any town.[2][3]

Key participants

  • Charles II King, who had promised 'liberty to tender consciences' at Breda (1660)
  • Edward Hyde, Earl of Clarendon Lord Chancellor whose name was given to the code
  • Gilbert Sheldon Bishop of London, later archbishop of Canterbury; strong supporter of uniformity
  • Richard Baxter Leading Puritan minister ejected in 1662

Outcomes and significance

  • The ejected ministers and their followers formed the Presbyterian, Congregational (Independent), and Baptist churches of English Nonconformity.[2][3]
  • The Toleration Act of 1689 allowed Nonconformists to worship, but they were barred from universities and public office until the 19th century.[2][3]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Thousands of Nonconformists were fined and imprisoned under the Clarendon Code between 1662 and 1689.

1662–1689

Prosecution of Nonconformists under the Clarendon Code

Ejected ministers and lay Nonconformists were fined, had property seized, and were imprisoned for holding services; Quakers and Baptists suffered most. John Bunyan spent 12 years in Bedford jail, and Richard Baxter was jailed in 1685 after trial before Judge Jeffreys.[2][3]
