John Wesley

1703–1791 · Modern

Anglican priest, evangelist, founder of Methodism

Anglican priest whose itinerant preaching and network of societies launched the Methodist movement in Britain and America.

Born
17 June 1703 (Old Style; 28 June New Style) · Epworth, Lincolnshire, England[2][3]
Died
2 March 1791 · London, England[2][3]

What they did

  • With his brother Charles led a small Oxford group, mocked as the "Holy Club" and "Methodists," devoted to prayer, study, and visiting prisoners.[2][3]
  • Recorded in his Journal his experience at a meeting in Aldersgate Street on 24 May 1738, where he felt assured of salvation by faith in Christ.[1][2]
  • Took up open-air preaching in 1739 at George Whitefield's urging and traveled an estimated 250,000 miles on horseback over five decades.[1][3]
  • Organized converts into societies, classes, and bands, with lay preachers and an annual Conference beginning in 1744.[2][3]
  • Taught Christian perfection: that believers can be made perfect in love in this life.[2]
  • Published sermons, tracts, and a Christian Library for ordinary readers, and with Charles produced hymnbooks for the movement.[2][3]
  • Ordained Thomas Coke as superintendent for American Methodists in 1784, opening the way for the Methodist Episcopal Church.[2][3]
  • Spoke against the slave trade; his Thoughts upon Slavery (1774) and his final known letter (1791, to William Wilberforce) urged its abolition.[2][3]
