John Oldcastle

c. 1378–1417 · Medieval

Also known as Sir John Oldcastle; Lord Cobham

English knight, soldier, member of Parliament, and Lollard leader

English knight and friend of Henry V who protected Wycliffe's Lollard followers. He was condemned as a heretic, escaped from the Tower of London, led a failed Lollard uprising, and after four years as a fugitive was hanged and burned.

Born
c. 1378 (exact date unknown) · Almeley, Herefordshire, England[4][6]
Died
14 December 1417 · St Giles's Fields, London; hanged and burned as a traitor and heretic[2][3]

What they did

  • Fought for Henry IV in Wales and became a companion of Prince Henry, the future Henry V.[4][6]
  • Rose through marriage to become Lord Cobham and a member of the House of Lords.[4][6]
  • Became the most prominent lay supporter of the Lollards, the followers of John Wycliffe, protecting unlicensed preachers on his estates and spreading their books.[5][4]
  • At his heresy trial in 1413, rejected transubstantiation, the need to confess to a priest, the veneration of images, and pilgrimages, and called the pope Antichrist.[1][3]
  • Was regarded as a martyr by later Protestant writers, especially John Foxe.[3][5]
  • Was the original model for Shakespeare's Falstaff, who was first named Oldcastle in Henry IV, Part 1. The name was changed after objections from Oldcastle's descendants, and the epilogue to Part 2 declares that "Oldcastle died a martyr, and this is not the man."[6]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Oldcastle was condemned as a heretic by the Archbishop of Canterbury, escaped from the Tower of London, and was outlawed after the failed Lollard rising of 1414. Captured in Wales in 1417, he was condemned by Parliament as a traitor and was hanged and burned. Historians debate how far the 1414 rising was a real plot and how far the government exaggerated it.

Excommunications

Oldcastle was condemned as a heretic and excommunicated by Archbishop Thomas Arundel in September 1413. The sentence was never lifted. Later Protestants, led by John Foxe, honored him as a martyr.

1410

Interdict on churches on his estates

Because an unlicensed Lollard chaplain had been preaching on Oldcastle's estates in Kent, Archbishop Arundel placed several local churches, including Cooling, Hoo, and Halstow, under interdict, halting services there.[5][4]

August–October 1413

Heresy trial before Archbishop Arundel; escape from the Tower

Lollard books belonging to Oldcastle were found at a London illuminator's shop. Henry V tried privately to persuade his old friend to submit, then allowed the church to proceed. Oldcastle was arrested, held in the Tower, and examined in September 1413 at St Paul's and at the Blackfriars by Archbishop Arundel with other bishops. He refused to accept the church's teaching on the sacraments and the pope, and was condemned as a heretic. The king granted forty days' delay, and in October Oldcastle escaped from the Tower with outside help.[1][2][3]

9–10 January 1414

The Lollard rising crushed at St Giles's Fields

Lollards planned to gather at St Giles's Fields outside London, reportedly to seize the king. Henry V, warned in advance, occupied the field and broke up the gathering. Dozens of Lollards were hanged, and some burned, in the following days. Oldcastle was outlawed and attainted for treason.[2][4][5]

November–14 December 1417

Capture in Wales and execution

Oldcastle was captured, after a fight in which he was injured, by men of Edward Charlton, Lord of Powys, in mid-Wales and brought to London. With Henry V in France, Parliament, under the king's brother John, Duke of Bedford, condemned him on his earlier outlawry for treason. He was taken to St Giles's Fields the same day, hanged in chains, and burned.[2][3][4]
