Lutheran Churches: Beginnings and Splits

1530–2010 · Reformation

How the Lutheran churches took shape around the Augsburg Confession, became state churches in Germany and Scandinavia, and divided in Germany and America over union with the Reformed, confessional strictness, and liberal theology.

Began
25 June 1530[1][2]
Ended
August 2010[3]
Place
Germany, Scandinavia, and North America

Background

  • Luther's reform movement (from 1517) led princes and cities across Germany and Scandinavia to establish evangelical churches under their own authority.[2][5]

What happened

  • The Augsburg Confession (1530), written mainly by Philip Melanchthon, became the defining statement of Lutheran faith; after quarrels following Luther's death, the Formula of Concord (1577), partly written by Martin Chemnitz, united most Lutherans.[1][2]
  • Lutheranism became the state religion of much of Germany and of the Scandinavian kingdoms.[2][5]
  • In 1817 King Frederick William III of Prussia merged the Lutheran and Reformed churches, and confessional 'Old Lutherans' who resisted were persecuted and emigrated.[4][2]
  • In America, Lutheran immigrants formed many synods by language and theology, which merged and divided through the 20th century over biblical authority, fellowship, and later sexuality.[3][5]

Key participants

  • Martin Luther Reformer from whom the churches take their name
  • Philip Melanchthon Author of the Augsburg Confession
  • Martin Chemnitz A principal author of the Formula of Concord
  • C. F. W. Walther First president of the Missouri Synod

Outcomes and significance

  • Lutherans number tens of millions worldwide, with the Lutheran World Federation and the more conservative International Lutheran Council as the main bodies.[2][5]
  • In 1999 the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church signed the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification.[2][5]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The main persecution within Lutheran history was the Prussian state's suppression of Old Lutherans (see the Prussian Union event). Lutheran rulers in turn suppressed Reformed, Anabaptist, and Catholic dissenters in their territories.

1548–1552

The Augsburg Interim

After defeating the Lutheran princes, Charles V imposed the Augsburg Interim, restoring much Catholic practice; Lutheran pastors who refused it, perhaps several hundred, were removed, and Magdeburg resisted under siege.[2][5]
