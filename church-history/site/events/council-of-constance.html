Council of Constance

1414–1418 · Medieval

Also known as Sixteenth ecumenical council (Catholic count)

The council that ended the Western Schism of three rival popes, condemned John Wycliffe, and burned Jan Hus and Jerome of Prague.

Began
5 November 1414[1][3]
Ended
22 April 1418[1][3]
Place
Konstanz, Holy Roman Empire (modern Germany)

Background

  • Since 1378 rival lines of popes in Rome and Avignon had divided Western Christendom; the Council of Pisa (1409) elected a third pope without removing the other two.[3][5]
  • King Sigismund of the Romans pressed the Pisan pope John XXIII to call a council, and promised Jan Hus safe conduct to attend it.[3][4]

What happened

  • John XXIII fled the city in March 1415 hoping to dissolve the council; it continued without him, and on 6 April 1415 issued the decree Haec Sancta, claiming authority directly from Christ that even a pope must obey in matters of faith, the schism, and reform.[1][3]
  • The council deposed John XXIII (29 May 1415), accepted the resignation of the Roman pope Gregory XII (4 July 1415), and deposed the Avignon pope Benedict XIII (26 July 1417).[1][3]
  • On 4 May 1415 it condemned 45 articles of John Wycliffe and ordered his remains exhumed and burned.[1][4]
  • It tried Jan Hus, condemned him as a heretic, and handed him to the secular power; he was burned on 6 July 1415.[2][4][1]
  • It forbade giving the cup to the laity in communion (15 June 1415), which the Hussites had begun to do.[1]
  • On 9 October 1417 it issued Frequens, requiring councils to meet regularly, and on 11 November 1417 the cardinals and representatives of the nations elected Martin V, ending the schism.[1][3]

Key participants

  • Sigismund King of the Romans and of Hungary; protector of the council
  • John XXIII (Baldassare Cossa) Pisan pope who convened the council; deposed
  • Gregory XII Roman pope; resigned
  • Benedict XIII (Pedro de Luna) Avignon pope; deposed but never submitted
  • Martin V (Oddone Colonna) Elected pope in 1417
  • Jean Gerson and Pierre d'Ailly Leading theologians of the council; both took part in the proceedings against Hus
  • Jan Hus Bohemian reformer tried and burned by the council
  • John Wycliffe Dead since 1384; his teaching was condemned and his remains ordered burned
  • Jerome of Prague Hus's associate; recanted, withdrew the recantation, and was burned

Outcomes and significance

  • The Western Schism ended, and Martin V was accepted across Western Christendom.[3][5]
  • Haec Sancta became the founding text of conciliarism, the view that a general council is above the pope; later popes rejected that reading, and the Fifth Lateran Council and Vatican I affirmed papal authority.[3][5]
  • The burning of Hus, despite Sigismund's safe conduct, led Bohemian nobles to protest and helped start the Hussite revolution and wars (1419–1434).[4][5]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The council's condemnations were carried out by the secular authorities under Sigismund, the usual practice for heresy executions.

28 November 1414 – 6 July 1415

Arrest, trial, and burning of Jan Hus

Hus came to Constance under Sigismund's safe conduct. He was arrested on 28 November 1414 on the cardinals' orders, held in the Dominican friary and then Gottlieben castle, tried in public hearings in June 1415, and burned outside the city after refusing to recant. Sigismund accepted the council's argument that a safe conduct did not protect a heretic.[2][4]

April 1415 – 30 May 1416

Arrest and burning of Jerome of Prague

Jerome came to Constance to support Hus, fled, was arrested at Hirsau and brought back in chains. Imprisoned for a year, he recanted in September 1415, withdrew the recantation in May 1416, and was burned as a relapsed heretic.[4][5]
