Elizabeth I

1533–1603 · Reformation

Also known as Elizabeth Tudor; the Virgin Queen; Good Queen Bess; Gloriana

Queen of England and Ireland (1558–1603), Supreme Governor of the Church of England

Daughter of Henry VIII and Anne Boleyn who succeeded her Catholic half-sister Mary I in 1558, restored a Protestant Church of England with herself as Supreme Governor, and ruled until 1603, when the crown passed to James VI of Scotland. Excommunicated by Pope Pius V in 1570, her government fined Catholic recusants and executed about 180 Catholics, most of them priests, as traitors, and also punished Puritans and separatists who wanted further reform.

What they did

  • Reversed Mary I's Catholic restoration in the Elizabethan Settlement (1559). The Act of Supremacy again cut the church off from Rome, making her "Supreme Governor" rather than "Supreme Head"; the Act of Uniformity made a revised Book of Common Prayer (based on the 1552 book of her brother Edward VI) the only legal form of worship.[2][8][12]
  • Under Elizabeth the swing went back to Protestantism: the Latin Mass was replaced by the English Book of Common Prayer, altars and images were removed again, and all but one of Mary's bishops were deprived for refusing the oath of supremacy; she kept bishops and some traditional ceremonies, which Puritans opposed.[6][9]
  • For ordinary worship, the swing back meant the end of the Latin Mass, which became illegal; services in English from the Prayer Book; the removal of roods and images in the royal visitation of 1559; and communion tables in place of stone altars. The monasteries Mary had refounded, such as Westminster Abbey, were dissolved again.[9][8][6]
  • Punished those who refused the 1559 settlement. All but one of Mary's surviving bishops refused the Oath of Supremacy and were deprived, and several, including Edmund Bonner, died in custody; Catholics who stayed away from church (recusants) were fined, at first 12 pence a Sunday, raised to £20 a month in 1581.[2][6][8]
  • Defined the Church of England's doctrine in the Thirty-nine Articles (1563, made binding on clergy by Parliament in 1571), a revision of Cranmer's Forty-two Articles that was Protestant in doctrine while keeping bishops and much traditional ceremony.[3][8]
  • Survived Catholic risings and plots that aimed to replace her with her Catholic cousin Mary, Queen of Scots: the Northern Rising (1569), after which several hundred rebels were executed, and the Ridolfi (1571), Throckmorton (1583), and Babington (1586) plots. She approved the execution of Mary, Queen of Scots, in 1587.[10][6][12]
  • Treated Catholic priests trained abroad as traitors. Laws of 1571, 1581, and 1585 made it treason to bring in papal bulls, to reconcile anyone to Rome, or for a Jesuit or seminary priest to be in England. About 180 Catholics were executed in her reign, roughly 120 of them priests, many after torture authorized by the Privy Council.[2][6][8]
  • Resisted Puritans who wanted further reform. She suspended Archbishop Edmund Grindal (1577) for refusing to suppress the clergy's preaching meetings ("prophesyings"), backed Archbishop John Whitgift's enforcement of conformity through the Court of High Commission from 1583, and after the Marprelate tracts (1588–1589) saw separatist leaders hanged in 1593.[7][6]
  • Signed the writ for the burning of two Dutch Anabaptists at Smithfield in 1575, despite a plea for mercy from John Foxe. Arians were also burned at Norwich and elsewhere in her reign.[4][8]
  • Led England through the Spanish Armada crisis of 1588. Pope Sixtus V supported the invasion. Its defeat strengthened the identification of English national feeling with Protestantism.[6][12]
  • Never married and had no children. On her death the Tudor line ended, and the crown passed to the Protestant James VI of Scotland (James I of England), son of Mary, Queen of Scots.[11][12]

    Not her own words. Bacon, describing and defending her religious policy (in a paper often printed as a letter of Sir Francis Walsingham), wrote that the queen did not like "to make windows into men's hearts and secret thoughts." The "souls" wording is a later paraphrase. The policy described was outward conformity, enforced by fines and, for priests, by treason law.

    Not found in any contemporary account of her death; a later legend.

Notable people executed on Elizabeth I's authority

About 180 Catholics were executed in Elizabeth's reign, roughly 120 of them priests, almost all under treason laws rather than for heresy; the government's position was that they died for treason, not religion, which Catholics denied. Several hundred rebels were executed after the Northern Rising of 1569. A few Protestant radicals (Anabaptists and anti-Trinitarians) were burned for heresy, and separatists were hanged for sedition. This is a selection. Elizabeth's personal part varied: some sentences she signed or allowed against advice to pardon, while most were carried out by courts and the Privy Council under her statutes. Persecution, arrests, and executions

Elizabeth was declared illegitimate after her mother's execution, questioned as a teenager over Thomas Seymour's schemes, and imprisoned in the Tower and then under guard at Woodstock by her half-sister Mary I, who suspected her of involvement in Wyatt's rebellion. As queen she was excommunicated and declared deposed by the pope, and was the target of several Catholic assassination plots and a papally backed invasion.

Excommunications

Elizabeth was excommunicated by Pope Pius V in 1570. The sentence was never lifted. In 1580 Pope Gregory XIII allowed that English Catholics could obey her in civil matters for the time being without sin, which eased but did not revoke the bull.

May–July 1536

Declared illegitimate after her mother's execution

When Anne Boleyn was executed, Cranmer declared Henry's marriage to her invalid, and the Second Act of Succession (1536) made Elizabeth, aged two, illegitimate and removed her from the succession.[6][12]

January–March 1549

Questioned over Thomas Seymour's schemes

After Thomas Seymour, who had flirted with her in his household, was arrested for plotting against his brother the Lord Protector, Elizabeth, then fifteen, was questioned at Hatfield by Sir Robert Tyrwhitt, and her governess Kat Ashley and her cofferer Thomas Parry were sent to the Tower. She denied any plan to marry Seymour without the council's consent.[6][12]

March 1554 – April 1555

Imprisoned in the Tower and held at Woodstock under Mary I

After Sir Thomas Wyatt's Protestant rebellion against Mary's Spanish marriage, Elizabeth was suspected of involvement, brought to London, questioned by the council led by Bishop Stephen Gardiner, and sent to the Tower on 18 March 1554. Some councillors urged her execution. In May she was moved under guard to Woodstock, Oxfordshire, where she was held by Sir Henry Bedingfield, and she attended Mass under pressure.[4][6][12]

1570–1588

Papal deposition, assassination plots, and the Armada

Pope Pius V declared her deposed in 1570 and released her subjects from obedience. Plots followed to kill or depose her and crown Mary, Queen of Scots: the Ridolfi Plot (1571), with the knowledge of Pius V and Philip II; the Throckmorton Plot (1583), involving the Spanish ambassador Bernardino de Mendoza; and the Babington Plot (1586). In 1588 Philip II sent the Armada, with the backing of Pope Sixtus V.[10][6][12]
