Augustine of Hippo

354–430 · Early Church

Also known as Saint Augustine; Aurelius Augustinus

Bishop of Hippo Regius, theologian

North African bishop whose writings on grace, sin, the Church, and the two cities shaped Western theology more than any other author after the apostle Paul.

Born
13 November 354 · Thagaste, Numidia (modern Souk Ahras, Algeria)[3][4]
Died
28 August 430 · Hippo Regius (modern Annaba, Algeria), during the Vandal siege[2][3]
Fever (illness), during the Vandal siege of Hippo

What they did

  • Wrote the Confessions, an autobiographical prayer to God often called the first Western autobiography, tracing his restless search ending in conversion.[1][3]
  • Wrote The City of God (413–426) in response to pagan claims that Christianity caused the sack of Rome by the Goths in 410, contrasting the earthly city with the city of God.[3][4]
  • Served about 35 years as bishop of Hippo, preaching, judging disputes, and living in community with his clergy under a common rule.[2][3]
  • Opposed the Manichaeans, whose sect he had followed for about nine years before his conversion, writing several works against their dualism.[1][3]
  • Led the Catholic side against the Donatist schism in North Africa, including the conference of Carthage in 411.[3][4]
  • Defended the priority of God's grace against Pelagius and his followers, whose teaching was condemned by African councils (Carthage, 418).[3][4]
  • Wrote On the Trinity, a major work of Latin theology on the doctrine of God.[4]
  • Near the end of his life wrote the Retractations (c. 426–427), reviewing and correcting his own earlier works.[3][2]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Augustine at first opposed forcing the Donatists back into the Catholic church, then from about 405 defended imperial coercion of them, citing Luke 14:23 ("compel them to come in") in Letters 93 and 185, a defense later invoked to justify persecuting heretics. He also helped secure the condemnation of the Pelagians. He repeatedly pleaded with officials not to execute or torture Donatists, even those convicted of killing Catholic clergy, and argued that Jews should not be killed but allowed to live as witnesses (City of God 18.46).

c. 405–420

Defense of coercion against the Donatists

Called for it

Against: Donatist clergy and laity in North Africa

Having earlier held that no one should be forced into unity, Augustine came to support the Emperor Honorius's Edict of Unity (405) and later laws imposing fines, confiscation of churches, and exile on Donatists. In Letter 93 (c. 408) and Letter 185, On the Correction of the Donatists (c. 417), he argued that the church could rightly use imperial force to compel heretics and schismatics to return, citing "compel them to come in." The laws were issued by the emperor and enforced by imperial officials at the request of the African Catholic bishops; Augustine asked that punishments stop short of death and torture.[5][3][4]

Outcome: Many Donatist congregations were absorbed into the Catholic church; Augustine's arguments were cited by later defenders of persecuting heretics.

411

Conference of Carthage and the edict of 412

Took part

Against: Donatist bishops and congregations

Augustine was a leading Catholic speaker at the conference of 411 called by Honorius and chaired by the imperial tribune Marcellinus, who ruled against the Donatists. An imperial edict of 412 then outlawed the Donatist church, with fines, confiscations, and exile for its clergy; Augustine wrote to Marcellinus asking that Donatists convicted of violence not be executed.[5][2][3]

416–418

Condemnation of the Pelagians

Took part

Against: Pelagius, Caelestius, and later Julian of Eclanum and other Pelagian bishops

With other African bishops, Augustine pressed Pope Innocent I through the councils of Carthage and Milevis (416), and then Pope Zosimus, to condemn Pelagius and Caelestius. In 418 a council at Carthage condemned Pelagianism, Honorius issued a rescript expelling Pelagians from Rome, and Zosimus's Tractoria followed; Julian of Eclanum and about eighteen Italian bishops who refused to sign were deposed and exiled. How far the African bishops lobbied the imperial court directly is debated.[5][3][4]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Augustine was never arrested, tried, or executed. The one recorded attempt on his life came from Donatist militants during the Donatist controversy. He died of a fever during the Vandal siege of Hippo, not by violence.

Excommunications

Augustine was never excommunicated. He remained a bishop in full communion with the Catholic Church in North Africa and with Rome until his death. (The Donatist and Catholic churches each rejected the other's clergy, but no sentence against Augustine personally is recorded.)

Before 411 (exact year not recorded)

Ambush by Donatist militants

Possidius records that armed bands of circumcellions, militant supporters of the Donatist church, lay in wait on a road to attack Augustine while he was traveling to visit his churches. Possidius blames the Donatist party for this and other attacks on Catholic clergy in this period. He names no individual for this ambush.[2][3]

430

Siege of Hippo by the Vandals (context)

Included for completeness; this was not persecution of Augustine himself. In 430 the Vandals besieged Hippo. Augustine fell ill with a fever during the third month of the siege and died. The Vandals were Arian Christians, and under Gaiseric they later persecuted Nicene (Catholic) clergy in North Africa.[2][3]
