John Wycliffe

c. 1330–1384 · Medieval

Also known as John Wyclif

Oxford theologian and philosopher, rector of Lutterworth, reformer

Oxford theologian who argued that Scripture is the supreme authority for Christians, attacked the wealth and power of the clergy and papacy, and inspired the first complete English Bible and the Lollard movement.

Born
c. 1330 (some scholars suggest the mid-1320s) · Yorkshire, England, probably near Richmond[5][8]
Died
31 December 1384, after a stroke suffered while hearing Mass · Lutterworth, Leicestershire, England[6][8]

What they did

  • Was the leading Oxford philosopher and theologian of his generation: Master of Balliol College and a doctor of theology from 1372.[5][8]
  • Argued in On the Truth of Holy Scripture (1378) that the Bible is the highest authority for Christian belief, above church tradition and papal decrees.[5][7]
  • Argued in On Civil Dominion (c. 1375–1376) that clergy who lived in sin forfeited their right to property, and that secular rulers could take church wealth. This won him the support of John of Gaunt and other nobles.[5][6]
  • Challenged papal power. After the Great Schism of 1378 he argued that a pope who did not follow Christ was not his true representative and could be Antichrist.[5][8]
  • Rejected the doctrine of transubstantiation in On the Eucharist (c. 1380). This lost him many supporters and led to his condemnation at Oxford.[5][1]
  • Inspired the first complete translation of the Bible into English, made from the Latin Vulgate by his associates (c. 1382, revised c. 1388–1395). How much he translated himself is debated.[7][3]
  • His ideas were spread by itinerant preachers and gave rise to the Lollard movement in England.[7][6]
  • Influenced Jan Hus and the Bohemian reform. Czech students carried his writings from Oxford to Prague after Richard II married Anne of Bohemia in 1382.[5][8]
  • Later Protestant writers called him the "Morning Star of the Reformation."[8]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Wycliffe was condemned by a pope, tried before English bishops, and had his teachings condemned at Oxford and at the Blackfriars Council. Powerful protectors kept him from being imprisoned, and he died a natural death at Lutterworth. His followers, the Lollards, were later persecuted and burned under English law. In 1415 the Council of Constance declared him a heretic, and in 1428 his remains were dug up and burned.

Excommunications

Wycliffe was not excommunicated during his lifetime. Powerful protectors prevented the papal bulls of 1377 from being enforced, and the Blackfriars Council of 1382 condemned his teachings without naming him. After his death, the Council of Constance declared in 1415 that he had died an obstinate heretic, and condemned his memory.

February 1377

Summoned before the bishops at St Paul's, London

Wycliffe was called to answer for his preaching before the bishops at St Paul's Cathedral. He came with powerful protectors, and the hearing broke up in a quarrel between the Bishop of London and John of Gaunt before any judgment was given.[2][6]

May 1377

Condemned by Pope Gregory XI

Pope Gregory XI issued five bulls condemning 18 propositions from Wycliffe's writings, mostly on church property and authority. He ordered the Archbishop of Canterbury and the Bishop of London to investigate and, if the charges were proved, to imprison him. Separate bulls went to King Edward III and to the University of Oxford.[6][5][8]

1378

Hearing at Lambeth Palace

Acting on the papal bulls, the bishops examined Wycliffe at Lambeth Palace. Before they could pass sentence, a messenger from Joan of Kent, the young King Richard II's mother, forbade them to condemn him, and a London crowd broke into the hearing.[2][6]

1381

Teaching on the Eucharist condemned at Oxford

The Chancellor of Oxford and a council of doctors condemned Wycliffe's denial of transubstantiation and forbade anyone at the university to teach it, on pain of imprisonment and excommunication.[1][5]

May–July 1382

The Blackfriars Council and the purge of his Oxford followers

Archbishop Courtenay held a council at the Blackfriars in London, remembered as the "Earthquake Synod" because an earthquake struck while it met. It condemned 24 propositions drawn from Wycliffe's teaching, 10 as heretical and 14 as erroneous. With royal backing, the archbishop then purged Wycliffe's supporters at Oxford. Nicholas Hereford, Philip Repingdon, and John Aston were suspended and either recanted or fled. Wycliffe himself was not named or summoned.[1][7][6]

1401–1417 (after his death)

Lollards burned under English law; English Bible restricted

Wycliffe's followers, the Lollards, were hunted after his death. In 1401 Parliament passed the statute De heretico comburendo, which allowed convicted heretics to be burned, and William Sawtrey became the first Lollard executed. Archbishop Arundel's Constitutions of Oxford (1407–1409) banned new English translations of the Bible, and the reading of unapproved ones, without a bishop's permission. Sir John Oldcastle, a Lollard leader, was executed in 1417.[7][6]

1415 and 1428 (after his death)

Declared a heretic at Constance; remains dug up and burned

On 4 May 1415 the Council of Constance condemned Wycliffe as a heretic, condemned articles drawn from his writings, ordered his books burned, and ordered his remains removed from consecrated ground. The order was carried out in 1428 at the command of Pope Martin V. Richard Fleming, Bishop of Lincoln, had his bones dug up at Lutterworth and burned, and the ashes were thrown into the River Swift.[4][5][8]
