Council of Trent

1545–1563 · Reformation

Also known as Nineteenth ecumenical council (Catholic count)

The Catholic Church's council in response to the Protestant Reformation, which defined Catholic teaching against Protestant doctrines and launched wide-ranging reforms.

Began
13 December 1545[1][4]
Ended
4 December 1563[1][4]
Place
Trento, Prince-Bishopric of Trent (modern Italy); briefly Bologna (1547–1549)

Background

  • Luther and other reformers had called for a free general council since 1518; years of conflict between the papacy, Charles V, and France delayed it.[4][5]

What happened

  • The council met in three periods: 1545–1547 (moved to Bologna in 1547) under Paul III, 1551–1552 under Julius III, and 1562–1563 under Pius IV; attendance was small at first, rising to over 200 bishops at the end, most of them Italian.[4][5]
  • In April 1546 it declared Scripture and unwritten tradition both to be received with equal reverence, fixed the canon including the deuterocanonical books, and made the Latin Vulgate the authoritative text.[1][4]
  • In January 1547 it issued its decree on justification, rejecting justification by faith alone with 33 canons pronouncing anathema on those who taught it.[1][4]
  • It affirmed seven sacraments, transubstantiation (1551), and the Mass as a true sacrifice (1562), each with anathemas on opposing views.[1]
  • Protestant delegates from Württemberg, Strasbourg, and Saxony came to the second period in 1551–1552 under safe conduct, but no real debate took place.[4][5]
  • In 1563 it required bishops to live in their dioceses, ordered a seminary in each diocese to train priests, and regulated marriage (the decree Tametsi).[1][4]

Key participants

  • Paul III, Julius III, Pius IV Popes who convened and resumed it; none attended in person
  • Giovanni Morone Papal legate who steered the final period to a close
  • Reginald Pole One of the first papal legates
  • Charles Borromeo Pius IV's nephew and secretary of state; later a leading enforcer of the decrees
  • Martin Luther Not present; died in 1546, two months after the council opened, and its decrees on justification and Scripture answered his teaching
  • Martin Chemnitz Not present; wrote the four-volume Lutheran reply, Examination of the Council of Trent (1565–1573)
  • John Calvin Not present; wrote a Protestant reply, Acts of the Council of Trent with the Antidote (1547)

Outcomes and significance

  • Its decrees defined Catholic teaching for four centuries, until the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), which did not revoke them.[4][6]
  • It led to the Roman Catechism (1566), a revised Missal (1570), and a revised Vulgate (1592), giving the Catholic Church a uniform liturgy.[4][6]
  • It confirmed the division between Catholics and Protestants; Protestants rejected it as neither free nor general.[2][4]

Condemnations and excommunications

Condemned at or by this event

Trent condemned doctrines, not named people. Its canons pronounce 'let him be anathema' on anyone holding the listed Protestant positions. In 1999 the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification between the Catholic Church and the Lutheran World Federation stated that the condemnations of the 16th century on justification do not apply to the teaching of the Lutheran churches as set out in that declaration.

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The council itself did not try or punish anyone. The Roman Inquisition and the Index of Prohibited Books, which enforced Catholic teaching in this period, were separate institutions run by the papacy.
