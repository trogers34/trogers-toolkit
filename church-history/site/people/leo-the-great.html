Leo the Great

c. 400–461 · Early Church

Also known as Pope Leo I; Saint Leo the Great

Bishop of Rome (pope), 440–461

Pope whose letter on the two natures of Christ, the Tome, became the basis of the Council of Chalcedon's definition, who strongly asserted the authority of the bishop of Rome, and who met Attila the Hun to turn him away from Italy.

Born
c. 400 · Tuscany or Rome, Italy[2][4]
Died

What they did

  • Wrote the Tome of Leo (449), explaining that Christ is one person in two natures, fully divine and fully human. The Council of Chalcedon (451) accepted it, with the bishops reportedly declaring that "Peter has spoken through Leo."[2][3][4]
  • Developed the idea that the bishop of Rome holds authority over the whole church as the successor of the apostle Peter.[3][4]
  • Led an embassy that met Attila the Hun in 452, after which Attila withdrew from Italy.[2][4]
  • When the Vandals took Rome in 455, persuaded King Gaiseric to refrain from burning the city and from massacre, though the city was plundered for two weeks.[2][4]
  • Left nearly 100 sermons and about 140 letters, among the most important sources for the church of his time.[1][2]
  • Was declared a Doctor of the Church in 1754, one of only two popes (with Gregory I) called "the Great."[4]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Leo conducted an inquiry into the Manichaeans of Rome in 443–444, urged the faithful to denounce them, and pressed other bishops to hunt down those who fled; an imperial law of 445 then punished them. In 447 he wrote approvingly of the execution of Priscillian in 385 and urged action against the Priscillianists of Spain. No record shows him calling for violence against Jews.

443–445

Inquiry against the Manichaeans of Rome

Carried it out

Against: Manichaeans in Rome

Leo held an investigation with bishops, clergy, and senators present at which Manichaean leaders were questioned and confessed; in his sermons he urged Romans to report Manichaeans to their priests. In Letter 7 (444) he asked the bishops of Italy to seek out Manichaeans who had fled Rome. Valentinian III's law of 445 (Novel 18), which cites Leo's inquiry, barred them from the army and civic life and ordered them expelled.[5][1][2]

Outcome: Manichaeans who would not recant were exiled from Rome; some recanted and did penance.

447

Approval of Priscillian's execution and action against Priscillianists

Approved or defended it

Against: Priscillianists in Spain (and, after the fact, Priscillian, executed in 385)

Replying to Bishop Turribius of Astorga (Letter 15), Leo said the secular rulers had rightly punished Priscillian and his followers with death, describing that severity as a help to the church's leniency, and urged a Spanish synod to condemn the sect. The execution itself was ordered by the usurper Maximus at Trier in 385, decades before Leo.[5][2]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Leo was never arrested or exiled. His principal clash was with Dioscorus, Patriarch of Alexandria: at the "Robber Synod" of Ephesus (449) Leo's Tome was suppressed and his envoys mistreated, and in 451 Dioscorus pronounced an excommunication against Leo himself. Both actions were reversed at the Council of Chalcedon.

Excommunications

Leo was "excommunicated" once, by a rival patriarch. In 451 Dioscorus of Alexandria and a small group of bishops pronounced a sentence of excommunication against him. No other church recognized it, and the Council of Chalcedon later that year condemned Dioscorus.

August 449

The "Robber Synod" of Ephesus

At the Second Council of Ephesus, presided over by Dioscorus of Alexandria with the backing of Emperor Theodosius II, Leo's Tome was not allowed to be read, his legates were ignored and intimidated, and Flavian of Constantinople, who had appealed to Leo, was deposed and so badly treated that he died soon after. Leo's deacon Hilarus escaped with difficulty. Leo condemned the meeting as a "robber synod" (latrocinium).[2][3][4]
