Saint George

c. 280–c. 303 · Early Church

Also known as George of Lydda; George the Great Martyr; the Trophy-bearer

Soldier-martyr venerated in East and West; patron saint of England, Georgia, and many other places

A Christian martyr, probably killed in the persecution under Diocletian, about whom almost nothing is historically certain. His veneration is ancient and his tomb at Lydda was a pilgrimage site by the 6th century, but the stories of his life grew into fantastic legends, including, from about the 11th century, his rescue of a princess from a dragon. He became the warrior saint of Byzantium, the Crusaders, and medieval England.

What they did

  • Almost nothing about his life can be confirmed: scholars generally accept that a martyr named George died, probably at Lydda or Nicomedia in the Diocletianic persecution, and that his cult was early, but the details of his life are legend and are labelled as such here.[4][6]
  • His veneration is old: a church over his tomb at Lydda (Diospolis) was visited by pilgrims by the 6th century, and churches were dedicated to him across the eastern Mediterranean.[4][5]
  • The earliest accounts of his martyrdom (the Greek and Latin 'Passions') were so full of marvels, including repeated deaths and resurrections, that a church list attributed to Pope Gelasius (late 5th or early 6th century) placed his Acts among writings not to be read in church, while still honouring him as a saint 'whose deeds are known only to God'.[2][4]
  • The dragon story is late: it first appears around the 11th century, apparently in Georgia or the Byzantine world, and became famous in the West through Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend (c. 1260), where George kills a dragon at 'Silene' in Libya and the town converts.[3][4]
  • Became the model warrior saint: Byzantine armies honoured him as a 'military saint', and the chronicles of the First Crusade report that George, with Saints Demetrius and Mercurius, was seen fighting for the Crusaders at Antioch (1098).[4][5]
  • Became patron saint of England in the later Middle Ages: Edward III made him patron of the Order of the Garter (founded c. 1348), and his feast was raised in importance after Agincourt (1415); his red cross became England's flag.[5][6]
  • Is patron of Georgia, Ethiopia, Catalonia, Aragon, Portugal, Moscow, Beirut, and many other places, and is honoured by Christians of every ancient tradition and by some Muslims, who identify him with the figure al-Khidr.[4][6]
  • In the Catholic Church's 1969 calendar reform his feast was reduced to an optional memorial in the general calendar because so little is known of him; he was not removed from the list of saints, and his feast keeps a higher rank in England and elsewhere.[6][5]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of George supporting or carrying out persecution of anyone. In later centuries his name and image were used as a war banner, including by the Crusaders and in English and other wars, but those uses have nothing to do with the historical man.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Everything about George's death rests on legend. The persecution in which he is said to have died is historical; the details of his trial and tortures are not.

Excommunications

No record of any excommunication or condemnation of George. The only church censure connected with him was of the legendary Acts of his martyrdom, which the Gelasian list barred from reading in church.

c. 303 (traditional)

Martyrdom in the Diocletianic persecution (tradition)

According to the legends, George, a soldier, declared himself a Christian when the edicts against Christians were published, refused to sacrifice to the gods, and after many tortures was beheaded. Some versions name the emperor Diocletian; others name a ruler called 'Dacian', who is not a known historical figure. The persecution itself is historical: Diocletian and his Caesar Galerius issued edicts from February 303 ordering churches destroyed, Scriptures burned, and Christians compelled to sacrifice.[1][4][3]
