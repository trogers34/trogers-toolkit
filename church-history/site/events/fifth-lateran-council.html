Fifth Lateran Council

1512–1517 · Reformation

Also known as Lateran V; eighteenth ecumenical council (Catholic count)

The last council before the Reformation, called by Julius II against a rival council at Pisa, which defined the individual immortality of the soul and issued reform decrees that were largely not carried out.

Began
10 May 1512[1][2]
Ended
16 March 1517[1][2]
Place
Lateran Basilica, Rome

Background

  • In 1511 a group of cardinals backed by King Louis XII of France and Emperor Maximilian I called a council at Pisa to reform the church and pressure Pope Julius II; Julius answered by calling his own council in Rome.[2][4]

What happened

  • In his opening sermon, Giles of Viterbo, head of the Augustinian order, declared that 'men must be changed by religion, not religion by men'.[2]
  • The decree Apostolici regiminis (1513) condemned the view that the intellectual soul is mortal or one for all humans, and told philosophers to refute such arguments in their teaching.[1]
  • The council required that books be approved by the bishop or papal officials before printing (1515), approved the montes pietatis (charitable pawnshops), and issued reform decrees on preaching, the curia, and cardinals.[1][2]
  • Under Leo X it approved the Concordat of Bologna (1516), which gave the king of France the right to nominate bishops and abbots, and abolished the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges.[2][4]

Key participants

  • Julius II Pope who called the council; died in 1513
  • Leo X Pope who continued and closed it
  • Giles of Viterbo Augustinian prior general who preached the opening sermon
  • Martin Luther Not present; posted his Ninety-five Theses seven months after it closed

Outcomes and significance

  • Its reforms were mostly not carried out, and it closed seven months before Luther's Ninety-five Theses; historians often cite it as a missed chance for reform.[2][4]
  • Its decree on the soul led to controversy over Pietro Pomponazzi's On the Immortality of the Soul (1516), which argued that reason alone could not prove immortality.[3][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

No one was executed as a result of the council. Its censorship decree and the reaction to Pomponazzi are the main coercive measures connected to it.

1516–1518

Burning of Pomponazzi's book in Venice

After Pietro Pomponazzi published On the Immortality of the Soul (1516), arguing that immortality could be known only by faith, the book was publicly burned in Venice and he was attacked by churchmen. He was protected by Cardinal Pietro Bembo and was never formally condemned.[3]
