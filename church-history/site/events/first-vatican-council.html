First Vatican Council

1869–1870 · Modern

Also known as Vatican I; twentieth ecumenical council (Catholic count)

Pope Pius IX's council, which defined papal infallibility and the pope's universal jurisdiction, and was cut short when Italian troops took Rome.

Began
8 December 1869[1][3]
Ended
Suspended 20 October 1870[1][3]
Place
St Peter's Basilica, Vatican

Background

  • Pius IX had lost most of the Papal States to the new Kingdom of Italy and had condemned modern errors in the Syllabus of Errors (1864). The ultramontane movement wanted papal authority strengthened.[3][4]

What happened

  • About 700 bishops attended. The constitution Dei Filius (24 April 1870) taught that God can be known with certainty by natural reason and that faith and reason cannot contradict each other.[1][3]
  • The constitution Pastor Aeternus (18 July 1870) defined that the pope has full and immediate jurisdiction over the whole church, and that when he speaks ex cathedra to define a doctrine of faith or morals he is infallible, and such definitions are irreformable 'of themselves, and not from the consent of the church'.[1]
  • About 60 bishops of the minority, who thought the definition unwise or untrue, left Rome the day before the final vote rather than vote against it before the pope. The vote was 533 to 2; the two bishops who voted no submitted immediately.[3][2]
  • The Franco-Prussian War broke out the next day; French troops left Rome, Italian forces captured it on 20 September 1870, and Pius IX suspended the council indefinitely on 20 October.[3][4]

Key participants

  • Pius IX Pope who called the council
  • Henry Edward Manning Archbishop of Westminster; leader of the infallibilist majority
  • Félix Dupanloup Bishop of Orléans; leader of the minority
  • John Henry Newman Not present; thought the definition inopportune but accepted it, and defended it against Gladstone in 1875
  • Ignaz von Döllinger Munich church historian who rejected the definition

Outcomes and significance

  • Papal infallibility has been invoked explicitly once since, for the Assumption of Mary (1950); Catholic theologians also count the 1854 Immaculate Conception.[3][4]
  • Groups in Germany, Switzerland, and Austria who rejected the decrees formed the Old Catholic churches, joining the Union of Utrecht in 1889.[3][4]
  • Protestant and Orthodox churches rejected the decrees, and they became a major issue in later ecumenical dialogue; the council's work on bishops was left unfinished until Vatican II.[3][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Opponents of the decrees were disciplined by the church, and in Germany the state turned on the Catholic Church partly in reaction to the council.

1871–1878

The Kulturkampf in Germany

Otto von Bismarck's government, citing the danger of papal power after the council, passed laws expelling the Jesuits (1872) and, in the May Laws of 1873, putting the training and appointment of clergy under state control. Bishops and priests who refused were fined, imprisoned, or exiled; Archbishop Mieczysław Ledóchowski of Gniezno-Poznań was imprisoned in 1874.[3][4]
