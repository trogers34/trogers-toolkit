Jerome

c. 347–c. 420 · Early Church

Also known as Eusebius Sophronius Hieronymus; Saint Jerome

Biblical scholar, translator of the Vulgate, ascetic, and controversialist; Doctor of the Church

Latin scholar and ascetic who translated most of the Bible into the Latin version later called the Vulgate, wrote extensive commentaries and letters, and was known for bitter polemics against opponents including his former friend Rufinus.

What they did

  • Translated most of the Bible into Latin, revising the Gospels from Greek (383–384) and translating the Hebrew Old Testament (c. 390–405); with other texts, his work formed the core of the Vulgate, the standard Bible of the Western church for over a thousand years.[4][6]
  • Insisted on translating from the Hebrew original ('Hebrew truth') rather than the Greek Septuagint, and studied with Jewish teachers to do so; critics including Augustine and Rufinus objected to the departure from the Septuagint.[4][5]
  • Distinguished the books of the Hebrew Bible from those found only in the Greek Old Testament, calling the latter apocrypha; Protestant reformers later appealed to his view.[4][6]
  • Wrote commentaries on most books of the Old Testament prophets and several of Paul's letters, as well as Matthew.[4][6]
  • Wrote On Illustrious Men (393), a catalog of 135 Christian writers, and translated and continued Eusebius's Chronicle; he also translated many of Origen's homilies.[2][4]
  • Promoted ascetic life through letters, lives of the hermits Paul, Malchus, and Hilarion, and spiritual direction of Roman women such as Paula, Eustochium, and Marcella; with Paula he founded monasteries at Bethlehem.[1][4]
  • Defended Mary's perpetual virginity against Helvidius (c. 383) and the superiority of virginity over marriage against Jovinian (393); his disparagement of marriage in Against Jovinian was so extreme that his friend Pammachius tried to withdraw copies in Rome.[2][4]
  • Wrote some of the harshest polemics of the early church: he turned on his former friend Rufinus over Origen, mocked him even after his death in 411, and abused opponents such as Vigilantius (406) in personal terms.[2][4]
  • Exchanged tense letters with Augustine (c. 394–405) over Paul's rebuke of Peter in Galatians 2 and over his new translation from the Hebrew; the two were later reconciled and united against Pelagius.[1][4]
  • Was blamed by many in Rome for the death in 384 of Paula's daughter Blesilla after severe fasting under his guidance; the outcry contributed to his leaving the city.[4]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Jerome took no part in physical persecution but wrote fierce polemics against Jovinian, Vigilantius, the Origenists, and the Pelagians, defending their church condemnations and in one case urging a bishop to punish an opponent. He supported Theophilus of Alexandria's campaign against Origenism, which included expelling Origenist monks from Egypt.

393

Against Jovinian

Approved or defended it

Against: Jovinian, a Roman monk who denied that celibacy was holier than marriage

After Pope Siricius at Rome and Ambrose at Milan condemned Jovinian (c. 390–393), Jerome wrote Against Jovinian (393) defending the condemnation. Jovinian was later banished by imperial order, and Jerome mocked him after his death.[2][4]

c. 399–402

Support for the condemnation of the Origenists

Approved or defended it

Against: Origenist monks in Egypt and Palestine, and Jerome's former friend Rufinus

Jerome backed Theophilus of Alexandria's synodal condemnation of Origenism (400) and translated Theophilus's letters against Origen into Latin. Theophilus drove Origenist monks, including the Tall Brothers, out of Nitria with armed force; Jerome's part was polemical support, not involvement in the expulsions.[2][4][5]

404–406

Against Vigilantius

Called for it

Against: Vigilantius, a Gallic priest who criticized the cult of relics and vigils

In his letter to Riparius (Letter 109) Jerome wondered that Vigilantius's bishop did not break this useless vessel with an apostolic rod and deliver him to the destruction of the flesh, calling for church discipline; he followed it with the treatise Against Vigilantius (406). No action against Vigilantius is recorded.[2][4]

415

Writings against the Pelagians

Called for it

Against: Pelagius and his followers

Jerome attacked Pelagius in his Letter to Ctesiphon and his Dialogue against the Pelagians (415) and supported Augustine and Orosius in seeking Pelagius's condemnation, which Palestinian synods at Jerusalem and Diospolis declined in 415. Pelagius was later condemned by Pope Innocent I (417) and Pope Zosimus and by imperial edict (418).[2][4][5]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Jerome was never arrested or tried by the state. He was forced out of Rome in 385 under suspicion from the Roman clergy, was placed under a church ban by Bishop John of Jerusalem during the Origenist controversy (c. 394–397), and in 416 his monasteries at Bethlehem were attacked and burned by a band whose members have never been identified. His bitter disputes with Rufinus, Augustine, and others were literary quarrels rather than persecution.

Excommunications

Jerome was never excommunicated by a pope or council, and he is venerated as a saint and Doctor of the Church. The only church sanction recorded against him is John of Jerusalem's ban on his monastery (c. 394–397), listed below.

August 385

Forced out of Rome

After Pope Damasus died in December 384, Jerome lost his patron. Rumors about his relationship with the widow Paula and anger over Blesilla's death led to an inquiry by members of the Roman clergy, which he called a 'senate of Pharisees'; he was not convicted but left the city under a cloud. The new pope, Siricius, did not support him.[1][4]

c. 394–397

Ban by John of Jerusalem

During the Origenist controversy, Jerome sided with Epiphanius of Salamis against Bishop John of Jerusalem. After Epiphanius ordained Jerome's brother Paulinian a priest for the Bethlehem monastery, outside John's authority, John barred the monks from the churches of Bethlehem and sought an imperial order to expel Jerome from Palestine.[2][4]

416

Attack on the Bethlehem monasteries

A band of armed men attacked the monasteries of Jerome and Eustochium, killed a deacon, and set buildings on fire; Jerome took refuge in a fortified tower. The attack was blamed on supporters of Pelagius, whom Jerome had attacked in writing, though the attackers were not identified.[4][5]
