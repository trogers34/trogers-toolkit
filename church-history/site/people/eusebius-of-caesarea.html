Eusebius of Caesarea

c. 263–c. 339 · Early Church

Also known as Eusebius Pamphili; 'the Father of Church History'

Bishop of Caesarea in Palestine, biblical scholar, and the first historian of the church

Bishop and scholar whose Ecclesiastical History is the main surviving source for the first three centuries of Christianity. He lived through the Great Persecution, became a leading adviser and admirer of Constantine, sympathized with Arius before accepting the Nicene Creed, and later took part in the depositions of Eustathius, Athanasius, and Marcellus.

What they did

  • Wrote the Ecclesiastical History (in several editions, c. 300–326), the first history of the church, preserving long quotations from earlier Christian and Jewish writers, lists of bishops, and accounts of martyrs that survive nowhere else.[1][7]
  • His Chronicle set world history from Abraham onward in parallel columns; Jerome translated and continued it, and it shaped Western chronology for a thousand years.[7][8]
  • Devised the Eusebian Canons, tables for finding parallel passages in the four Gospels, which were copied into Gospel manuscripts for centuries.[7][8]
  • Wrote the Preparation for the Gospel and the Demonstration of the Gospel, a large defence of Christianity against pagan and Jewish critics, and the Onomasticon, a gazetteer of biblical place names.[6][8]
  • Recorded the persecution he lived through in The Martyrs of Palestine, naming victims and the governors who condemned them.[2][6]
  • Became the leading Christian admirer of Constantine: his orations and Life of Constantine present the emperor as God's chosen ruler, and they are the main source for Constantine's conversion story, though historians treat them as panegyric rather than neutral history.[3][6]
  • At first sympathized with Arius and wrote in his support; at Nicaea he presented his church's baptismal creed, then signed the Nicene Creed and wrote to his church explaining how he understood 'of one substance' (homoousios).[4][6]
  • Took part in the anti-Nicene party's campaigns after Nicaea, joining the depositions of Eustathius of Antioch (c. 327), Athanasius (335), and Marcellus of Ancyra (336).[6][5]
  • His letter to Constantine's sister Constantia, refusing her request for an image of Christ, was later cited by iconoclasts, and the Second Council of Nicaea (787) rejected his authority on images and questioned his orthodoxy.[6][8]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Eusebius never ordered or carried out violence, but he took part in church councils that deposed and helped send into exile three bishops of the opposing Nicene party, and in a polemical work he urged the condemnation of Marcellus. He also praised Constantine's laws against heretics and pagan sacrifice in the Life of Constantine.

c. 327–328

Deposition of Eustathius of Antioch

Took part

Against: Eustathius, bishop of Antioch, and his supporters

A synod at Antioch led by bishops of the pro-Arian party, including Eusebius and Eusebius of Nicomedia, deposed Eustathius, a leading defender of Nicaea, on charges that included doctrinal error and insulting the emperor's mother; Constantine exiled him. Eusebius then declined the vacant see.[4][6]

Outcome: Eustathius exiled to Thrace, where he died.

335

Council of Tyre against Athanasius

Took part

Against: Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria

Eusebius was one of the bishops at the Council of Tyre, which deposed Athanasius on charges of violence against the Melitians that Athanasius said were invented; Constantine then exiled Athanasius to Trier. Athanasius later named Eusebius among his persecutors.[5][6]

Outcome: Athanasius deposed and exiled (336–337).

336

Condemnation of Marcellus of Ancyra

Called for it

Against: Marcellus, bishop of Ancyra

Eusebius attended the synod at Constantinople that deposed Marcellus for Sabellianism and wrote Against Marcellus and On the Theology of the Church to justify the sentence.[6][8]

Outcome: Marcellus deposed and exiled.

c. 337–339

Praise of imperial laws against heretics and pagan sacrifice

Approved or defended it

Against: Novatianists, Valentinians, Marcionites, Paul of Samosata's followers, and other groups; pagan cults

In the Life of Constantine he quoted and praised Constantine's edict banning the meetings of named heretical groups and seizing their churches, and the emperor's measures against pagan sacrifice and temples.[3][6]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Eusebius lived through the Great Persecution (303–313), in which his teacher Pamphilus was martyred. He was later accused, at the Council of Tyre in 335, of having sacrificed to escape prison; the charge is known only from hostile sources and is unproven.

Excommunications

He was provisionally excommunicated in 325, before Nicaea, and restored when he signed the Nicene Creed. He was never condemned by name as a heretic, though the Second Council of Nicaea (787) questioned his orthodoxy.

303–313

The Great Persecution and the martyrdom of Pamphilus

During the persecution begun by Diocletian and continued in the East by Galerius and Maximinus Daia, Eusebius's teacher Pamphilus was imprisoned at Caesarea (307) and executed (310) with several companions. Eusebius visited him in prison and helped him write a defence of Origen; he also saw martyrdoms in Egypt. Whether Eusebius himself was imprisoned is not clear.[2][6]

335

Accusation of apostasy at Tyre

According to Epiphanius, the Egyptian bishop Potamon of Heraclea, a supporter of Athanasius, accused Eusebius at the Council of Tyre of having escaped prison during the persecution by sacrificing to the gods. Eusebius left the meeting in anger. No evidence supports the charge.[6]
