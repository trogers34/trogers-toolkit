Francis Schaeffer

1912–1984 · Modern

Also known as Francis August Schaeffer IV

American Presbyterian pastor, apologist, and founder of L'Abri in Switzerland

A Presbyterian pastor who, with his wife Edith, opened their Swiss chalet as L'Abri ('the shelter'), where thousands of young people came with questions about faith, art, and philosophy. His books, such as The God Who Is There and How Should We Then Live?, made him one of the most influential evangelical thinkers of the 20th century. In his last years he helped mobilize American evangelicals against abortion and called for civil disobedience against laws he considered tyrannical, shaping the rise of the Christian Right. His son Frank later wrote a critical memoir of him and the movement.

What they did

  • Founded L'Abri with Edith Schaeffer in 1955, an open home and study centre in the Swiss Alps that welcomed students and seekers of any background; branches later opened in England, the Netherlands, the United States, and elsewhere.[3][6]
  • Developed a 'presuppositional' cultural apologetic arguing that Western thought, art, and culture had lost a basis for truth and meaning without the God of the Bible, in The God Who Is There (1968), Escape from Reason (1968), and He Is There and He Is Not Silent (1972).[7][11]
  • Brought evangelicals into serious engagement with art, film, philosophy, and history; How Should We Then Live? (1976) was a widely viewed book and film series.[7][6]
  • With C. Everett Koop produced Whatever Happened to the Human Race? (1979), a book and film that helped turn many American evangelicals against abortion and euthanasia.[7][4]
  • In A Christian Manifesto (1981) urged Christians to resist the 'humanist' state through politics, the courts, and civil disobedience, and argued that force could be justified as a last resort against a tyrannical government; the book influenced the Christian Right and later anti-abortion activists, including some who blockaded clinics.[2][7]
  • Called for a 'balanced' separatism after leaving Carl McIntire's Bible Presbyterian Church in the 1950s split, and wrote The Mark of the Christian (1970) on love as the sign of true Christians.[7][6]
  • Warned in The Great Evangelical Disaster (1984) that evangelicals were accommodating to the culture, especially on biblical inerrancy.[7]
  • His son Frank Schaeffer, after first working with him on the films, later left evangelicalism and in Crazy for God (2007) described his father as prone to rages and alleged that he sometimes hit Edith; other family members and L'Abri colleagues disputed parts of the account.[4][7]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Schaeffer ordering or carrying out persecution. In his last years he called for laws against abortion and for civil disobedience, and argued that force could be legitimate against a tyrannical state; critics, including his son Frank, have said this rhetoric helped inspire militant anti-abortion activism, though Schaeffer did not call for attacks on people.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

In 1955 the authorities of the Catholic canton of Valais ordered the Schaeffers to leave because of their 'religious influence' after a local woman joined their Bible studies. They moved to Huémoz in Protestant Vaud and stayed in Switzerland. He also faced criticism and isolation within Carl McIntire's separatist movement, which split in 1956.

Excommunications

Schaeffer was never excommunicated or deposed. He left Carl McIntire's Bible Presbyterian Church with the majority in the 1956 split; his denomination later became the Reformed Presbyterian Church, Evangelical Synod, which joined the Presbyterian Church in America in 1982.

14 February 1955

Expulsion order from the canton of Valais

While the family lived at Champéry, a Catholic village in the canton of Valais, they received official letters ordering them to leave the canton for having had a 'religious influence' in the village, after a local woman began attending their Bible studies. Edith Schaeffer's account says Catholic local leaders and officials pressed for the order. The family found a chalet at Huémoz in the Protestant canton of Vaud, where they were allowed to stay, and opened L'Abri there.[3][5]
