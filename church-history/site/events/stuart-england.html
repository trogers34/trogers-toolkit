Religion in Stuart England

1603–1714 · Reformation

Also known as ['Religion under the Stuarts, 1603–1714']

How the Church of England, Puritans, Catholics, Scots Presbyterians, and new sects fought over worship and church government from James I's accession to Queen Anne's death, through civil war, the abolition and restoration of bishops, the Great Ejection, the Glorious Revolution, and the Toleration Act.

Began
24 March 1603[2][13]
Ended
1 August 1714[2][13]
Place
England, Scotland, Wales, and Ireland

Background

  • Elizabeth I left a Protestant Church of England governed by bishops and the Book of Common Prayer; Puritans wanted further reform of ceremonies and some wanted to replace bishops with presbyteries, while Catholics (recusants) were fined and their priests treated as traitors.[2][3]
  • James I had been raised in the Presbyterian Church of Scotland but favored bishops, summing up his view at Hampton Court as 'No bishop, no king'.[2][13]

What happened

  • In 1603 Puritan clergy presented the Millenary Petition, said to speak for a thousand ministers, asking for changes such as dropping the sign of the cross in baptism and the surplice. At the Hampton Court Conference (January 1604) James granted few of them, but accepted John Rainolds's proposal for a new translation of the Bible, published in 1611.[1][9][2]
  • The 1604 canons required clergy to subscribe to the Prayer Book and the Thirty-Nine Articles; some dozens of ministers (estimates run to about 80) were deprived for refusing.[2][3]
  • After the Gunpowder Plot (5 November 1605), in which Robert Catesby, Guy Fawkes, and other Catholics planned to blow up the king and Parliament, Parliament passed harsher recusancy laws and an Oath of Allegiance (1606) denying the pope's power to depose kings, which Pope Paul V forbade Catholics to take.[8][13]
  • In 1612 Bartholomew Legate and Edward Wightman were burned for heresy (anti-Trinitarian and other beliefs), the last people burned for heresy in England.[2][13]
  • Under Charles I, William Laud (archbishop of Canterbury from 1633) and his allies, called 'Arminians' by opponents, promoted ceremony, railed altars at the east end of churches, and the 'beauty of holiness', and used the Court of High Commission and Star Chamber against Puritan critics. Puritans saw this as a drift toward Rome.[7][5]
  • Imposing a new Prayer Book on Scotland led to a riot in St Giles' Cathedral, Edinburgh (23 July 1637; the story of Jenny Geddes throwing a stool is tradition) and to the National Covenant (February 1638). The Glasgow Assembly (late 1638) abolished bishops in Scotland, and the Bishops' Wars (1639–40) that followed forced Charles to recall Parliament.[5][13]
  • The Long Parliament (from 1640) impeached Laud, removed bishops from the House of Lords (1642), and after civil war began (1642) called the Westminster Assembly (1643), which wrote the Westminster Confession and Catechisms. Parliament made the Solemn League and Covenant with the Scots (1643), replaced the Prayer Book with the Directory for Public Worship (1645), and abolished bishops (1646).[2][3][13]
  • Laud was executed in January 1645 and Charles I on 30 January 1649. Under the Commonwealth and Protectorate, Oliver Cromwell allowed wide liberty to Protestant groups (Independents, Baptists, and others), though not to Catholics, Anglican Prayer Book worship, or those judged blasphemous; Quakers, Ranters, Fifth Monarchists, and other sects multiplied.[6][2][3]
  • After the Whitehall Conference (December 1655), called in answer to Menasseh ben Israel's petition, reached no formal decision, Cromwell let the small Jewish community in London worship and acquire a burial ground in 1656, an informal readmission 366 years after the expulsion of 1290.[10][13]
  • After the Restoration of Charles II (1660), bishops and the Prayer Book returned. The Act of Uniformity (1662) ejected about 2,000 ministers, and the 'Clarendon Code' (Corporation Act 1661, Conventicle Act 1664, Five Mile Act 1665) restricted Dissenters. Charles's Declaration of Indulgence (1672) was withdrawn under pressure from Parliament, which passed the Test Act (1673) excluding Catholics and Dissenters from office.[3][4][1]
  • Titus Oates's false story of a Jesuit 'Popish Plot' to murder Charles II (1678) led to the trials and executions of Catholics, including Archbishop Oliver Plunkett of Armagh (1681), and to the Exclusion Crisis over whether Charles's Catholic brother James could inherit the throne.[12][13]
  • James II (1685–88), a Catholic, issued Declarations of Indulgence (1687 and 1688) suspending the penal laws for Catholics and Dissenters. When Archbishop William Sancroft and six bishops petitioned against reading the 1688 Declaration in churches, they were sent to the Tower and tried for seditious libel, and were acquitted on 30 June 1688.[4][2]
  • William of Orange landed in November 1688 and James fled. The Bill of Rights (1689) barred Catholics from the throne, the Toleration Act (1689) let Trinitarian Protestant Dissenters worship in licensed meeting houses, and in Scotland bishops were abolished and the Presbyterian Church of Scotland established (1690).[3][2][13]
  • Archbishop Sancroft, several bishops, and about 400 clergy who would not swear allegiance to William and Mary while James lived (the non-jurors) were deprived of their posts and formed a small separate church.[4][13]
  • The Act of Settlement (1701) gave the succession to the Protestant heirs of Sophia of Hanover and required the monarch to be in communion with the Church of England. Under Anne, High Church and Low Church parties clashed, notably over the trial of Henry Sacheverell (1710), the Occasional Conformity Act (1711), and the Schism Act (1714).[2][13]

Outcomes and significance

  • The King James Bible became the standard English Bible for some three centuries.[9][13]
  • The Church of England survived with bishops and the Prayer Book, but English Protestantism was permanently divided between the established church and Dissenting Presbyterians, Congregationalists, Baptists, and Quakers, who had legal worship after 1689 but stayed barred from universities and most public office until the 19th century.[3][4]
  • Catholics remained under penal laws, and the Catholic exclusion from the throne set in 1689 and 1701 remains in law, although a 2013 act lifted the bar on heirs marrying Catholics.[2][13]
  • Scotland kept a Presbyterian established church, guaranteed in the Act of Union of 1707; Scottish Episcopalians became a separate, largely non-juring church.[2][13]
  • The Westminster Confession, written for an English church that never adopted it, became the standard of Presbyterians worldwide.[2][13]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Every party that held power used it against others: Catholics, Puritans, Laudians, Quakers, Covenanters, and Dissenters were all fined, imprisoned, or executed at some point between 1603 and 1689, and war in Ireland brought killings of Protestants and Catholics. Numbers for the Irish killings are disputed. After 1689 Protestant Dissenters had legal worship but Catholics and anti-Trinitarians did not.

1603–1640s

Executions and fines of Catholics under James I and Charles I

After the Gunpowder Plot the surviving plotters were hanged, drawn, and quartered (January 1606), as was the Jesuit superior Henry Garnet (May 1606), who had learned of the plot in confession. Catholic priests continued to be executed as traitors in smaller numbers than under Elizabeth, and recusants paid heavy fines.[8][2]

March–April 1612

Burning of Bartholomew Legate and Edward Wightman

Legate, who denied the Trinity, was burned at Smithfield after James I questioned him personally; Wightman was burned at Lichfield after recanting at the stake at a first attempt and then withdrawing his recantation.[2][13]

1630s

Punishment of Puritan critics under Laud

Star Chamber had the ears of the pamphleteers William Prynne, Henry Burton, and John Bastwick cut off in 1637 for attacking the bishops; Puritan ministers were suspended by the Court of High Commission, and many Puritans emigrated to New England.[7][5]

1641–1649

Killings in Ireland: the 1641 rising and Drogheda

In the Catholic rising of October 1641 Protestant settlers in Ulster were driven out and many killed, as at Portadown; modern estimates are about 4,000 killed directly, far below contemporary claims of over 100,000. In September 1649 Cromwell's army stormed Drogheda and killed most of the garrison, along with Catholic priests and some civilians, about 3,500 people in all by most counts.[2][13]

1640–1649

Executions of William Laud and Charles I

Laud was impeached by the Long Parliament in December 1640, imprisoned in the Tower, tried in 1644, and beheaded under an ordinance of attainder after the Lords would not convict him of treason. Charles I was tried by a High Court of Justice set up by the purged Commons and beheaded on 30 January 1649.[7][2]

October–December 1656

Punishment of James Nayler

After the Quaker James Nayler rode into Bristol with followers singing 'Holy, holy, holy', Parliament itself tried him and found him guilty of 'horrid blasphemy'. He was whipped through London, pilloried, had his tongue bored with a hot iron and his forehead branded with 'B', and was imprisoned.[11][6]

1662–1689

Prosecution of Dissenters under the Clarendon Code

Ejected ministers and lay Dissenters were fined and imprisoned for holding services; Quakers suffered most, and hundreds died in prison. John Bunyan was jailed for about 12 years in Bedford.[3][4]

1660s–1688

Persecution of Covenanters in Scotland

After bishops were restored in Scotland, Presbyterian ministers who would not conform held field meetings (conventicles), which were broken up by troops; risings in 1666 and 1679 were crushed, and in the 'Killing Time' of the 1680s Covenanters were shot without trial or executed. Covenanters murdered Archbishop James Sharp of St Andrews in 1679.[2][13]

1678–1681

Popish Plot trials

On the false evidence of Titus Oates and other informers, about 22 Catholics, mostly priests and laymen accused of plotting to kill the king, were executed, among them Edward Coleman (1678), Viscount Stafford (1680), and Archbishop Oliver Plunkett (1681).[12][13]
