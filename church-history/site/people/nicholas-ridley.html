Nicholas Ridley

c. 1500–1555 · Reformation

Also known as Bishop Ridley; one of the Oxford Martyrs

Bishop of Rochester and of London under Edward VI; burned at Oxford with Hugh Latimer

A Cambridge scholar and close ally of Archbishop Thomas Cranmer who became one of the leading bishops of the Edwardian Reformation. As Bishop of London he replaced altars with communion tables and helped shape the Book of Common Prayer and Protestant teaching on the Lord's Supper. He sat on the commissions that deprived Catholic bishops and condemned radical heretics, two of whom were burned. After backing Lady Jane Grey in 1553 he was imprisoned by Mary I, and in 1555 he was condemned for heresy and burned at Oxford with Hugh Latimer.

What they did

  • Was one of the main theologians of the Edwardian Reformation; his reading of the ninth-century monk Ratramnus helped move Cranmer to a Reformed view of the Lord's Supper, which shaped the 1549 and 1552 Books of Common Prayer.[4][2]
  • As Bishop of London from 1550 ordered the removal of stone altars in favour of wooden communion tables, a step soon imposed across England by the Privy Council.[3][4]
  • Wrote A Brief Declaration of the Lord's Supper in prison, a defence of the Protestant view of the Eucharist, published after his death.[2][6]
  • Defended Protestant doctrine against Catholic theologians in the Oxford disputations of April 1554.[1][4]
  • Urged Edward VI to found charitable institutions for London's poor; Christ's Hospital, St Thomas's Hospital, and Bridewell were set up in 1552–1553 after a sermon he preached before the king.[3][6]
  • Insisted against John Hooper in the vestments controversy (1550–1551) that bishops wear the prescribed robes, an early clash between conforming Protestants and those who later became Puritans.[3][4]
  • Supported the attempt to make Lady Jane Grey queen in July 1553, preaching against Mary's right to the throne.[3][5]
  • Became, with Latimer and Cranmer, one of the best-known Protestant martyrs, commemorated by the Martyrs' Memorial in Oxford (1843) and in John Foxe's Acts and Monuments.[1][7]

Persecution they supported or carried out

As a bishop under Edward VI, Ridley sat on royal commissions that deprived Catholic bishops and condemned two radical Protestants who were burned for heresy, and he pressed John Hooper to conform over vestments until Hooper was imprisoned. Like most magisterial Reformers he accepted that obstinate heretics could be put to death.

September–October 1549

Deprivation of Bishop Edmund Bonner

Took part

Against: Edmund Bonner, Catholic Bishop of London

Ridley sat with Archbishop Cranmer, Secretary William Petre, Sir Thomas Smith, and others on the commission that tried Bonner for failing to preach royal supremacy and the new order as instructed. Bonner was deprived and imprisoned in the Marshalsea, and Ridley succeeded him as Bishop of London.[3][4]

Outcome: Bonner was jailed until Mary I's accession in 1553, when he was restored and Ridley removed.

1549–2 May 1550

Condemnation of Joan Bocher

Took part

Against: Joan Bocher (Joan of Kent), an Anabaptist who denied that Christ took flesh from Mary

Ridley was among the commissioners, headed by Cranmer, who examined and condemned Joan Bocher for heresy; he and others tried to persuade her to recant during a year's delay. Edward VI's council signed the warrant, and she was burned at Smithfield.[4][3]

Outcome: Burned at Smithfield, London, on 2 May 1550.

February 1551

Deprivation of Bishop Stephen Gardiner

Took part

Against: Stephen Gardiner, Catholic Bishop of Winchester

Ridley served on the commission under Cranmer that heard the case against Gardiner, imprisoned since 1548 for resisting the religious changes, and deprived him of his see.[4][6]

Outcome: Gardiner stayed in the Tower until Mary I freed him in 1553 and made him Lord Chancellor.

April 1551

Condemnation of George van Parris

Took part

Against: George van Parris, a Flemish surgeon who denied the divinity of Christ

Ridley sat with Cranmer, Miles Coverdale, and others on the commission that excommunicated and condemned van Parris for Arianism; the royal council then ordered his execution.[4][3]

Outcome: Burned at Smithfield, 24 April 1551.

1550–January 1551

Pressure on John Hooper in the vestments controversy

Called for it

Against: John Hooper, bishop-elect of Gloucester

Ridley led the opposition to Hooper's refusal to wear episcopal vestments at his consecration, writing against his position. The Privy Council confined Hooper to his house and then to the Fleet Prison until he gave way.[3][4]

Outcome: Hooper submitted and was consecrated in March 1551; the two were reconciled in prison under Mary.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Ridley was arrested for supporting Lady Jane Grey, imprisoned for over two years under Mary I, and burned for heresy after Parliament restored the medieval heresy laws and England was reconciled with Rome.

Excommunications

Ridley was condemned as an obstinate heretic in 1555 by Cardinal Pole's legatine commission, degraded from holy orders, and handed over to be burned. The sentence was never formally revoked by Rome; the Church of England honours him as a martyr on 16 October.

July 1553–March 1554

Arrest and imprisonment in the Tower

After Mary I defeated Lady Jane Grey, Ridley, who had preached against Mary's claim, was arrested, deprived of the bishopric of London (Bonner was restored), and imprisoned in the Tower of London.[3][5]

April 1554

Oxford disputations

Ridley, Cranmer, and Latimer were taken to Oxford and made to dispute on the Mass before representatives of the Convocation and the universities, led by Hugh Weston, prolocutor of the lower house of Convocation. They were declared heretics at the end, though no legal sentence could yet be carried out.[1][4]

Outcome: Held in Oxford (at the house of the mayor, Edmund Irish) for another eighteen months.

30 September–16 October 1555

Heresy trial, degradation, and burning

After the heresy laws were revived, Cardinal Reginald Pole, as papal legate, appointed John White (Bishop of Lincoln), James Brooks (Bishop of Gloucester), and John Holyman (Bishop of Bristol) to try Ridley and Latimer in the Divinity School at Oxford. Ridley refused to accept papal authority or the Mass and was condemned, handed to the secular power, degraded from the priesthood on 15 October, and burned on 16 October. Cranmer was made to watch from the Bocardo prison. His brother-in-law tied gunpowder to him, but the fire, banked with green wood, burned his lower body slowly before it went off.[1][5][3]
