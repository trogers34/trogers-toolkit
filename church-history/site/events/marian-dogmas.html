Marian Dogmas of the Catholic Church

431–1950 · Medieval

Also known as Mother of God (431), Perpetual Virginity (649), Immaculate Conception (1854), Assumption (1950)

The four teachings about Mary that the Catholic Church has defined as dogma, binding on all Catholics: that she is the Mother of God, that she remained a virgin, that she was conceived without original sin, and that she was taken body and soul into heaven.

Began
22 June 431 (Council of Ephesus)[1][6]
Ended
1 November 1950 (Munificentissimus Deus)[3][7]
Place

Agreed
Four dogmas: Mother of God (431), Perpetual Virginity (649), Immaculate Conception (1854), Assumption (1950).

Background

  • The New Testament says Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the virgin Mary; later teachings about her developed over centuries from the creeds, the church fathers, liturgy, and popular devotion.[6][5]
  • The title 'ever-virgin' and the belief that Mary had no other children were widely held by the fourth century; Jerome defended them against Helvidius (c. 383), who argued that the 'brothers of Jesus' were Mary's children.[5][6]
  • Augustine wrote that, out of honor to the Lord, he would raise no question about Mary when it came to sin, a passage later cited in the debate over the Immaculate Conception.[5]
  • Stories of Mary's death and bodily assumption circulated from about the fifth century, and the feast of her Dormition or Assumption was kept in both East and West by the seventh century, though the New Testament does not describe her death.[6][3]

What happened

  • Mother of God (431): the Council of Ephesus, led by Cyril of Alexandria, upheld the title Theotokos ('God-bearer') against Nestorius; the point was mainly about Christ being one person, divine and human.[1][6]
  • Perpetual Virginity (649): the Lateran Synod under Pope Martin I declared anathema anyone who did not confess that Mary conceived without seed, gave birth without corruption, and remained a virgin after the birth.[2][5]
  • Immaculate Conception (1854): after centuries of dispute, mainly between Dominicans, who followed Thomas Aquinas in denying it, and Franciscans, who followed John Duns Scotus in affirming it, Pius IX declared that Mary was preserved from original sin from the first moment of her conception, by a singular grace in view of Christ's merits. He had consulted the world's bishops in 1849.[2][5][7]
  • Assumption (1950): after consulting the bishops in 1946, Pius XII declared that Mary, 'having completed the course of her earthly life, was assumed body and soul into heavenly glory', leaving open whether she died first. It is the only definition made explicitly under the papal infallibility defined in 1870.[3][7]

  • Martin Luther Kept the title Mother of God and belief in Mary's perpetual virginity, while rejecting prayer to the saints
  • John Henry Newman Defended the Immaculate Conception and Marian devotion in his Letter to Pusey (1865–66)
  • Pius IX Defined the Immaculate Conception (1854)
  • Pius XII Defined the Assumption (1950)

Outcomes and significance

  • The Eastern Orthodox churches hold Mary as Mother of God and ever-virgin and celebrate her Dormition, but do not accept the Immaculate Conception, which assumes a Western view of original sin, and object to the pope defining dogma alone.[6][7]
  • The Protestant Reformers kept 'Mother of God' and most held to her perpetual virginity, but Protestant churches reject the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption as dogmas without biblical basis, and most modern Protestants do not hold to her perpetual virginity.[6][4]
  • The 1950 definition was criticized by Protestant and Orthodox churches as a new obstacle to unity. The Anglican–Roman Catholic International Commission's statement Mary: Grace and Hope in Christ (2005) sought common ground on the two papal dogmas.[4][7]
  • The Second Vatican Council placed its teaching on Mary within its document on the church (Lumen Gentium, 1964) rather than issue a separate document, and no further Marian dogma has been defined; proposals to define Mary as 'Co-Redemptrix' have not been adopted.[1][7]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The Marian dogmas were not enforced by executions. The main coercive episode was the medieval fight over the Immaculate Conception, in which universities expelled or disciplined theologians who denied it.

1387–1403

Paris against the Dominicans over the Immaculate Conception

The theology faculty of the University of Paris condemned propositions of the Dominican Juan de Monzón, including his denial of the Immaculate Conception. When the Dominicans backed him, the university excluded the order from its faculty; the Avignon pope Clement VII upheld the condemnation, and the Dominicans were not readmitted until 1403. In 1483 Pope Sixtus IV forbade either side, on pain of excommunication, to call the other heretics.[5]
