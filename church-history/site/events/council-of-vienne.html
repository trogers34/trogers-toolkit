Council of Vienne

1311–1312 · Medieval

Also known as Fifteenth ecumenical council (Catholic count)

Pope Clement V's council, held under pressure from Philip IV of France, which suppressed the Knights Templar and defined that the rational soul is the form of the human body.

Began
16 October 1311[1][2]
Ended
6 May 1312[1][2]
Place
Vienne, Dauphiné (modern France)

Background

  • On 13 October 1307 Philip IV had every Templar in France arrested on charges of heresy, idolatry, and sodomy; under torture by royal agents and the French inquisitor, most, including the Grand Master Jacques de Molay, confessed to some charges.[2][3]
  • Clement V, who lived in France, first protested, then ordered all Christian rulers to arrest the Templars and set up papal commissions; in the Chinon parchment (1308) his cardinals absolved the Templar leaders.[2]
  • In May 1310 the provincial council of Sens, under Archbishop Philippe de Marigny, had 54 Templars burned as relapsed heretics after they withdrew their confessions to defend the order.[2]

What happened

  • Most bishops at the council wanted the Templars allowed to defend themselves, but under Philip's pressure (he arrived in Vienne with an armed escort in March 1312) Clement suppressed the order on 22 March 1312 'not by judicial sentence but by apostolic provision'.[2][1]
  • The bull Ad providam gave the Templars' property to the Knights Hospitaller, though much of it in France and Spain went to the crown or to new orders.[1][2]
  • The decree Fidei catholicae defined that the rational soul is of itself and essentially the form of the human body, against views attributed to the Franciscan Peter John Olivi.[1]
  • The council condemned eight errors attributed to the Beghards and Beguines, regulated Franciscan poverty (Exivi de paradiso), and ordered chairs of Hebrew, Arabic, Greek, and Syriac at leading universities.[1][3]

Key participants

  • Clement V Pope who called the council
  • Philip IV of France King who pressed for the Templars' condemnation
  • Jacques de Molay Last Grand Master of the Templars

Outcomes and significance

  • The Templar order ended. Most surviving members were pensioned or joined other orders; only in France did the persecution end in mass executions.[2][3]
  • Most historians now consider the charges against the Templars false, driven largely by Philip IV's need for money and control over the church.[2][3]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The council itself condemned no Templar to death, but it ended the order after years of torture and executions in France and left its leaders to their fate.

1307–1314

Arrest, torture, and burning of the Templars in France

Templars were arrested, tortured into confessions, and in 1310 burned in groups. In March 1314, after a papal commission sentenced Jacques de Molay and Geoffroi de Charney to life imprisonment, both retracted their confessions and Philip IV had them burned that evening on an island in the Seine.[2][3]
