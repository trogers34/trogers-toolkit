Dutch Reformed Church, Race, and Apartheid

1857–1994 · Modern

The division of the Dutch Reformed Church in South Africa into separate churches by race, its theological support for apartheid, its suspension by the world Reformed body in 1982, and the Belhar Confession and reunification efforts that followed.

Began
1857[2][3]
Ended
1994[2][4]
Place
South Africa

Background

  • The Dutch Reformed Church (NG Kerk) had served the Cape since 1652; in 1829 its synod held that communion should be given to all members regardless of color.[3][2]

What happened

  • In 1857 the synod allowed separate services by race as a concession to white members; this grew into separate churches for 'Coloured' (1881), Black African, and Indian members.[2][3]
  • In the 20th century church leaders and theologians developed a biblical defense of racial separation, which supported the National Party's apartheid policy after 1948.[2][4]
  • After the Sharpeville massacre (1960), the Cottesloe Consultation called for an end to racial exclusion in worship and other reforms; the church's synods rejected it and withdrew from the World Council of Churches.[2]
  • In 1982 the World Alliance of Reformed Churches declared apartheid a sin and its theological justification a heresy, and suspended the white Dutch Reformed churches; the Mission Church drafted the Belhar Confession, adopted in 1986.[2][1]

Key participants

  • Beyers Naudé Dutch Reformed minister who broke with apartheid and founded the Christian Institute
  • Allan Boesak Mission Church leader and president of the World Alliance of Reformed Churches (1982)
  • Desmond Tutu Anglican archbishop and critic of the church's support for apartheid

Outcomes and significance

  • The Dutch Reformed Church began to reject apartheid from 1986 and confessed its guilt in the 1990 Rustenburg Declaration; it was readmitted to the world Reformed body in 1998.[2][4]
  • The Mission Church and the Dutch Reformed Church in Africa united in 1994 as the Uniting Reformed Church; full reunion with the white Dutch Reformed Church has not been achieved.[2][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The apartheid state persecuted church critics of apartheid, while the church itself supported the system for decades.

1963–1984

Persecution of Beyers Naudé and the Christian Institute

Naudé was shunned and lost his ministry in the Dutch Reformed Church for founding the ecumenical, anti-apartheid Christian Institute (1963). In October 1977 the government banned the Institute and placed Naudé under a banning order restricting his movement, speech, and contacts for seven years.[2][4]
