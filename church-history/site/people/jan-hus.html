Jan Hus

c. 1372–1415 · Medieval

Also known as John Hus; John Huss

Priest, preacher at Bethlehem Chapel, rector of the University of Prague, reformer

Czech priest and university master who preached church reform in Prague, drawing on Wycliffe's ideas. Despite a promise of safe conduct, he was arrested at the Council of Constance and burned as a heretic, and his death set off the Hussite movement.

Born
c. 1372 (exact date unknown) · Husinec, southern Bohemia (modern Czech Republic)[4][6]
Died
6 July 1415 · Constance (Konstanz), burned at the stake by order of the Council of Constance[1][5]

What they did

  • Preached in Czech, not Latin, at the Bethlehem Chapel in Prague from 1402, drawing large crowds of ordinary people and calling for reform of clergy morals.[4][5]
  • Served as rector of the University of Prague (1409–1410) after King Wenceslas IV gave the Czech masters control of the university.[4][6]
  • Defended Wycliffe's writings in Prague when the church authorities ordered them surrendered and burned.[4][5]
  • Publicly opposed the sale of indulgences to fund Pope John XXIII's war against King Ladislaus of Naples (1412).[4][5]
  • Wrote The Church (De ecclesia, 1413), arguing that Christ, not the pope, is the head of the church, and that a pope or prelate who lives against Christ's law need not be obeyed.[3][4]
  • Wrote extensively in Czech, including sermons and instruction for lay people, and is traditionally credited with reforming Czech spelling with diacritical marks (his authorship of that treatise is debated).[6]
  • At the Council of Constance refused to recant unless shown from Scripture that he was wrong, and denied holding several of the views he was charged with.[1][5]
  • Wrote letters from prison to friends in Bohemia that were preserved and widely read.[2][4]
  • His execution sparked the Hussite movement and the Hussite Wars (1419–1434). The Unity of the Brethren, ancestor of the Moravian Church, grew out of his followers, and Luther wrote in 1520 that "we are all Hussites" without knowing it.[5][6]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Hus was excommunicated several times, driven out of Prague, arrested at the Council of Constance in spite of the emperor-elect's safe-conduct, tried for heresy, and burned on 6 July 1415. There was no reigning pope when he was condemned. Pope John XXIII, under whom he was arrested, had fled Constance and been deposed, so the council itself passed judgment. In 1999 Pope John Paul II expressed "deep regret" for his cruel death.

1408–1410

Wycliffe's books burned; Hus excommunicated by the Archbishop of Prague

The Archbishop of Prague ordered Wycliffe's writings surrendered. Using a bull of Pope Alexander V (December 1409) that banned preaching in private chapels such as the Bethlehem Chapel, he had about 200 Wycliffite manuscripts burned in July 1410 and excommunicated Hus for continuing to preach.[4][5]

February 1411

Excommunicated by the papal court

Hus was summoned to the papal court, but he sent representatives instead of going in person. Cardinal Odo Colonna, the judge in the case, excommunicated him for failing to appear.[4][5]

1412

Indulgence protest; protesters beheaded; interdict on Prague

Pope John XXIII proclaimed indulgences to fund a crusade against King Ladislaus of Naples. Hus denounced them. Three young men who interrupted indulgence preachers were beheaded by the Prague city authorities. At the urging of Hus's opponents, including Michael de Causis, the curia renewed his excommunication and placed Prague under interdict, halting church services, for as long as he stayed in the city. King Wenceslas, wanting good relations with the pope, withdrew much of his support.[4][5]

28 November 1414

Arrested at the Council of Constance despite a safe-conduct

Hus went to the Council of Constance to defend himself under a safe-conduct from Sigismund, King of the Romans and later emperor. About three weeks after he arrived, he was summoned before the cardinals and arrested. He was held first in a Dominican friary on an island in the lake, then in the Bishop of Constance's castle at Gottlieben. Sigismund protested at first, but then accepted the council's position that its authority over heresy came before his promise.[1][5][4]

5 June – 6 July 1415

Heresy trial and execution at Constance

Hus was given public hearings on 5, 7, and 8 June 1415 before the council, with Cardinal Pierre d'Ailly prominent in the questioning. His accusers included Michael de Causis and his former friend Stephen Páleč, and the Paris theologian Jean Gerson pressed for his condemnation. He refused to recant articles he said he had never held, or to abjure others unless shown wrong from Scripture. On 6 July 1415 the council condemned him as a heretic in Constance Cathedral, and bishops ritually stripped him of his priesthood. He was handed to the secular power: Sigismund gave him to Louis III, Elector Palatine, who had him burned outside the city, and his ashes were thrown into the Rhine. There was no reigning pope at the time, since John XXIII had been deposed in May and Gregory XII had resigned on 4 July.[1][5][4]

30 May 1416

His companion Jerome of Prague burned

Jerome of Prague, a scholar and Hus's ally who had come to Constance to support him, was arrested and pressured into a recantation, which he later withdrew. He was then condemned and burned at the same place as Hus.[5][6]
