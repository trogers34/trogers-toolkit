Richard Baxter

1615–1691 · Modern

English Puritan pastor, theologian, and devotional writer

Puritan minister whose pastoral work at Kidderminster became a model for parish ministry; a moderate who sought unity between Presbyterians, Episcopalians, and Independents, he was ejected in 1662, repeatedly prosecuted for preaching, and wrote some of the most widely read devotional books in English.

What they did

  • At Kidderminster (1641–42 and 1647–61) he turned an unruly parish into a model congregation by preaching and by catechizing every family in their homes, a method he urged on other ministers in The Reformed Pastor (1656).[1][2]
  • Organized the Worcestershire Association (1653), in which ministers of different church views agreed to cooperate in discipline and catechizing; it was copied in other counties.[3][2]
  • Wrote The Saints' Everlasting Rest (1650), A Call to the Unconverted (1658), and A Christian Directory (1673), a huge manual of Puritan practical ethics; he published well over a hundred works.[2][5]
  • Sought a 'mere Christian' unity: he refused to be bound to any party, argued for a church that Presbyterians and moderate Episcopalians could share, and at the Savoy Conference (1661) proposed revisions to the Prayer Book, which the bishops rejected.[1][2]
  • Served as a Parliamentary army chaplain (1645–47) in order to argue against the radical sects among the soldiers; he later opposed Cromwell's rule as a usurpation and supported the return of Charles II, whom he served briefly as a royal chaplain (1660).[1][3]
  • His theology, a middle way between Calvinism and Arminianism later called 'Baxterianism', provoked controversy with stricter Calvinists such as John Owen.[2][5]
  • His autobiography, Reliquiae Baxterianae (published 1696), is a major source for the religious history of the Civil War and Restoration.[1][2]
  • His 1681 Breviate of the life of his wife Margaret, who died that year, is one of the earliest English memoirs of a marriage.[2]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Baxter opposed the persecution of moderate dissenters and spent much of his life arguing for a broad church. He did not, however, support general toleration: in the 1650s he argued that the magistrate should restrain Catholics, Quakers, and other sects he judged dangerous, and he wrote sharply against them. No record shows him taking part in any arrest or punishment.

1650s–1659

Against: Catholics, Quakers, Socinians, and other sects

In A Holy Commonwealth (1659) and other writings he argued that a Christian magistrate should suppress public Catholic worship and restrain blasphemy and heresy, and he opposed Cromwell's toleration of the sects. He later withdrew A Holy Commonwealth (1670).[4][2]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

As a Nonconformist after 1662, Baxter was repeatedly prosecuted under the Clarendon Code for preaching. He was imprisoned twice, his goods and books were seized, and at his 1685 trial Judge Jeffreys abused him from the bench.

Excommunications

No record of his being excommunicated. He was barred from ministry in the Church of England by the Act of Uniformity (1662) but continued to attend his parish church.

1669

Imprisoned for holding a conventicle at Acton

Arrested for preaching to his household and neighbours at Acton, Middlesex, contrary to the Five Mile Act; he spent about a week in Clerkenwell prison before being released on a technicality.[1][2]
