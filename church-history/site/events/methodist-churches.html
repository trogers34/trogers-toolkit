Methodist Churches: Beginnings and Splits

1729–2023 · Modern

How Methodism grew from the Wesleys' Oxford Holy Club into separate churches in Britain and America, and divided over race, church government, slavery, holiness, and sexuality.

Began
1729[1][2]
Ended
2019–2023[3][4]
Place
England and the United States, then worldwide

Background

  • Methodism began as a renewal movement inside the Church of England, with societies, classes, and lay preachers organized by John Wesley.[1][2]

What happened

  • After the American Revolution, Wesley ordained preachers for America, and the 1784 Christmas Conference founded the Methodist Episcopal Church under Francis Asbury and Thomas Coke.[3][1]
  • Black Methodists, facing segregation in white churches, founded the African Methodist Episcopal (1816) and AME Zion (1821) churches.[3][4]
  • Splits followed over lay representation and bishops (1830), slavery (1843–44), and holiness teaching (1860s–1900s); British Methodism also divided and reunited in 1932.[3][2]
  • The United Methodist Church's long dispute over homosexuality ended in mass departures (2019–23) and the removal of its restrictions in 2024.[4]

Key participants

  • John Wesley Founder of Methodism
  • Charles Wesley Co-founder and hymn writer
  • George Whitefield Leader of Calvinistic Methodism
  • Francis Asbury First bishop of American Methodism
  • Richard Allen Founder of the AME Church

Outcomes and significance

  • Methodist and Wesleyan churches, including Holiness and (indirectly) Pentecostal churches that grew from them, have tens of millions of members worldwide.[2][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Early Methodists faced mobs in Britain (see the Wesleys' and Whitefield's pages). In America Black Methodists faced discrimination within the church itself.

c. 1787–1792

Black worshippers pulled from prayer at St. George's, Philadelphia

At St. George's Methodist Church, white trustees pulled Absalom Jones and other Black worshippers from their knees during prayer for sitting in a section reserved for whites; Richard Allen, Jones, and others walked out, a step toward the founding of the AME Church and St. Thomas African Episcopal Church.[3][4]
