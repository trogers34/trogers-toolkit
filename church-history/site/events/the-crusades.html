The Crusades

1095–1291 · Medieval

Also known as The crusades to the Holy Land, 1095–1291, with related crusades in Europe

A series of wars called by popes, which promised spiritual rewards to those who fought: above all the campaigns to capture and hold Jerusalem and the Holy Land, from Urban II's call in 1095 to the fall of Acre in 1291.

Began
27 November 1095 (Urban II's sermon at Clermont)[1][3]
Ended
18 May 1291 (fall of Acre)[3][4]
Place
The eastern Mediterranean (Syria, Palestine, Egypt), Constantinople, and parts of Europe

Background

  • The Seljuk Turks had taken most of Anatolia from the Byzantine Empire after the Battle of Manzikert (1071), and Emperor Alexios I Komnenos asked the West for military help in 1095.[3][8]
  • Pilgrimage to Jerusalem, which had been under Muslim rule since the 630s, was a central devotion of Western Christians; the church already offered remission of penance for pilgrimage.[3][4]
  • The reforming papacy of the 11th century sought to direct the violence of the knightly class toward ends it considered holy.[3][4]

What happened

  • On 27 November 1095 at the Council of Clermont, Pope Urban II called on Western knights to free the Eastern Christians and Jerusalem, offering full remission of penance to those who went; the crowd answered 'God wills it' (Deus vult), according to the chroniclers.[1][3]
  • The First Crusade took Antioch (1098) and Jerusalem (1099) and founded four crusader states: the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the County of Edessa, the Principality of Antioch, and the County of Tripoli.[1][3]
  • Military religious orders, the Knights Templar and the Knights Hospitaller, were founded to protect pilgrims and defend the crusader states.[3][8]
  • After Edessa fell (1144), the Second Crusade, led by Louis VII of France and Conrad III of Germany, failed at Damascus (1148). Saladin, sultan of Egypt and Syria, destroyed the army of Jerusalem at Hattin in 1187 and took the city.[3][2]
  • The Third Crusade recovered Acre and much of the coast but not Jerusalem; Richard I and Saladin agreed the Treaty of Jaffa (1192), allowing Christian pilgrims access to the city.[3][4]
  • The Fourth Crusade, in debt to Venice, attacked the Christian city of Zara (1202) and then Constantinople, which it sacked in April 1204, setting up a Latin empire there that lasted until 1261.[6][3]
  • Popes extended crusading to other enemies: the Cathars of southern France (from 1209), pagans in the Baltic, Muslims in Spain, and the popes' political opponents in Italy.[4][7]
  • Later crusades to Egypt (1217–21, 1248–54) failed. Frederick II regained Jerusalem by treaty in 1229, but it was lost in 1244, and the Mamluks took the last crusader strongholds, ending with Acre in 1291.[3][4]

Outcomes and significance

  • The crusader states lasted almost two centuries but were lost entirely by 1291.[3][8]
  • The sack of Constantinople in 1204 deepened the split between the Catholic and Orthodox churches; Pope John Paul II expressed regret for it to the Greek Orthodox Church in Athens in 2001.[6][8]
  • Crusade indulgences and their financing helped develop the system of indulgences that Luther later attacked.[4][3]
  • Crusading brought repeated violence against European Jews, and the memory of the crusades has shaped relations between Christians, Muslims, and Jews to the present day.[5][4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The crusades involved large-scale killing of non-combatants by crusaders and, in turn, by their Muslim opponents. The incidents below are among the best documented. Figures in medieval chronicles are often exaggerated, and modern estimates are given where possible.

May–June 1096

Rhineland massacres of Jews

Bands of crusaders on their way east attacked Jewish communities in Speyer, Worms, Mainz, Cologne, and other cities, killing thousands and forcing conversions; many Jews killed themselves and their families rather than be baptized. Some bishops, such as John of Speyer, protected Jews; others could not or did not. Urban II had not called for these attacks.[5][4]

15–16 July 1099

Sack of Jerusalem

When the First Crusade took Jerusalem, the crusaders killed many of the Muslim and Jewish inhabitants. The Christian chroniclers describe a massacre, including in the area of the Temple Mount; the later Muslim historian Ibn al-Athir's figure of 70,000 is considered greatly exaggerated, and a modern estimate is about 3,000.[1][2][3]

July 1187

Execution of captured military monks after Hattin

After the Battle of Hattin, Saladin had about 200 captured Templars and Hospitallers executed and personally killed Reynald of Châtillon, who had attacked Muslim caravans and pilgrims. Saladin's later capture of Jerusalem was notably less bloody: most inhabitants were allowed to ransom themselves.[2][4]

March 1190

Massacres of Jews in England

As England prepared for the Third Crusade, crowds attacked Jews in several towns. At York, about 150 Jews who had taken refuge in the royal castle died, many by their own hands, on 16 March 1190. Local nobles in debt to Jewish moneylenders, including Richard Malebisse, led the attack and burned the records of their debts.[4][8]

20 August 1191

Execution of Muslim prisoners at Acre

After taking Acre, Richard I had about 2,700 Muslim prisoners from the garrison, with women and children according to some accounts, killed outside the city when ransom negotiations with Saladin broke down.[2][4]

12–15 April 1204

Sack of Constantinople

Crusaders and Venetians stormed the Christian capital of the Byzantine Empire and plundered it for three days, killing civilians, assaulting nuns, and stripping churches, including the Hagia Sophia, of relics and treasure. Innocent III had forbidden attacks on Christians and condemned the sack, but accepted the Latin empire that followed.[6][3]

22 July 1209

Massacre at Béziers

In the first major action of the crusade against the Cathars, the crusader army took Béziers and killed much of its population, Catholic and Cathar alike, in the church of the Madeleine and elsewhere. The papal legate Arnaud Amalric reported to Innocent III that about 20,000 were killed. The words 'Kill them all; God will know his own' were attributed to him by Caesarius of Heisterbach some years later, and are probably not authentic.[7][4]

May 1268

Sack of Antioch

The Mamluk sultan Baybars took Antioch, one of the oldest Christian cities, killed or enslaved much of the population, and boasted of it in a letter to its absent prince, Bohemond VI.[3][2]
