Maximus the Greek

c. 1470–1556 · Reformation

Also known as Maxim Grek; Michael Trivolis (birth name); Saint Maximus the Greek

Greek monk, scholar, and translator in Muscovite Russia

A Greek scholar trained in Renaissance Italy who became a monk on Mount Athos and was sent to Moscow in 1518 to translate and correct Russian church books. He brought Greek learning to Muscovy, but his criticism of monastic landholding and of Russian church practices, and his alleged political contacts, led to two church trials (1525 and 1531), long imprisonment, and exclusion from communion. He was never allowed to go home, and was canonized by the Russian Orthodox Church in 1988.

What they did

  • Brought Greek and Renaissance learning to Muscovy: he translated the Psalter with patristic commentary (finished 1519, through Latin with Russian interpreters) and corrected errors in Slavonic liturgical books against the Greek originals.[2][3]
  • Wrote some 350 works in Russian Church Slavonic, on theology, ethics, grammar, and the duties of rulers, which circulated widely in Russian manuscripts.[2][4]
  • Supported the 'Non-possessors', led by Vassian Patrikeev, who argued that monasteries should not own villages and serfs, against the 'Possessors' (Josephites) who followed Joseph of Volokolamsk.[2][3]
  • Criticized injustice by rulers and officials, the oppression of peasants, usury by monasteries, and superstition such as astrology, in writings addressed to the grand prince and later to Ivan IV.[2][3]
  • His Italian experience gave Russians one of their first descriptions of Western scholarship, the Dominicans and Franciscans, and Savonarola, whom he admired.[2][3]
  • Wrote polemics against Latin (Catholic) doctrine, Islam, and Judaism, defending Orthodoxy in the manner usual for his time.[2][4]
  • Taught pupils who carried his influence into later Russian learning, and is honoured as a 'Russian enlightener'; the Russian Orthodox Church canonized him in 1988, and his relics were found at the Trinity Lavra in 1996.[3][4]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Maximus supporting or carrying out persecution of anyone. He wrote sharp polemics against Catholics, Muslims, Jews, and astrologers, but they argued doctrine and did not call for punishment. He criticized the harsh treatment of peasants and the poor.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Maximus was tried twice by Russian church councils, held in monastery prisons for about twenty years, barred from communion for much of that time, and refused permission to return to Mount Athos despite appeals from Eastern patriarchs. Historians debate how far the charges were doctrinal and how far political.

Excommunications

He was barred from receiving communion after his first trial; the ban was relaxed only years later. The condemnation was never formally reversed, but the Russian Orthodox Church canonized him in 1988.

1525

First trial and imprisonment at Volokolamsk

A church council in Moscow charged him with heresy, based on errors in his translations (which he blamed partly on his interpreters), with criticizing the Russian church's independence from Constantinople, and with treasonous contacts with the Ottoman ambassador Skinder. Many historians think his opposition to the Josephites and, possibly, to Vasily III's plan to divorce his wife were the real causes.[1][2]

1531

Second trial and confinement at Tver

Tried again with his ally Vassian Patrikeev, on charges including corrupting the sacred books and heretical opinions. He was transferred to the Otroch Monastery at Tver under Bishop Akaky, who treated him more kindly.[1][2]

Refused permission to return home
