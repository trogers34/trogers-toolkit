J. C. Ryle

1816–1900 · Modern

Also known as John Charles Ryle

Evangelical Anglican clergyman, writer, and first Bishop of Liverpool (1880–1900)

A leading voice of evangelical Anglicanism in Victorian England, known for plain, forceful writing such as Holiness and the Expository Thoughts on the Gospels. After his family's bank collapsed he became a country parson in Suffolk for nearly forty years, then the first Bishop of Liverpool. He was a strong opponent of Anglo-Catholic ritualism, and in his diocese a ritualist priest was prosecuted and briefly jailed.

What they did

  • Wrote Holiness (1877–79), a defence of Reformed teaching on sanctification against the 'higher life' (Keswick) movement, which remains one of the most widely read books on the Christian life.[2][3]
  • Wrote Expository Thoughts on the Gospels (1856–1873), plain devotional commentaries for family reading, and hundreds of popular tracts that sold in the millions.[3][4]
  • Wrote Knots Untied (1874) and Christian Leaders of the Last Century (1869), defending evangelical Anglican teaching and recalling the eighteenth-century revival leaders.[3][5]
  • As the first Bishop of Liverpool he put money into more clergy, mission halls, and a clergy pension fund rather than building a cathedral, a choice that drew criticism.[3][5]
  • Led the evangelical party in the Church of England against Anglo-Catholic ritualism, while urging evangelicals to stay in the established church and take part in its councils.[3][4]
  • Was widowed twice and lost his third wife in 1889; he raised several children, among them Herbert Edward Ryle, who became Bishop of Winchester and Dean of Westminster.[1][3]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Ryle did not prosecute anyone himself, but as Bishop of Liverpool he allowed a prosecution of a ritualist priest under the Public Worship Regulation Act to go ahead when he had the power to stop it. He supported legal limits on ritual while saying he regretted imprisonment.

1885–1887

Prosecution of the ritualist priest James Bell Cox

Approved or defended it

Against: James Bell Cox, vicar of St Margaret's, Princes Road, Liverpool, and his Anglo-Catholic congregation

Members of the Church Association brought a case against Bell Cox under the Public Worship Regulation Act (1874) for using ritual practices the courts had banned. Under the Act the bishop could veto proceedings; Ryle did not. Bell Cox refused to obey the court and was jailed in Walton prison in 1887 for about two weeks before being released on a legal technicality.[3][5]

Outcome: Bell Cox was released and continued at St Margaret's; the case damaged the reputation of the Act.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Ryle was never persecuted. Anglo-Catholics and others criticized him sharply, and his decisions as bishop were attacked in the church press, but this was opposition, not persecution.

Excommunications

Ryle was never excommunicated or disciplined by any church.
