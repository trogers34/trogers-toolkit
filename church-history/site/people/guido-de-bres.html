Guido de Brès

1522–1567 · Reformation

Also known as Guy de Bray; Guy de Brès

Reformed preacher in the Southern Netherlands; author of the Belgic Confession

A glass painter from Mons who became a Reformed preacher to the persecuted 'churches under the cross' of the Habsburg Netherlands. He wrote the Belgic Confession (1561), which became, with the Heidelberg Catechism and the Canons of Dort, one of the standards of Reformed churches. Hunted by the authorities for most of his ministry, he was captured after the siege of Valenciennes in 1567 and hanged. The original text of his Confession also called on magistrates to suppress 'idolatry and false worship'.

What they did

  • Wrote the Belgic Confession (1561), modelled on the French Confession of 1559, to show Philip II that the Reformed were law-abiding Christians, not rebels or Anabaptists.[3][1]
  • Saw his Confession adopted by Reformed synods at Antwerp (1566) and later confirmed by the Synod of Dort (1618–19), making it a standard of Dutch Reformed and many other Reformed churches to this day.[3][5]
  • Served as an itinerant pastor to secret Reformed congregations at Lille, Tournai, Valenciennes, and elsewhere at the risk of his life.[3][2]
  • Wrote Le Baston de la foy chrestienne (1555), gathering patristic texts to argue that Reformed teaching was the faith of the early church.[3]
  • Worked to separate the Reformed movement from Anabaptism in La racine, source et fondement des Anabaptistes (1565).[3]
  • Wrote letters from prison to his wife and mother, published in Jean Crespin's martyrology, which became classics of Reformed devotion.[2]

Persecution they supported or carried out

De Brès never held power and was himself persecuted, but the original Article 36 of his Belgic Confession taught that magistrates must 'remove and prevent all idolatry and false worship, that the kingdom of antichrist may be destroyed', and the Confession condemned the Anabaptists. Later Reformed churches revised or footnoted that article as endorsing religious coercion.

Called for it

Against: Catholic worship ('idolatry') and dissenting sects, especially Anabaptists

Article 36 of the Belgic Confession gave civil rulers the duty to protect the church and to 'remove and prevent all idolatry and false worship', and declared detestation of the Anabaptists and other seditious people. The text did not prescribe penalties, but it was later read as approving state coercion in religion. Synods of the Christian Reformed Church (1910, 1938, 1958) and other churches later removed or qualified the clause.[1][3]

Outcome: The clause stood in the Dutch Reformed churches for centuries; many Reformed churches have since amended it.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

De Brès lived most of his adult life as a fugitive under the Habsburg heresy edicts (the 'placards') of Charles V and Philip II, which carried the death penalty. He was hunted after the Tournai disturbances of 1561 and captured and hanged after Valenciennes fell in 1567.

Excommunications

No individual papal excommunication of de Brès is recorded; as a Protestant minister he was treated by the Catholic and Habsburg authorities as a heretic under the general heresy edicts and condemned on that basis in 1567.

September–December 1561

Hunted after the Tournai psalm-singing demonstrations

After Reformed believers in Tournai marched singing psalms ('chanteries') in September 1561, the regent's officials investigated; de Brès was identified as the author of the Confession, his lodging was searched, and his books and papers were seized and burned with an effigy of him. He fled to France and became chaplain at Sedan.[3][2]

December 1566–31 May 1567

Capture after the siege of Valenciennes and execution

Valenciennes, where Reformed services were held openly after 1566, was declared in rebellion and besieged by royal troops under Philippe de Noircarmes. When it surrendered on 23 March 1567, de Brès and his fellow minister Peregrin de la Grange fled, were captured at Saint-Amand, and were held at Tournai and then in a dungeon at Valenciennes. Condemned as heretics and leaders of rebellion, they were hanged on 31 May 1567. Accounts differ on how far de Brès had supported the town's armed resistance.[2][3][4]
