Martin Bucer

1491–1551 · Reformation

Also known as Martin Butzer

Former Dominican friar, reformer of Strasbourg, Regius Professor of Divinity at Cambridge

Reformer of Strasbourg who worked for unity among Protestants and with Catholics, mentored John Calvin, and ended his life in English exile. Under Mary I his body was dug up and burned as a heretic.

Born
11 November 1491 · Sélestat (Schlettstadt), Alsace[3][5]
Died
28 February 1551 · Cambridge, England[3][5]
Illness (tuberculosis and other ailments), in exile

What they did

  • Led the Reformation in Strasbourg for a quarter-century, building a church order, schools, and care for the poor that influenced Reformed churches widely.[3][4]
  • Worked tirelessly for agreement between the Lutheran and Swiss Reformations on the Lord's Supper, achieving the Wittenberg Concord with Luther (1536).[3][5]
  • Co-wrote the Tetrapolitan Confession (1530) for Strasbourg and three other cities.[3][5]
  • Hosted and mentored John Calvin in Strasbourg (1538–1541), whose later church order in Geneva drew on Bucer's model.[3][4]
  • Took part in talks with Catholic theologians, including at Regensburg (1541), seeking reunion of the Western church.[3]
  • With Luther and Melanchthon, gave secret approval in 1539 to the bigamous second marriage of Landgrave Philip of Hesse, which caused a scandal when it became known in 1540.[3][5]
  • In England, wrote On the Kingdom of Christ (De Regno Christi, 1550) for Edward VI and a critique of the 1549 Prayer Book that influenced its 1552 revision.[1][3]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Bucer generally preferred persuasion to force with religious dissenters, and won back some Anabaptists in Hesse through debate. But he supported the Strasbourg council's measures against Anabaptists and Spiritualists, advised Philip of Hesse to place harsh restrictions on Jews, and in his last book urged Edward VI to suppress false religion by law.

1533

Strasbourg synod against the sectarians

Took part

Against: Anabaptists and Spiritualists in Strasbourg, including Melchior Hoffman and Caspar Schwenckfeld

Bucer led the 1533 Strasbourg synod that condemned the teachings of Hoffman, Schwenckfeld, and other radicals, and pressed the city council to enforce a church order against them. Hoffman was imprisoned by the council and died in prison about ten years later; Schwenckfeld left the city; the council's decrees against Anabaptists threatened banishment.[3][4]

1538–1539

Advice on the Jews of Hesse

Called for it

Against: Jews in Landgrave Philip of Hesse's territories

In a memorandum for Philip of Hesse (the 'Judenratschlag'), Bucer and other Hessian ministers recommended that Jews be allowed to stay only under strict limits: no new synagogues, no disputing religion with Christians, exclusion from money-lending and restriction to menial work, and compulsory attendance at Christian sermons. Philip adopted a milder ordinance.[3]

1550

De Regno Christi: calls to suppress false religion

Called for it

Against: Catholic worship and religious dissenters in England

In the book he wrote for Edward VI, Bucer argued that a Christian king must abolish false worship and should punish those who obstinately spread false religion or blasphemy, with severe penalties for serious offenses.[1][3]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Bucer was excommunicated by his bishop in 1523, forced out of Strasbourg by the emperor's religious settlement in 1549, and after his death in England was condemned as a heretic under Mary I, his body dug up and burned.

Excommunications

Bucer was excommunicated by the Bishop of Speyer in 1523 and condemned as a heretic posthumously in 1557. The Catholic Church never lifted these sentences. The University of Cambridge publicly restored his honor in 1560 under Elizabeth I.

1523

Excommunicated and driven out of Wissembourg

As a married former friar preaching Reformation doctrine at Wissembourg (Weissenburg) in Alsace, Bucer was excommunicated by the Bishop of Speyer. He fled to Strasbourg, where, as the son of a citizen, he found protection.[3][5]

1548–1549

Forced out of Strasbourg by the Augsburg Interim

After defeating the Protestant princes, Emperor Charles V imposed the Augsburg Interim (1548), a religious settlement restoring much Catholic practice. Bucer refused to accept it, and the Strasbourg city council, under imperial pressure, dismissed him in March 1549.[3][5]

January–February 1557 (six years after his death)

Posthumous heresy trial; body exhumed and burned

During Mary I's restoration of Catholicism, commissioners sent by Cardinal Reginald Pole to visit Cambridge University tried Bucer and the Hebrew scholar Paul Fagius for heresy after their deaths. Their bodies were dug up and burned on Market Hill in Cambridge on 6 February 1557, together with their books.[2][3][5]
