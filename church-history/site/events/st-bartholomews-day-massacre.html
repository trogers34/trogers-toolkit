St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre

1572 · Reformation

The killing of Huguenot (French Protestant) leaders in Paris on royal orders, which turned into mass killings of Protestants by Catholic crowds in Paris and other French cities.

Began
Night of 23–24 August 1572[1][4]
Ended
Paris killings c. 24–29 August; provincial killings into October 1572[1][4]
Place
Paris, then about a dozen other French cities

Background

  • France had fought three religious wars since 1562; the Peace of Saint-Germain (1570) gave Huguenots limited toleration and brought their leader, Admiral Gaspard de Coligny, into the royal council.[4][1]
  • On 18 August 1572 the king's Catholic sister Margaret of Valois married the Protestant Henry of Navarre in Paris, drawing many Huguenot nobles to a city that was strongly Catholic and hostile to them.[4][2]
  • On 22 August an assassin, probably acting for the Guise family, shot and wounded Coligny.[4][2]

What happened

  • After a council meeting on 23 August, Charles IX authorized the killing of Coligny and other Huguenot military leaders, apparently fearing a Huguenot revenge for the attack on Coligny.[4][2]
  • Early on 24 August, St. Bartholomew's Day, the Duke of Guise's men killed Coligny in his lodging and threw his body from the window; Huguenot nobles lodged in the Louvre were killed in the courtyard.[4][1]
  • Members of the Paris militia and Catholic crowds then killed Protestants of every rank across the city for several days, despite royal orders to stop.[1][4]
  • From late August into October, killings followed in about a dozen cities, including Lyon, Orléans, Rouen, Meaux, Bourges, Toulouse, and Bordeaux.[4][2]
  • Henry of Navarre and the Prince of Condé were spared by converting to Catholicism under pressure.[4]

Key participants

  • Charles IX King of France; authorized the killing of the Huguenot leaders and took public responsibility
  • Catherine de' Medici Queen mother; most historians see her as central to the decision, though how far is debated
  • Henry, Duke of Anjou The king's brother (later Henry III); part of the decision
  • Henry, Duke of Guise Catholic leader; led the killing of Coligny
  • Gaspard de Coligny Admiral of France and Huguenot leader; killed
  • Henry of Navarre Protestant leader and the king's new brother-in-law; spared after converting (later Henry IV)
  • Gregory XIII Pope; did not order the massacre but celebrated it in Rome

Outcomes and significance

  • Estimates are about 2,000–3,000 dead in Paris and roughly 5,000 to 10,000 across France in all; some older and contemporary estimates go much higher.[4][2]
  • The killings started a fourth religious war and led Huguenot writers to develop theories of resistance to tyrannical kings, such as the Vindiciae contra tyrannos (1579).[4][3]
  • Many Huguenots converted or emigrated, and Protestantism in northern France never recovered its earlier strength.[4][1]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Responsibility is still debated, especially how far the massacre was planned in advance (most historians now say it was not) and Catherine de' Medici's personal role. All parties named below are recorded in contemporary sources.

24 August 1572

Killing of Coligny and the Huguenot leaders

The royal council's decision was carried out by the Duke of Guise's men and the king's guards. Charles IX told the Parlement of Paris on 26 August that he had ordered it to stop a Protestant conspiracy against the crown.[4][2][3]

24 August – October 1572

Mass killing of Protestants in Paris and the provinces

Parish militias and crowds, encouraged by Catholic preachers who had denounced the Protestants for years, killed men, women, and children. In some provincial cities local governors or councils took part or allowed it; in others officials protected Protestants.[1][4]

September 1572

Papal celebration of the massacre

Gregory XIII did not order the massacre. On hearing the news he held a Te Deum of thanksgiving, struck a commemorative medal, and later had the event painted by Giorgio Vasari in the Vatican's Sala Regia, accepting the French court's account of a foiled Protestant plot.[4][2]
