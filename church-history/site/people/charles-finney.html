Charles Grandison Finney

1792–1875 · Modern

American revivalist, theologian, and president of Oberlin College

Lawyer turned revivalist whose 'new measures' and teaching that revival is the result of using the right means made him the leading evangelist of the Second Great Awakening.

Born
29 August 1792 · Warren, Connecticut[3][5]
Died
16 August 1875 · Oberlin, Ohio[3][5]

What they did

  • Led revivals across upstate New York's 'burned-over district' and in eastern cities from 1824, including a 1830–31 campaign in Rochester that drew hundreds into the churches.[4][1]
  • Used 'new measures' such as the 'anxious bench' for those under conviction, protracted nightly meetings, direct personal appeals, and women praying aloud in mixed meetings, which conservative Calvinists criticized.[3][2]
  • Taught in his Lectures on Revivals of Religion (1835) that revival 'is not a miracle' but the result of rightly using means, a view that shaped later evangelism.[2][5]
  • Rejected the Calvinist doctrines of inherited guilt and human inability, teaching that people can and must choose to repent; critics such as Charles Hodge of Princeton attacked his theology as unorthodox.[3][5]
  • Taught 'Oberlin perfectionism', that Christians can attain entire sanctification in this life.[3]
  • Made Oberlin College, which admitted Black students from 1835 and women to degree courses from 1837, a center of evangelical reform and abolitionism.[3][5]
  • Opposed slavery from the pulpit and refused communion to slaveholders, though he put revival ahead of political abolitionism.[3]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Finney was never arrested or persecuted. He faced strong opposition from fellow ministers, including Lyman Beecher and Asahel Nettleton at the 1827 New Lebanon Convention and the Princeton theologians, and from people hostile to revivals, but no formal action was taken against him.

Excommunications

Finney was never excommunicated or tried. He left the Presbyterian Church for Congregationalism in 1836 of his own accord, having said he had not read the Westminster Confession closely before his ordination.
