Council of Chalcedon

451 · Early Church

Also known as Fourth Ecumenical Council

The council that defined Christ as one person in two natures, divine and human, using Pope Leo I's Tome, and that split the eastern churches.

Began
8 October 451[1][2]
Ended
1 November 451[1][2]
Place
Chalcedon, across the Bosporus from Constantinople (modern Kadıköy, Istanbul)

Background

  • In 449 a council at Ephesus under Dioscorus of Alexandria restored the monk Eutyches, who taught that Christ had only one nature after the incarnation, and deposed Flavian of Constantinople, who died soon after from mistreatment. Leo I called it a 'robber council' (latrocinium).[1][4]
  • After Emperor Theodosius II died in 450, the new emperor Marcian and Empress Pulcheria summoned a new council to reverse Ephesus 449.[1]

What happened

  • Around 350–500 bishops met, nearly all from the East; imperial officials presided over the sessions and controlled the agenda, with Leo's legates given the first place among the bishops.[1]
  • The bishops read and accepted Leo's Tome to Flavian; the minutes record the acclamation 'Peter has spoken through Leo'.[1][3]
  • On 22 October the council issued its definition: Christ is one person 'acknowledged in two natures, without confusion, without change, without division, without separation'.[2][1]
  • It deposed Dioscorus of Alexandria and restored bishops removed in 449, including Theodoret of Cyrrhus and Ibas of Edessa after they condemned Nestorius.[1]
  • Canon 28 gave Constantinople, as 'New Rome', privileges equal to Old Rome and second rank after it; Leo's legates protested, and Leo refused to confirm the canon.[2][1]

Key participants

  • Marcian and Pulcheria Emperor and empress who summoned the council; Marcian attended the sixth session
  • Leo I Bishop of Rome; his Tome became the basis of the definition, and his legates (Paschasinus, Lucentius, Boniface) led the bishops
  • Anatolius of Constantinople Bishop of the capital; supported canon 28
  • Dioscorus of Alexandria Bishop who presided at Ephesus 449; deposed
  • Theodoret of Cyrrhus Theologian deposed in 449 and restored at the council

Outcomes and significance

  • The Chalcedonian definition became the standard teaching on Christ for the Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and most Protestant churches.[4][2]
  • Much of Egypt, Syria, and Armenia rejected the council as a betrayal of Cyril of Alexandria's teaching; they formed the churches now called Oriental Orthodox (Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean), and the split has never been formally healed.[4][1]
  • Imperial attempts to reconcile the two sides, such as Zeno's Henotikon (482) and later monothelitism, failed and caused further schisms, including the Acacian schism between Rome and Constantinople (484–519).[1][4]
  • Rome's refusal of canon 28 marked an early stage in the rivalry between Rome and Constantinople.[1]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

The council was followed by decades of violence between supporters and opponents of its definition, with the imperial government enforcing it by force.

451–454

Exile of Dioscorus

After his deposition Dioscorus was banished by imperial order to Gangra in Paphlagonia, where he died in 454.[1][4]

457

Murder of Proterius of Alexandria

Proterius, the pro-Chalcedon bishop installed in Alexandria with military support, faced riots from the start. After Marcian's death, an anti-Chalcedon crowd killed him in 457, and Timothy Aelurus took his see.[1][4]
