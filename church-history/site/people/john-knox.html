John Knox

c. 1514–1572 · Reformation

Catholic priest turned Protestant preacher, minister of St Giles', Edinburgh, leader of the Scottish Reformation

Scottish preacher who survived the French galleys and exile to lead the Scottish Reformation, helping bring about the 1560 break with Rome and shaping the Presbyterian Church of Scotland.

Born
c. 1514 (older accounts give 1505) · Haddington, East Lothian, Scotland[3][5]
Died
24 November 1572 · Edinburgh, Scotland[3][5]

What they did

  • Led the Scottish Reformation. In 1560 the Scottish Parliament rejected the pope's authority, forbade the Mass, and adopted a Protestant confession of faith.[3][5]
  • Co-wrote the Scots Confession (1560) with five other ministers, the confession of faith of the Church of Scotland until it was replaced by the Westminster Confession in 1647.[3][5]
  • Co-wrote the First Book of Discipline (1560), which set out a reformed church order with kirk sessions and called for a school in every parish and care for the poor.[3][4]
  • Served as minister of St Giles' in Edinburgh from 1559/1560 until his death, the leading pulpit of the Scottish Reformation.[3][5]
  • As a royal chaplain to Edward VI, influenced the 1552 English Book of Common Prayer, including the "Black Rubric" explaining that kneeling at communion did not imply adoration of the bread and wine. He declined the bishopric of Rochester.[3][5]
  • Led the English exile congregation in Geneva (1556–1559), the community that produced the Geneva Bible (1560), though he was not one of its main translators.[3][5]
  • Wrote The First Blast of the Trumpet Against the Monstrous Regiment of Women (1558), arguing that rule by women was contrary to nature and God's law. It was aimed at Catholic queens, but it permanently angered Elizabeth I.[2][3]
  • Argued in his Appellation (1558) and other works that subjects and nobles may resist rulers who suppress true religion.[2][3]
  • Confronted Mary, Queen of Scots, in a series of face-to-face interviews (1561–1563), which he recorded in his History.[1][3]
  • Wrote the History of the Reformation in Scotland, published after his death.[1][5]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Knox was a galley slave, an exile, a man burned in effigy, an outlaw, and a defendant in a treason trial. He was never executed, and he died of natural causes in Edinburgh in 1572. His mentor George Wishart was burned for heresy in 1546.

Excommunications

Knox was a Catholic priest who became a Protestant. The Scottish Catholic bishops condemned him as a heretic in his absence in 1556 and burned his effigy. After the 1560 Reformation, the Catholic Church had no legal authority in Scotland, and the sentence was never lifted.

1 March 1546

His mentor George Wishart burned for heresy

Knox had become a follower and bodyguard of the Protestant preacher George Wishart, famously carrying a two-handed sword before him. Wishart was arrested, tried for heresy at St Andrews, and burned there. Knox went into hiding. Protestant conspirators murdered Cardinal Beaton three months later, though Knox was not involved.[1][3]

July 1547 – early 1549

Captured at St Andrews and made a galley slave

The castle at St Andrews, held by the Protestants who had killed Beaton, surrendered to a French fleet sent to aid the Scottish government. Knox was taken to France and forced to row in the French galleys for about nineteen months, which damaged his health for life.[1][3][5]

March 1555

Expelled from Frankfurt

Among the English Protestant exiles at Frankfurt, Knox led those who wanted a simpler service than the English Prayer Book. The rival group, led by Richard Cox, gave the city magistrates passages from Knox's writings that called Emperor Charles V an enemy of Christ, which could be treated as treason in an imperial city. The magistrates told Knox to leave.[3][4]

1556

Condemned in his absence; burned in effigy in Edinburgh

During a visit to Scotland, Knox was summoned to appear before the bishops in Edinburgh, but the summons was dropped when his supporters gathered. After he returned to Geneva, the Scottish church authorities condemned him as a heretic in his absence and burned an effigy of him at the Market Cross in Edinburgh.[1][3]

1559

Declared an outlaw by the regent Mary of Guise

When Knox returned to Scotland in May 1559 and his preaching set off the Protestant revolt, the regent Mary of Guise declared him a rebel and outlaw. Elizabeth I, offended by The First Blast of the Trumpet, refused to let him travel through England on his way home.[1][3][5]

December 1563

Tried for treason before the Privy Council

After two Protestants were arrested for disrupting a Mass at Holyrood Palace, Knox sent a letter calling Protestants to gather in Edinburgh for their trial. Mary, Queen of Scots, treated this as treason and had him tried before the Privy Council.[1][3]

1570–1572

Threatened during the civil war; leaves Edinburgh

In the civil war between supporters of the deposed Mary, Queen of Scots, and those of the infant James VI, Mary's party held Edinburgh Castle and the city. Knox, weakened by a stroke and a target as the king's party's leading preacher, was threatened. According to his secretary Richard Bannatyne, a shot was fired into his house. In May 1571 he was persuaded to leave for St Andrews.[3][4]
