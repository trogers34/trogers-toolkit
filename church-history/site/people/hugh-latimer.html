Hugh Latimer

c. 1487–1555 · Reformation

Also known as Bishop Latimer; one of the Oxford Martyrs

Reformation preacher and Bishop of Worcester; burned at Oxford with Nicholas Ridley

The most famous English preacher of Henry VIII's and Edward VI's reigns, known for blunt, homely sermons attacking clerical abuses and calling for social justice. Converted to reforming views at Cambridge by Thomas Bilney, he became Bishop of Worcester under Henry VIII but resigned over the Six Articles and was twice imprisoned. Under Edward VI he preached before the king and at Paul's Cross. Under Mary I he was condemned for heresy and burned at Oxford with Nicholas Ridley. He also preached at the burning of the Franciscan friar John Forest in 1538.

What they did

  • Became the leading popular preacher of the English Reformation; his vivid, colloquial sermons, such as the 'Sermon of the Plough' (1548), are among the best-known prose of the period.[2][3]
  • Attacked clerical neglect and 'unpreaching prelates', declaring that the most diligent bishop in England was the devil, who never left his diocese.[2][3]
  • Preached against enclosures, rack-renting, bribery, and the neglect of the poor before Edward VI and his court, a voice of the 'Commonwealth' reformers.[2][6]
  • As Bishop of Worcester (1535–1539) promoted preaching and the English Bible and attacked pilgrimages and relics, including the Holy Blood of Hailes, exposed in 1538.[3][4]
  • Supported Henry VIII's case for annulling his marriage to Catherine of Aragon and the break with Rome, gaining royal favour.[3][7]
  • Refused to resume a bishopric under Edward VI, preferring to preach; lived for a time in Cranmer's household at Lambeth.[3][6]
  • Became, with Ridley and Cranmer, one of the most celebrated Protestant martyrs, remembered through Foxe's account and the Martyrs' Memorial in Oxford.[1][7]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Latimer was himself repeatedly prosecuted, but under Henry VIII he preached at the burning of the Franciscan friar John Forest and asked to be the preacher. He later preached in support of Thomas Seymour's execution for treason (1549), a political rather than religious case. No other part in heresy trials is recorded.

22 May 1538

Sermon at the burning of John Forest

Took part

Against: John Forest, Franciscan friar who denied the royal supremacy

Forest, Catherine of Aragon's former confessor, was condemned for heresy and treason for rejecting Henry VIII's supremacy. Latimer wrote to Thomas Cromwell offering to preach at the execution and did so from a pulpit at Smithfield, urging Forest to recant; Forest refused and was burned, slowly, over the wooden Welsh image Derfel Gadarn. Henry VIII, Cromwell, and Archbishop Cranmer shared responsibility for the trial and sentence.[1][3][6]

Outcome: Forest was burned at Smithfield; the Catholic Church later beatified him (1886).

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Latimer was banned from preaching, excommunicated and confined by Convocation, imprisoned twice under Henry VIII, and finally imprisoned, tried, and burned under Mary I.

Excommunications

Latimer was excommunicated by Convocation in 1532 and absolved the same year after submitting. In 1555 he was condemned as a heretic by Cardinal Pole's commissioners; that sentence was never revoked by Rome. The Church of England commemorates him with Ridley on 16 October.

c. 1525

Banned from preaching in Ely diocese

Nicholas West, Bishop of Ely, forbade Latimer to preach in the diocese after hearing his reforming sermons; Cardinal Wolsey examined him and licensed him to preach anywhere in England.[1][3]

January–April 1532

Prosecuted before Convocation

Summoned by Bishop John Stokesley of London for a sermon at St Mary Abchurch, Latimer was examined by Convocation under Archbishop William Warham, refused at first to subscribe to articles on purgatory, saints, and images, was excommunicated and confined at Lambeth, and was absolved after he submitted, apparently with Henry VIII's intervention.[3][6]

July 1539–1540

Custody after resigning over the Six Articles

After Parliament passed the Act of Six Articles enforcing traditional doctrine, Latimer resigned the bishopric of Worcester and was held in custody, first in the Tower and then with Bishop Richard Sampson of Chichester, and forbidden to preach or to visit London, Oxford, Cambridge, or his former diocese.[3][4]

September 1553–16 October 1555

Imprisonment, Oxford disputation, trial, and burning

Summoned by Mary I's council in September 1553, he was imprisoned in the Tower, moved to Oxford in March 1554, and made to dispute on the Mass in April 1554 before commissioners led by Hugh Weston. After the heresy laws were revived, Cardinal Reginald Pole's commissioners, Bishops John White of Lincoln, James Brooks of Gloucester, and John Holyman of Bristol, tried him with Ridley at the Divinity School on 30 September–1 October 1555 and condemned him. He was burned with Ridley on 16 October.[1][5][3]
