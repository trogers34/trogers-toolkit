John Duns Scotus

c. 1266–1308 · Medieval

Also known as Blessed John Duns Scotus; the Subtle Doctor (Doctor Subtilis)

Scottish Franciscan philosopher and theologian

Franciscan theologian at Oxford and Paris, known as the 'Subtle Doctor', whose defense of the Immaculate Conception and original ideas on being, individuality, and the will made him one of the most influential medieval thinkers.

Born

What they did

  • Argued that Christ's redemption could preserve Mary from original sin in advance, the argument that made the Immaculate Conception defensible against Thomas Aquinas's objections; it was defined as Catholic dogma in 1854.[2][4]
  • Taught the 'univocity of being', that 'being' means the same when said of God and creatures, against Aquinas's doctrine of analogy.[2][3]
  • Developed the idea of 'haecceity' ('thisness'), the principle that makes each individual the unique thing it is.[2][3]
  • Introduced the 'formal distinction', a way of distinguishing aspects of one thing that are neither merely mental nor separate realities.[2][3]
  • Stressed the freedom of the will, in God and humans, over the intellect, an emphasis that shaped later Franciscan theology.[3][4]
  • Taught that the Son would have become incarnate even if humanity had never sinned (the 'absolute primacy of Christ').[2]
  • Founded 'Scotism', a school that rivaled Thomism for centuries; Renaissance humanists mocked his followers as 'Duns men', the origin of the English word 'dunce'.[4][3]
  • Was beatified by Pope John Paul II in 1993.[4]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Duns Scotus argued that a Christian prince could lawfully take Jewish children from their parents to be baptized, and could compel Jewish adults to accept baptism by threats, keeping only a small remnant of Jews. This went beyond the common view of his time, held by Aquinas, that such force was wrong. No record shows him taking part in any act of persecution.

c. 1300–1304

Argued for the forced baptism of Jewish children

Called for it

Against: Jews, especially Jewish children

In his commentary on the Sentences (Ordinatio IV, d. 4, q. 9) Scotus argued that a ruler may take young Jewish children from their parents and have them baptized, and may compel adult Jews to be baptized by threats and fear, reasoning that God's claim outranks the parents'. He proposed keeping a small number of Jews, for example on an island, to fulfill the prophecy of their conversion at the end of time.[5][4]

Outcome: The view was rejected by most theologians and by papal practice, which forbade baptizing Jewish children against their parents' will, though it was cited by later advocates of forced baptism.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Scotus was never arrested, but he was expelled from France for supporting the pope against the king.

Excommunications

Scotus was never excommunicated or condemned; his views were challenged by Dominican and other theologians but never censured by the church.

June 1303 – 1304

Expulsion from France

During Philip IV's conflict with Pope Boniface VIII, royal commissioners required the Paris Franciscans to support the king's appeal for a general council against the pope. Scotus was among some 80 friars who refused, and they were given days to leave France.[2][4]
