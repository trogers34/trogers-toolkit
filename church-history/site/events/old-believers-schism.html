Old Believers Schism

1652–1667 · Modern

Also known as The Raskol

The split in the Russian Orthodox Church over Patriarch Nikon's reforms of worship, which the church and state enforced with exile, torture, and burning, and which led thousands of Old Believers to burn themselves to death.

Began
1652 (Nikon becomes patriarch)[2][4]
Ended
1667 (anathemas of the Great Moscow Synod)[2][4]
Place
Muscovite Russia

Background

  • Patriarch Nikon, with Tsar Alexis I's support, set out to bring Russian service books and rituals into line with contemporary Greek practice, which differed from older Russian customs.[2][4]

What happened

  • The reforms changed the sign of the cross (three fingers instead of two), the spelling of Jesus' name, the number of alleluias, and the direction of processions; opponents saw them as abandoning the true faith.[2][1]
  • Nikon quarrelled with the tsar and left the patriarchal throne in 1658; the Great Moscow Synod (1666–67), attended by the patriarchs of Alexandria and Antioch, deposed him but upheld his reforms and anathematized the old rites and those who kept them.[2][4]
  • The Solovetsky Monastery refused the reforms and was besieged by the tsar's troops from 1668 to 1676; most of the defenders were killed when it fell.[2][4]
  • Archpriest Avvakum, the most famous Old Believer, wrote his autobiography in exile and was burned at Pustozersk in 1682.[1][2]

Key participants

  • Nikon Patriarch of Moscow who introduced the reforms
  • Alexis I Tsar who supported the reforms
  • Avvakum Petrov Archpriest and leader of the Old Believers
  • Feodosia Morozova Noblewoman and Old Believer who died in prison

Outcomes and significance

  • Millions of Russians remained Old Believers, splitting into priestly and priestless groups; they suffered legal penalties and double taxation under Peter I and later tsars until the 1905 Edict of Toleration.[3][4]
  • In 1971 a council of the Russian Orthodox Church lifted the anathemas of 1666–67 and declared the old rites equally saving.[4]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Church and state punished Old Believers severely for decades; the scale of the self-immolations is uncertain, but historians put the deaths in the thousands to tens of thousands.

1653–1682

Exile and execution of Avvakum and his companions

Avvakum was exiled to Siberia, defrocked and anathematized in 1667, and held for 15 years in an earthen pit at Pustozersk. On 14 April 1682 he and three companions were burned there by order of Tsar Fyodor III.[1][2]

1668–1676

Siege of the Solovetsky Monastery

Tsar Alexis's troops besieged the monastery for eight years; when it fell in January 1676, most of the monks and their supporters were killed.[2][4]

1670s–1690s

Laws against Old Believers and mass self-immolations

Decrees of 1684–85 under Regent Sophia ordered unrepentant Old Believers burned. Expecting the end of the world and facing arrest, many communities burned themselves to death in their churches rather than submit.[3][2]
