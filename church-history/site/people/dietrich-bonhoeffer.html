Dietrich Bonhoeffer

1906–1945 · Modern

German Lutheran pastor, theologian, and member of the resistance to Hitler

German theologian who opposed Nazism from 1933, led an underground seminary of the Confessing Church, joined the conspiracy against Hitler, and was hanged weeks before the end of the war.

Born
4 February 1906 · Breslau, Germany (modern Wrocław, Poland)[4][6]
Died
9 April 1945 · Flossenbürg concentration camp, Bavaria, Germany[4][5]
Executed by hanging on Nazi orders after a summary SS court-martial

What they did

  • Opposed Nazism from its first days, criticizing the 'leader principle' in a radio talk in February 1933 and arguing in 'The Church and the Jewish Question' (April 1933) that the church must help victims of the state, even if they were not Christians.[4][5]
  • Helped found the Confessing Church, which rejected the Nazi-aligned 'German Christians', and directed its illegal seminary for training pastors at Finkenwalde until the Gestapo closed it in 1937.[4][6]
  • Wrote Discipleship (1937), which attacks 'cheap grace' and calls for costly obedience to Christ, and Life Together (1939), on Christian community.[1][4]
  • Was moved by his time in the Black church in Harlem (1930–31) and later wrote about seeing history 'from below', from the perspective of the suffering.[5][4]
  • Worked with the resistance inside the Abwehr through his brother-in-law Hans von Dohnanyi, used church contacts abroad, including Bishop George Bell of Chichester (1942), to seek Allied support, and helped a group of Jews escape to Switzerland ('Operation 7', 1942).[4][5]
  • Knew of and supported the plans to kill Hitler; historians debate how directly he was involved.[4][5]
  • Wrote, in letters from Tegel prison, about a 'religionless Christianity' for a 'world come of age', published as Letters and Papers from Prison (1951) and widely influential after the war.[2][6]
  • Left his unfinished Ethics, published after his death.[4]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Bonhoeffer was restricted, banned, imprisoned for two years, and finally executed by the Nazi state.

Excommunications

Bonhoeffer was never excommunicated or formally disciplined by his church, though the official Nazi-aligned church leadership disowned the Confessing Church. After the war, West German courts in 1956 acquitted Huppenkothen and Thorbeck of murder over Flossenbürg, a ruling widely criticized; in 1996 a Berlin court formally declared the 1945 verdict void.

1936–1941

Bans and the closing of Finkenwalde

His right to teach at the University of Berlin was withdrawn (1936); the Gestapo closed the Finkenwalde seminary (September 1937) and arrested 27 of its former students; he was banned from Berlin (1938), from public speaking (1940), and from publishing (1941).[4][5]

5 April 1943 – 9 April 1945

Arrest, imprisonment, and execution

Arrested in 1943 in an investigation of Abwehr currency dealings used to smuggle Jews abroad, he was held at Tegel military prison. After the failed 20 July 1944 plot and the discovery of resistance files at Zossen, he was moved to the Gestapo prison on Prinz-Albrecht-Strasse, then Buchenwald and Flossenbürg. On Hitler's order that the Abwehr conspirators be killed, an SS summary court-martial condemned him on 8 April 1945, and he was hanged at dawn on 9 April with Wilhelm Canaris, Hans Oster, and others, two weeks before American troops reached the camp.[4][5]
