Desiderius Erasmus

c. 1466–1536 · Reformation

Also known as Erasmus of Rotterdam

Catholic priest and humanist scholar, editor of the Greek New Testament

The most famous scholar of the Renaissance, whose printed Greek New Testament became the basis for the Reformation's Bible translations. He criticized church abuses but stayed Catholic and broke publicly with Luther, so he was attacked from both sides.

What they did

  • Published the first printed edition of the Greek New Testament (1516), with a new Latin translation and notes. His later editions became the basis of the "Received Text" used by Luther, Tyndale, and the King James translators.[4][5]
  • In his third edition (1522) added the passage 1 John 5:7–8 known as the "Johannine Comma," which he had left out of earlier editions because he did not find it in Greek manuscripts.[4]
  • Wrote The Praise of Folly (1511), a satire of the corruption of clergy, monks, and theologians, and the Handbook of the Christian Soldier (Enchiridion, 1503), which called for inward, Bible-centered piety.[2][5]
  • Edited the works of Church Fathers, including Jerome (1516), Augustine, and others, making them widely available in print.[4][3]
  • Compiled the Adages, a growing collection of thousands of classical proverbs with commentary.[3][5]
  • Wrote On Free Will (1524), defending human free will against Luther, who replied with The Bondage of the Will (1525). The exchange marked his public break with the Reformation.[4][5]
  • Declined Pope Paul III's offer to make him a cardinal in 1535, citing his age and health.[3][5]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Erasmus generally urged persuasion and peace rather than force against heretics, and criticized the executions of his day. In replies to his Catholic critics in the late 1520s he conceded that the magistrate might execute heretics who were seditious, which historians read as a hardening or ambivalence. His letters also contain hostile remarks about Jews, such as "if it is Christian to hate Jews, here we are all Christians in abundance", but he did not call for action against them.

1527–1532

Conceded the death penalty for seditious heretics

Called for it

Against: Heretics judged seditious, including Anabaptists

Answering censures by Noël Béda and the Paris theology faculty, Erasmus wrote that heretics should first be corrected by teaching, but that the magistrate could put to death those who disturbed public order, much as thieves are punished. He kept insisting that error alone was not grounds for execution, so scholars disagree on how far this changed his earlier stance.[4][3]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Erasmus was never arrested or tried. He was attacked by conservative Catholic theologians, who accused him of preparing the way for Luther, and he lost Protestant friends after breaking with Luther. The Paris theology faculty formally censured his writings, the Spanish church investigated them, and after his death Pope Paul IV banned all his works. He also left Basel when it turned Protestant.

Excommunications

Erasmus was never excommunicated. He died a Catholic priest, and in 1535 the pope had offered to make him a cardinal. After his death, Pope Paul IV's Index (1559) treated him as a forbidden author and banned all his works, which is listed below.

1526–1531

Writings censured by the Paris theology faculty

The Faculty of Theology of the University of Paris (the Sorbonne), led by its syndic Noël Béda, condemned his Colloquies (1526) and later issued formal censures of many passages from his works, published in 1531.[4][3]

1529

Leaves Basel after the city turns Protestant

When Basel adopted the Reformation in 1529, its council abolished the Mass and images were destroyed in the churches. Erasmus, unwilling to be seen as approving, moved to Catholic Freiburg im Breisgau.[3][5]
