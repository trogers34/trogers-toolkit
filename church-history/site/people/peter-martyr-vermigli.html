Peter Martyr Vermigli

1499–1562 · Reformation

Also known as Pietro Martire Vermigli; Peter Martyr

Italian Reformed theologian; professor at Strasbourg, Oxford, and Zürich

A leading Italian Augustinian canon who embraced Protestant teaching, fled the newly refounded Roman Inquisition in 1542, and became one of the most respected Reformed theologians of his day. He taught at Strasbourg, at Oxford under Edward VI, where he helped shape the English Reformation and defended the Reformed view of the Lord's Supper, and at Zürich. His Loci Communes became a standard Reformed textbook.

What they did

  • Became one of the most influential Reformed theologians of the sixteenth century; his commentaries and treatises were collected after his death as the Loci Communes (1576), widely used as a Protestant textbook, especially in England.[5][6]
  • Defended a Reformed doctrine of the Lord's Supper, that Christ is truly received by faith but not physically present in the bread, in the 1549 Oxford disputation and later writings.[3][5]
  • Advised Cranmer on the English Reformation, influencing the 1552 Book of Common Prayer and working on the draft revision of church law (Reformatio legum ecclesiasticarum).[3][5]
  • Combined Aristotelian scholastic training with Reformed theology, helping to shape later Reformed scholasticism.[5][4]
  • Was a skilled Hebraist and wrote biblical commentaries on Judges, Kings, Romans, and 1 Corinthians, among others.[5][6]
  • Taught reformist ideas to his community at Lucca, many of whose members later left Italy for Protestant cities.[2]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Vermigli did not order or carry out persecution. Like most Reformers, he held that Christian magistrates should restrain heresy, and he worked on the draft English church-law code that proposed severe penalties for heretics; it was never enacted.

He served on the commission, led by Cranmer, that drafted the Reformatio legum ecclesiasticarum, which provided for heretics who refused to recant to be handed over to the civil authorities for punishment. Parliament never passed it.[3][5]

Outcome: Not enacted.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Vermigli fled Italy to escape the Roman Inquisition and was later forced out of England under Mary I; Catholic authorities in Oxford also exhumed and desecrated his wife's body.

Excommunications

Vermigli abandoned his religious order and the Catholic Church in 1542 and was treated by Rome as a heretic. His works were banned in the first class of the Index of Prohibited Books, which forbade all of an author's writings.

1542

Flight from the Roman Inquisition

Suspected of heresy for his teaching at Lucca, he was summoned to appear before his order's chapter at Genoa just as Pope Paul III refounded the Roman Inquisition. Fearing arrest, he fled Italy in August 1542.[2][1]

1553

House arrest under Mary I

When Mary I restored Catholicism, Vermigli was confined in Oxford and then in London under house arrest before being allowed to leave England, a release often credited to his status as a foreigner.[3][1]

1557

Exhumation of his wife's body

Commissioners sent by Cardinal Reginald Pole to Oxford had the body of his wife, Catherine Dammartin, who had died there in 1553, dug up from Christ Church cathedral and buried in a dunghill as the remains of a heretic's wife. Under Elizabeth I in 1561 her bones were reburied mixed with the supposed relics of St Frideswide.[3][5]
