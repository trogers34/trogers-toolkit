First Great Awakening

c. 1734–c. 1745 · Modern

Also known as The Great Awakening; the Evangelical Revival (in Britain)

A wave of revivals in the British American colonies, linked to the Evangelical Revival in Britain, which stressed personal conversion, divided churches into supporters and opponents, and shaped American evangelicalism.

Began
c. 1734 (Northampton revival)[2][1]
Ended
c. mid-1740s[2][4]
Place
British North American colonies; Britain

Background

  • Earlier local revivals had occurred under Theodorus Frelinghuysen in New Jersey (1720s) and Solomon Stoddard in Northampton; Presbyterians trained at William Tennent's 'Log College' in Pennsylvania spread revival preaching.[2][4]

What happened

  • Jonathan Edwards's Northampton revival (1734–35), described in his Faithful Narrative, became a model for revival across the colonies and in Britain.[1][2]
  • George Whitefield's tours from 1739 drew crowds of thousands, sometimes tens of thousands, from Georgia to New England, and spread the revival across denominational lines.[3][2]
  • Preachers stressed the 'new birth', a felt experience of conversion; some meetings saw crying out, fainting, and visions, which critics called 'enthusiasm'.[2][1]
  • Churches split between 'New Lights' (supporters) and 'Old Lights' (opponents): Presbyterians divided into Old Side and New Side synods (1741–58), and many New England congregations broke away as 'Separates', some becoming Baptists.[2][4]
  • The revival reached enslaved Africans and Native Americans, some of whom, like the Mohegan minister Samson Occom, became preachers.[2]

Key participants

  • Jonathan Edwards Leading preacher and theologian of the revival
  • George Whitefield Itinerant evangelist whose tours spread the revival
  • John Wesley Leader of the parallel Evangelical Revival in Britain
  • Charles Wesley Hymn writer of the Evangelical Revival in Britain
  • Gilbert Tennent Presbyterian revivalist
  • Charles Chauncy Boston minister and leading critic of the revival
  • James Davenport Radical itinerant preacher

Outcomes and significance

  • It shaped the evangelical emphasis on conversion and helped Baptists and later Methodists grow in America.[2][4]
  • Revivalists founded colleges to train ministers, including the College of New Jersey (Princeton, 1746), Brown (1764), and Dartmouth (1769).[2][4]
  • Some historians argue it weakened established churches and prepared the ground for religious liberty and the American Revolution; others question how unified a movement it was.[2]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Opponents used colonial law against radical revivalists, and Separates and Baptists were taxed and punished for refusing to support established churches.

1742–1750s

Connecticut's law against itinerant preaching

Connecticut's assembly passed a law allowing itinerant preachers to be expelled and ministers who preached in other parishes without permission to lose their salaries. Separates who refused to pay taxes for the established Congregational churches were fined or jailed.[2]

1742

Arrest and deportation of James Davenport

The radical itinerant James Davenport, who denounced ministers as unconverted, was arrested in Connecticut and deported, and later tried in Boston; both courts judged him mentally disturbed. In 1743 he led a bonfire of books and luxury goods at New London, which he later repented.[2]
