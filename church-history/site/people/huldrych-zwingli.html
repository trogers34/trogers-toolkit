Huldrych Zwingli

1484–1531 · Reformation

Also known as Ulrich Zwingli

Swiss Reformer, people's priest of the Grossmünster in Zürich

Leader of the Reformation in Zürich, founder of the Reformed tradition, and opponent of Luther on the Lord's Supper, who was killed in battle against the Catholic Swiss cantons.

Born
1 January 1484 · Wildhaus, Toggenburg (modern Switzerland)[3][6]
Died
11 October 1531 · Kappel am Albis, near Zürich (killed in battle)[3][6]

What they did

  • Led the Reformation in Zürich through preaching and public disputations, persuading the city council to adopt reform; it became the model for other Swiss cities.[5][3]
  • Began preaching straight through books of the Bible (lectio continua) at the Grossmünster in 1519, rather than following the set readings.[3][5]
  • Set out his program in 67 Articles for the First Zürich Disputation (1523), teaching that Scripture alone is authoritative.[1][4]
  • Oversaw the removal of images from Zürich's churches (1524) and the replacement of the Mass with a simple Lord's Supper (1525).[5][3]
  • Taught that the Lord's Supper is a memorial in which Christ's body is not physically present ('this signifies my body'), against Luther, which divided the Lutheran and Reformed churches.[4][6]
  • Founded the Prophezei (1525), a daily Bible study school that produced the Zürich Bible, published complete by Christoph Froschauer in 1531.[5]
  • Opposed Swiss mercenary service after serving as a chaplain at Swiss defeats in Italy.[3][6]
  • Married Anna Reinhart secretly in 1522 and publicly in 1524, in defiance of the rule of clerical celibacy.[3]
  • Supported the Zürich council's persecution of the Anabaptists, including his former followers Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz, which led to Manz's execution in 1527.[2][5]
  • Pushed Zürich toward force against the Catholic cantons, backing the food blockade of 1531 that provoked the Second Kappel War, in which he was killed.[5][3]

Anabaptists executed in Zürich in Zwingli's lifetime

The Zürich city council, not Zwingli, passed the sentences, but Zwingli led the disputations against the Anabaptists and supported the council's policy.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Zwingli was never arrested. His reforms were opposed by the bishop of Constance and the Catholic Swiss cantons, and he was killed in battle against them.

Excommunications

No papal excommunication of Zwingli by name is recorded. Pope Adrian VI even wrote to him courteously in 1523. Like Calvin's, all his works were later forbidden to Catholics.

1522–1523

Opposition from the bishop of Constance

After the Lenten fast was broken in Zürich, Bishop Hugo von Hohenlandenberg of Constance sent a delegation to the city to stop the innovations; at the First Disputation in 1523 his vicar general, Johann Fabri, argued against Zwingli. The city council rejected the bishop's authority.[5][3]

11 October 1531

Death at Kappel and the treatment of his body

Zwingli went with Zürich's army as chaplain and was killed when the forces of the five Catholic cantons defeated it. His successor Heinrich Bullinger reported that the victors quartered and burned his body and mixed the ashes with dung.[5][3]
