William Tyndale

c. 1494–1536 · Reformation

Scholar, Bible translator, reformer

English scholar who made the first printed New Testament in English, translated directly from the Greek, and whose wording carried through into the King James Bible; he was executed for heresy in 1536.

Born
c. 1494 (exact date unknown) · Gloucestershire, England, probably near Dursley[6][10]
Died
6 October 1536 (traditional date) · Vilvoorde, near Brussels (Habsburg Netherlands)[5][6]
Executed: strangled and burned as a heretic

What they did

  • Translated the New Testament into English directly from the Greek. It was the first New Testament printed in English (Worms, 1526); earlier Wycliffite versions were hand-copied translations from the Latin Vulgate.[1][6][10]
  • Aimed to put Scripture in the hands of ordinary people. Foxe reports him telling a learned opponent that, if God spared his life, he would cause "a boy that driveth the plough" to know more of the Scripture than he did.[5][6]
  • Worked in exile after failing to get a bishop's backing in England. His New Testaments were smuggled into England, where church authorities bought up and burned copies, including a public burning at St Paul's in 1526.[6][7]
  • Translated the first five books of the Old Testament (1530) and Jonah (1531) from Hebrew, the first English translations of those books made from the Hebrew.[6][10]
  • Wrote The Obedience of a Christian Man (1528), arguing for the authority of Scripture and for the obedience of subjects to their king rather than to the pope.[4][6]
  • Opposed Henry VIII's plan to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon in The Practice of Prelates (1530), costing himself the king's favor.[6][10]
  • Defended his translation choices and Reformation teaching in a printed debate with Sir Thomas More, including An Answer unto Sir Thomas More's Dialogue (1531).[6][10]
  • Introduced English words such as "Passover" and "scapegoat," along with many phrases still in common use (see Words and sayings below).[6][10]
  • Laid the foundation of later English Bibles: his work was the basis of Matthew's Bible (1537) and the Great Bible (1539). One study estimates that about 83% of the King James New Testament (1611) keeps his wording.[8][6]
  • Was condemned as a heretic and executed at Vilvoorde in 1536. Foxe records his last words as a prayer: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes."[5][6]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Tyndale supporting or carrying out persecution of others. He was himself hunted and executed as a heretic; his polemical writings attacked the papacy, the clergy, and Thomas More in harsh terms, but did not call for anyone to be punished.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Tyndale spent his last eleven years as a fugitive. He was hunted by English church and royal authorities, betrayed in Antwerp, tried for heresy under the Holy Roman Emperor's laws, and executed in 1536. People who read or distributed his books in England were also arrested, and several were burned.

Excommunications

No separate sentence of excommunication against Tyndale by name is recorded before his trial. His condemnation as a heretic in August 1536 cut him off from the Church under canon law, and he was formally degraded from the priesthood before being handed to the secular authorities.

c. 1522–1523

Accused of heresy before the diocesan chancellor

While Tyndale was tutor at Little Sodbury, local clergy who objected to his views reported him. He was called before the chancellor of the Diocese of Worcester, who, by the account Foxe preserves, threatened and abused him. No formal charge followed.[5][6]

1525

Printing of his New Testament stopped at Cologne

The Catholic controversialist Johann Cochlaeus learned that an English New Testament was being printed in Cologne. He alerted the city authorities through the senator Hermann Rinck, and the city council banned the printing.[6][7]

1526–1530

His New Testament banned and burned in England

When copies reached England, the Bishop of London banned the translation and preached against it at St Paul's Cross, where copies were burned in October 1526. The Archbishop of Canterbury arranged for copies to be bought up abroad so they could be destroyed. In 1530 a royal proclamation, issued after a meeting of bishops and scholars, banned Tyndale's New Testament and his other books.[6][7][5]

1529–1532

Arrest and burning of his readers and distributors in England

As Lord Chancellor, Sir Thomas More wrote at length against Tyndale and pursued people who owned or smuggled his books. Several were burned as heretics, among them Thomas Hitton (1530), Richard Bayfield (1531), and James Bainham (1532). Tyndale's brother John was fined in 1530 and paraded through London with the books he had distributed pinned to his clothing.[6][7][5]

1531–1532

Henry VIII seeks his return

Henry VIII was angered by The Practice of Prelates, which opposed the annulment of his marriage. He had his agent Stephen Vaughan try to persuade Tyndale to come back to England. When Tyndale refused, the king sought to have him handed over from the emperor's lands, according to Daniell through his ambassador Sir Thomas Elyot.[6]

May 1535

Betrayed and arrested in Antwerp

Henry Phillips, a young Englishman who had won Tyndale's trust, lured him out of the English House in Antwerp, where he was protected, and pointed him out to imperial officers waiting outside. Tyndale was taken to the state prison in the castle of Vilvoorde, near Brussels. Who paid Phillips is unknown. Historians have suspected English conservative clergy, including Bishop John Stokesley of London, but this has not been proven.[5][6][7]

August–October 1536

Heresy trial and execution

Tyndale was tried for heresy under Charles V's edicts against Lutheranism. The commission included theologians from the University of Louvain, who examined him and wrote against his views. He was condemned in August 1536, formally degraded from the priesthood, and handed over to the secular authorities, who strangled him and burned his body at Vilvoorde. No direct part by the pope (Paul III) is recorded: the emperor's government ran the case, with Catholic theologians acting as heresy examiners.[5][6]
