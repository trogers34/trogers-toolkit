Ignatius of Antioch

c. 35–c. 110 · Early Church

Also known as Saint Ignatius; Ignatius Theophorus ("God-bearer")

Bishop of Antioch; martyr

Bishop of Antioch who, while being taken under guard to Rome for execution, wrote seven letters that are the earliest evidence for the church being led by a single bishop with presbyters and deacons, and the first known use of the phrase "the catholic church." He is one of the "Apostolic Fathers," the earliest Christian writers after the New Testament (c. 90–150).

What they did

  • Wrote seven letters, to the churches at Ephesus, Magnesia, Tralles, Rome, Philadelphia, and Smyrna, and to Polycarp, while being taken to Rome to die.[1][3]
  • Gives the earliest clear evidence of a single bishop leading each church, assisted by presbyters and deacons, and urged the churches to stay united around their bishop.[3][4]
  • Is the first known writer to use the phrase "the catholic church" (meaning the whole or universal church), in his Letter to the Smyrnaeans.[1][3]
  • Opposed docetism, the teaching that Christ only seemed to have a real body and only seemed to suffer, insisting on Christ's true humanity, death, and resurrection.[3][4]
  • In his Letter to the Romans, begged the Christians at Rome not to try to save him from martyrdom, a text that deeply shaped later Christian ideals of martyrdom.[1][3]

  • The "long recension" and six additional letters Later expansions and forgeries[3][4]

    In the 4th century his seven genuine letters were expanded, and six more letters were written under his name. Scholars generally accept only the seven letters of the "middle recension" as authentic, though a minority question even those.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Ignatius was arrested in Antioch during the reign of Trajan, condemned, and sent under military guard across Asia Minor to Rome to be executed. His letters show he expected to die by wild beasts in the arena. No contemporary account of his death survives; tradition says he was killed by beasts in Rome.

Excommunications

No excommunication or condemnation of him is recorded.

c. 108–117

Arrested, condemned, and taken to Rome for execution

Ignatius was arrested in Antioch and condemned, apparently as a Christian leader. He was sent to Rome under a guard of ten soldiers, whom he called "ten leopards" because they treated him worse the better they were treated. On the way, Christians from the churches of Asia visited him, and he wrote his letters. Polycarp's Letter to the Philippians, written shortly afterward, assumes he had been martyred.[1][3][2]
