J. Gresham Machen

1881–1937 · Modern

Also known as John Gresham Machen

Presbyterian New Testament scholar; founder of Westminster Theological Seminary and the Orthodox Presbyterian Church

Princeton New Testament scholar who led the conservative side in the Presbyterian fundamentalist–modernist controversy, was suspended from the ministry by his church, and founded a new seminary and denomination.

Died

What they did

  • Wrote Christianity and Liberalism (1923), arguing that theological liberalism was not a form of Christianity but a different religion; it became the classic statement of the conservative case in the controversy.[1][3]
  • Taught New Testament at Princeton Seminary for 23 years and wrote scholarly defenses of traditional belief, The Origin of Paul's Religion (1921) and The Virgin Birth of Christ (1930).[3][5]
  • Wrote New Testament Greek for Beginners (1923), a textbook still used in seminaries.[3]
  • Served with the YMCA in France during the First World War (1918–19), working near the front lines.[2][3]
  • Founded Westminster Theological Seminary in Philadelphia in 1929 after the Presbyterian General Assembly reorganized Princeton Seminary to include a broader range of views.[3][4]
  • Founded the Independent Board for Presbyterian Foreign Missions in 1933, charging that the church's missions board tolerated missionaries who denied basic doctrines, in the wake of the report Re-Thinking Missions and Pearl S. Buck's comments on it.[3][4]
  • Founded the Presbyterian Church of America in June 1936 after his suspension; it was renamed the Orthodox Presbyterian Church in 1939 after a lawsuit by the larger church over the name.[3][5]
  • Held libertarian political views unusual among fundamentalists: he opposed Prohibition, a federal Department of Education, the proposed Child Labor Amendment, and compulsory Bible reading in public schools.[3]
  • Never married. He died of pneumonia on a speaking trip to North Dakota at 55.[2][3]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Machen supporting or carrying out persecution of others. His campaign against theological liberalism in the Presbyterian Church sought to keep liberals out of church office through church courts and argument, not civil penalties, and he was himself the one suspended from the ministry; in public affairs he defended civil and religious liberty, opposing measures such as compulsory Bible reading in public schools.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Machen was never arrested. His conflict was inside the Presbyterian Church in the U.S.A. (PCUSA), which suspended him from the ministry in 1935–36. Earlier, the 1926 General Assembly held up his promotion to Princeton's chair of apologetics, which he never received.

Excommunications

Machen was not excommunicated, but his church suspended him from the ministry, its most serious action against him.

1926–1929

Reorganization of Princeton Seminary

After conflict within the Princeton faculty and board, the General Assembly investigated the seminary and in 1929 replaced its board with a single new board including signers of the liberal Auburn Affirmation. Machen and several colleagues resigned rather than serve under it.[3][4]

1934–1936

Trial and suspension from the ministry

The 1934 General Assembly ordered all members to leave the Independent Board for Presbyterian Foreign Missions. Machen refused and was tried by the Presbytery of New Brunswick's judicial commission, which would not let him argue that the board's mandate was unlawful or that the missions board tolerated unbelief. He was convicted in March 1935 of disobeying his church and suspended; the 1936 General Assembly at Syracuse upheld the sentence.[3][4]
