Edward VI

1537–1553 · Reformation

Also known as Edward VI of England

King of England and Ireland (1547–1553)

Son of Henry VIII and Jane Seymour who became king at nine; under his regents England moved from Henry's Catholic-style church without the pope to a fully Protestant one, with the Book of Common Prayer, until his early death brought his Catholic half-sister Mary I to the throne.

What they did

  • His reign was the second swing in England's religious changes: Henry VIII had broken with Rome but kept Catholic worship; under Edward the English church became fully Protestant; under Mary I it returned to Rome; under Elizabeth I it became Protestant again.[3][5]
  • Under his regents Archbishop Thomas Cranmer replaced the Latin Mass with the English Book of Common Prayer (1549, revised in a more Protestant direction in 1552), allowed priests to marry, and issued the Forty-two Articles (1553), the basis of the later Thirty-nine Articles.[3][6]
  • In parish churches images, shrines, and wall paintings were removed or whitewashed, chantries that paid priests to pray for the dead were dissolved and their property seized, and stone altars were replaced by communion tables.[4][3]
  • Was educated as a convinced Protestant, and his journal and letters show him taking a personal interest in religious reform.[2][3]
  • Welcomed Continental Protestant refugees, including Martin Bucer and Peter Martyr Vermigli, who were made professors at Cambridge and Oxford, and allowed a church for foreign Protestants in London (1550).[3]
  • Conservative bishops who resisted the changes, including Stephen Gardiner of Winchester and Edmund Bonner of London, were deprived of their sees and imprisoned.[3][5]
  • Tried, as he was dying, to stop his Catholic half-sister Mary from succeeding by naming his Protestant cousin Lady Jane Grey in his 'Device for the Succession'; Jane reigned nine days before Mary took the throne.[2][6]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Edward himself was never persecuted. His government put down the 1549 Prayer Book Rebellion with great force: royal troops under Lord Russell and Lord Grey, including foreign mercenaries, killed several thousand rebels in Devon and Cornwall.

Excommunications

No pope excommunicated Edward by name. His realm was already outside the Catholic Church after Henry VIII's break with Rome, and Mary I reconciled it to Rome after his death.
