William Tyndale

c. 1494–1536 · Reformation

Scholar, Bible translator, reformer

English scholar who made the first printed New Testament in English, translated directly from the Greek, and whose wording carried through into the King James Bible; he was executed for heresy in 1536.

Born
c. 1494 (exact date unknown) · Gloucestershire, England, probably near Dursley[6][10]
Died
6 October 1536 (traditional date) · Vilvoorde, near Brussels (Habsburg Netherlands); strangled and burned as a heretic[5][6]

What they did

  • Translated the New Testament into English directly from the Greek. It was the first New Testament printed in English (Worms, 1526); earlier Wycliffite versions were hand-copied translations from the Latin Vulgate.[1][6][10]
  • Aimed to put Scripture in the hands of ordinary people. Foxe reports him telling a learned opponent that, if God spared his life, he would cause "a boy that driveth the plough" to know more of the Scripture than he did.[5][6]
  • Worked in exile after failing to get a bishop's backing in England. His New Testaments were smuggled into England, where church authorities bought up and burned copies, including a public burning at St Paul's in 1526.[6][7]
  • Translated the first five books of the Old Testament (1530) and Jonah (1531) from Hebrew, the first English translations of those books made from the Hebrew.[6][10]
  • Wrote The Obedience of a Christian Man (1528), arguing for the authority of Scripture and for the obedience of subjects to their king rather than to the pope.[4][6]
  • Opposed Henry VIII's plan to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon in The Practice of Prelates (1530), costing himself the king's favor.[6][10]
  • Defended his translation choices and Reformation teaching in a printed debate with Sir Thomas More, including An Answer unto Sir Thomas More's Dialogue (1531).[6][10]
  • Introduced English words such as "Passover" and "scapegoat," along with many phrases still in common use (see Words and sayings below).[6][10]
  • Laid the foundation of later English Bibles: his work was the basis of Matthew's Bible (1537) and the Great Bible (1539). One study estimates that about 83% of the King James New Testament (1611) keeps his wording.[8][6]
  • Was condemned as a heretic and executed at Vilvoorde in 1536. Foxe records his last words as a prayer: "Lord, open the King of England's eyes."[5][6]

Words and sayings

A selection of well-attested examples, not a complete list. Most reached modern English through the King James Bible (1611), which kept much of Tyndale's wording. "Original" is based on the earliest recorded use in the Oxford English Dictionary, or on comparison with the earlier Wycliffite Bible (translated from Latin in the late 1300s), which worded the same verse differently. New Testament verses are from his 1526/1534 translation; Old Testament verses are from his 1530 Pentateuch.

Original 17

First recorded in English in their writing, or first worded this way by them.

  • Passover Exodus 12:11 (1530)[2][11][6]

    Coined from "pass over" to render the Hebrew pesach; earlier English writers mostly used the borrowed word "pasch."

  • scapegoat Leviticus 16:8 (1530)[2][11][6]

    His rendering of the goat sent away into the wilderness on the Day of Atonement ("the goat that escapes").

  • mercy seat Exodus 25:17 (1530)[2][11]

    The cover of the ark of the covenant; modeled on Luther's German Gnadenstuhl.

  • Jehovah Exodus 6:3 (1530)[2][11]

    First use of this form of God's name in English, adapted from a Latin form of the Hebrew name.

  • long-suffering Galatians 5:22 (1526)[1][11]
  • busybody 1 Peter 4:15 (1526)[1][11]
  • "Let there be light" Genesis 1:3 (1530)[2][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible reads roughly "Light be made."

  • "Am I my brother's keeper?" Genesis 4:9 (1530)[2][9]
  • "Blessed are the peacemakers" Matthew 5:9[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible reads "Blessed be peaceable men."

  • "Judge not, that ye be not judged" Matthew 7:1[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible uses "deem" rather than "judge."

  • "The signs of the times" Matthew 16:3[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible reads "tokens of times."

  • "The spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak" Matthew 26:41[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible reads roughly "the spirit is ready, but the flesh is sick."

  • "Eat, drink, and be merry" Luke 12:19[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible reads roughly "eat, drink, and make feast."

  • "The powers that be" Romans 13:1[1][9]
  • "Fight the good fight" 1 Timothy 6:12[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible reads roughly "strive thou a good strife of faith."

  • "Filthy lucre" 1 Timothy 3:3[1][11]
  • "The patience of Job" James 5:11[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible reads "the sufferance of Job."

Popularized 6

Already in English before them, but their use made it widely known.

  • atonement Romans 5:11; 2 Corinthians 5:18 (1526/1534)[1][11][6]

    Already in English (recorded in Thomas More's writing about 1513), but Tyndale's use made it a central term of English theology. He used it in its literal sense of "at-one-ment," meaning reconciliation.

  • "Our Father which art in heaven, hallowed be thy name" Matthew 6:9[1][3][9]

    The Lord's Prayer was said in English long before Tyndale; the Wycliffite Bible reads "Our father that art in heavens, hallowed be thy name."

  • "The salt of the earth" Matthew 5:13[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible already reads "Ye be salt of the earth."

  • "Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find" Matthew 7:7[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible has nearly the same wording.

  • "In the beginning was the Word" John 1:1[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible has the same wording.

  • "In the twinkling of an eye" 1 Corinthians 15:52[1][3][9]

    The Wycliffite Bible already reads "in the twinkling of an eye."

Key dates

  1. c. 1494Born[6][10]
  2. 1512Takes his BA at Oxford (MA 1515)[6]
  3. c. 1522Tutor in the Walsh household at Little Sodbury, Gloucestershire[5][6]
  4. 1523Asks Bishop Cuthbert Tunstall of London to support a translation and is turned down[5][6]
  5. 1524Leaves England for Germany to translate in exile[6][10]
  6. 1525First printing at Cologne is raided; he escapes with part of the sheets[6]
  7. 1526Complete English New Testament printed at Worms[1][6]
  8. 1528Publishes The Obedience of a Christian Man[4][6]
  9. 1530Publishes the Pentateuch, translated from Hebrew[6][10]
  10. 1534Publishes his revised New Testament at Antwerp[1][6]
  11. 1535Betrayed by Henry Phillips; arrested in Antwerp and imprisoned at Vilvoorde[5][6]
  12. 1536Died[5][6]
