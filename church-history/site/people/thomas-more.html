Thomas More

1478–1535 · Reformation

Also known as Sir Thomas More; Saint Thomas More

Lawyer, humanist author, Speaker of the House of Commons, Lord Chancellor of England (1529–1532)

English lawyer, humanist, and author of Utopia who, as Lord Chancellor, vigorously pursued Protestant "heretics" and their books. He was himself executed by Henry VIII for refusing to accept the king as head of the Church of England, and was canonized by the Catholic Church in 1935.

What they did

  • Wrote Utopia (1516), a satire describing an ideal island society; the word "utopia" comes from this book.[4][7]
  • Was a leading English humanist and friend of Erasmus, who wrote The Praise of Folly at More's house. He gave his daughters, especially Margaret Roper, an education as full as any man's.[4][6]
  • Wrote The History of King Richard III, a main source for Shakespeare's portrait of Richard.[4][7]
  • Served as Speaker of the House of Commons (1523) and Lord Chancellor (1529–1532), the highest legal office in England, the first layman in that office for generations.[4][7]
  • Wrote extensive polemics against the Reformers: against Luther (1523), A Dialogue Concerning Heresies (1529), the Confutation of Tyndale's Answer (1532–1533), and the Apology (1533).[1][5]
  • As Lord Chancellor, led a campaign against Protestant books and the people who smuggled, owned, or spread them. Six people were burned for heresy during his chancellorship, and he defended these burnings in print (see the list of people below).[5][4][3]
  • Refused to swear the oath that rejected papal authority in the Act of Succession, and was executed for denying the king's supremacy over the church. His reported last words were that he died "the king's good servant, but God's first."[2][4]
  • Was canonized by Pope Pius XI in 1935 and named patron saint of statesmen and politicians by Pope John Paul II in 2000. He is also commemorated in the Church of England's calendar.[4][7]

People executed for heresy whose deaths are linked to Thomas More

These are the people executed for heresy whom More's critics, beginning with John Foxe, count as victims of his campaign against heresy. In every case a bishop condemned them and the secular authorities burned them; the executions themselves are fully documented. What is debated is More's own part. As Lord Chancellor (October 1529 – May 1532) he headed the Chancery, which issued the writs for burning heretics, and he publicly approved of these burnings in his writings. Foxe also accused him of having suspects whipped and racked; More denied using violence against heretics in his Apology (1533), and historians disagree. No surviving record shows More personally sentencing anyone to death. People he is accused of imprisoning or mistreating who were not executed, such as the London merchant John Petit, are not listed here. Richard Bayfield

Burned at Smithfield, London, 4 December 1531

Condemned by: John Stokesley, Bishop of London

What More is accused of: A former Benedictine monk who smuggled Protestant books into England. Foxe says More's searches tracked him down. More wrote about Bayfield's case at length and described his burning as deserved.[1][3][5][4]

More's close attention to the case and his approval are documented in his own writings. His direct role in the arrest rests mainly on Foxe, though many historians accept that More pursued book smugglers personally.

John Tewkesbury

Burned at Smithfield, London, 20 December 1531

Condemned by: John Stokesley, Bishop of London, as a relapsed heretic

What More is accused of: A London leather-seller. Foxe says More examined him at his house in Chelsea, had him tied to a tree in his garden ("the tree of truth") and whipped, and bound his head with cords until his eyes nearly burst.[3][1][5][6]

Execution: 100% verified Link to More: Disputed

The burning took place under More's chancellorship. The torture allegations come from Foxe; More denied in his Apology that he ever had heretics beaten, except for two cases unrelated to heresy trials. Historians are divided.

Burned at Smithfield, London, 30 April 1532

Persecution, arrests, and executions

More was imprisoned for over a year and executed by Henry VIII's government for refusing to accept the king's supremacy over the church. The evidence that convicted him was testimony that he himself called perjured.

Excommunications

More was never excommunicated. He died in communion with the Roman Catholic Church, which beatified him in 1886 and canonized him in 1935.

February–March 1534

Named in the attainder of the "Holy Maid of Kent"

More was included in a bill of attainder against Elizabeth Barton, the nun who had prophesied against Henry VIII's marriage to Anne Boleyn, and those said to have encouraged her. He defended himself before a committee of the king's council, and his name was removed.[2][4]

13–17 April 1534

Refuses the Oath of Succession; sent to the Tower

Summoned to Lambeth Palace, More said he would accept Anne Boleyn's children as heirs but would not swear the oath as written, because it rejected papal authority. He was committed to the Tower of London. In November, Parliament attainted him of misprision (concealment) of treason, taking his property.[2][4]

1–6 July 1535

Treason trial and execution

More was tried at Westminster Hall under the new Treasons Act for denying the king's supremacy. The decisive evidence was the Solicitor General Richard Rich's account of a conversation with More in the Tower, which More said was perjured. The jury convicted him in about a quarter of an hour. The sentence of hanging, drawing, and quartering was changed by the king to beheading, carried out on Tower Hill on 6 July.[2][4][6]
