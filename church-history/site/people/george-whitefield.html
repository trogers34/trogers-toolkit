George Whitefield

1714–1770 · Modern

Also known as pronounced "Whitfield"

Anglican priest and itinerant evangelist; leading preacher of the Great Awakening and Calvinistic Methodism

English evangelist whose open-air preaching on both sides of the Atlantic made him the most famous preacher of the 18th century and a central figure in the Great Awakening.

What they did

  • Preached in the open air to crowds of thousands, beginning with coal miners at Kingswood in 1739, and persuaded John Wesley to take up field preaching.[1][4]
  • Made seven preaching tours of the American colonies (13 Atlantic crossings) and was the best-known figure of the Great Awakening; he may have been heard by a majority of colonists.[3][4]
  • Preached thousands of sermons, perhaps 18,000 by his own reckoning, emphasizing the 'new birth'; Benjamin Franklin calculated that his voice could reach 30,000 people outdoors.[2][3]
  • Used newspapers and print to publicize his tours on a new scale; his friend Benjamin Franklin printed his journals and sermons.[4][2]
  • Led the Calvinistic wing of Methodism, which split from John Wesley in 1740–41 over predestination; the two men remained on friendly terms and Wesley preached his funeral sermon in London.[3][5]
  • Founded the Bethesda Orphanage near Savannah, Georgia (1740), and raised money for it throughout his life.[3][5]
  • Campaigned for slavery to be legalized in Georgia, which happened in 1751, arguing that the orphanage could not prosper without it; he bought enslaved people for its plantation and left about 50 to the Countess of Huntingdon at his death. In 1740 he had publicly criticized slaveholders' cruelty and urged that enslaved people be taught the gospel.[3][4]
  • Worked with Welsh and English Calvinistic Methodists such as Howell Harris and the Countess of Huntingdon, whose chapels became a separate denomination after his death.[3][5]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Whitefield was never imprisoned. Many Church of England clergy and bishops closed their pulpits to him and wrote against his 'enthusiasm'; Harvard's faculty published a testimony against him in 1744. He was formally suspended by a church court in South Carolina and was attacked by mobs in England and Ireland.

Excommunications

Whitefield was never excommunicated and died a priest of the Church of England. His only formal censure was the 1740 suspension in South Carolina.

July 1740

Suspended by the Bishop of London's commissary in Charleston

Alexander Garden, the Bishop of London's representative in the Carolinas, summoned Whitefield before a church court for preaching in dissenting meeting-houses and not using the Book of Common Prayer. Whitefield denied the court's authority and appealed; Garden suspended him from the ministry.[3][4]

July 1757

Stoned by a mob in Dublin

While preaching at Oxmantown Green in Dublin, Whitefield was attacked by a crowd, mostly Catholics angered by his anti-Catholic preaching, who stoned him and left him badly injured.[3]
