Kathryn Kuhlman

1907–1976 · Modern

Evangelist known for 'miracle services' and healing ministry; television preacher

An independent American evangelist whose 'miracle services' in Pittsburgh, Los Angeles, and elsewhere, where people reported being healed, made her one of the best-known healing ministers of the 20th century and a forerunner of the charismatic movement. She insisted that she healed no one and credited the Holy Spirit. Her healings were questioned by doctors, and her private life and finances drew criticism.

What they did

  • Held 'miracle services' for nearly thirty years, attended by thousands in Pittsburgh, the Shrine Auditorium in Los Angeles, and around the world, at which many people said they had been healed.[3][5]
  • Taught that she had no healing power of her own and that healing was the work of the Holy Spirit; she often named healings from the platform rather than laying hands on the sick.[1][3]
  • Was one of the bridges between Pentecostalism and the wider charismatic renewal, attracting Catholics and mainline Protestants; she met Pope Paul VI in 1972.[3][4]
  • Reached a national audience with the television programme I Believe in Miracles and with books such as I Believe in Miracles (1962) and God Can Do It Again (1969).[3][5]
  • Funded missions, church buildings, and relief work abroad through the Kathryn Kuhlman Foundation (established 1954).[3]
  • Influenced later healing evangelists, notably Benny Hinn, who attended her services and modelled his ministry on hers.[3][5]
  • Her ministry was dogged by questions: her marriage to a divorced man, which she later called a mistake; doubts from doctors about the healings; and, near the end of her life, a lawsuit by a former associate and disputes over her finances and will.[3][2]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Kathryn Kuhlman supporting or carrying out persecution of anyone. She avoided political and denominational fights and welcomed Christians of all traditions to her services.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Kuhlman was never arrested or persecuted. She faced public criticism and lawsuits rather than persecution.

Excommunications

Kuhlman was never excommunicated or disciplined by any church. She worked independently of denominations, though she was ordained by the Evangelical Church Alliance.

1974–1976

Medical criticism and lawsuits

The Minnesota surgeon William Nolen followed up people reported healed at one of her services and concluded that none of the serious organic illnesses he examined had been cured, and that some people had been harmed by abandoning treatment. In 1975 a former associate, Paul Bartholomew, sued her over money and alleged misuse of funds; the suit was settled. These were criticism and legal disputes, not persecution.[2][3]
