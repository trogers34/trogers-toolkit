Aimee Semple McPherson

1890–1944 · Modern

Also known as Sister Aimee; born Aimee Elizabeth Kennedy

Pentecostal evangelist, founder of Angelus Temple and the International Church of the Foursquare Gospel

Canadian-born Pentecostal evangelist who became one of the most famous preachers in America in the 1920s and 1930s. She built Angelus Temple in Los Angeles, used radio and stage-like 'illustrated sermons', founded the Foursquare Church, and ran large relief work in the Depression. Her career was shadowed by her 1926 disappearance, which she said was a kidnapping and prosecutors said was staged, and by family and legal disputes.

What they did

  • Founded the International Church of the Foursquare Gospel (1927), named for Christ as Saviour, Baptizer with the Holy Spirit, Healer, and Coming King; it is now a worldwide Pentecostal denomination.[2][5]
  • Built Angelus Temple (1923), one of the first American megachurches, and drew crowds with dramatic 'illustrated sermons' using costumes, sets, and music.[3][4]
  • Was an early Christian broadcaster: her station KFSG (1924) was among the first owned by a church and the first run by a woman evangelist.[3][2]
  • Ran the Angelus Temple commissary during the Great Depression, which handed out food and clothing to many thousands of people regardless of religion, and also ran a free employment service.[3][5]
  • Was one of the most prominent women preachers in American history and ordained women ministers in her church.[2][5]
  • Held healing services and interracial meetings, and drew support from Mexican Americans and other minorities in Los Angeles, at a time when segregation was common.[3][2]
  • Took public political stands: she supported Prohibition and the teaching of creation rather than evolution in schools, campaigned against communism, and promoted Christian patriotism in the 1930s and during the Second World War.[3]
  • Her career was marked by controversy: the unresolved 1926 disappearance, a public break with her mother and business partner Minnie Kennedy, lawsuits, a third marriage that her church opposed as remarriage after divorce, and her early death from a drug overdose.[3][4]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of McPherson supporting or carrying out persecution of anyone. She attacked evolution, communism, and liberal theology in sermons, and was later criticized for reports that Ku Klux Klan members attended her meetings in the early 1920s; she preached to them but there is no evidence she endorsed the Klan, and she held interracial services.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

McPherson was never imprisoned. She faced a criminal prosecution over her 1926 disappearance, which was dropped, and hostile attacks from some clergy and newspapers. She also faced lawsuits and public ridicule.

Excommunications

McPherson was never excommunicated. She held ministerial credentials with the Assemblies of God from 1919 and returned them in 1922 amid disagreements over church property and control; she was also briefly licensed by a Baptist church. Neither was a disciplinary condemnation.

July 1926 – January 1927

Prosecution over the 1926 disappearance

After she reappeared saying she had been kidnapped and held in Mexico, Los Angeles prosecutors concluded she had staged it, possibly to spend time with her married radio engineer Kenneth Ormiston, and charged her, her mother, and others with conspiracy to obstruct justice and manufacturing evidence. A preliminary hearing sent the case to trial, but the district attorney dropped the charges. What really happened has never been established.[3][4]

1920s–1930s

Attacks by rival clergy and the press

The Methodist preacher Robert 'Fighting Bob' Shuler and other Los Angeles clergy denounced her from the pulpit and on radio, and newspapers covered her private life intensely. This was opposition rather than persecution.[3][4]
