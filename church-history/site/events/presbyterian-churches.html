Presbyterian Churches: Beginnings and Splits

1560–2012 · Reformation

How Presbyterianism grew from Calvin's Geneva and Knox's Scottish Reformation into churches governed by elders, and how it divided in Scotland and America over revival, patronage, slavery, and theology.

Began
August 1560[1][3]
Ended
January 2012[2]
Place
Geneva, Scotland, England, and North America

Background

  • John Calvin organized the Geneva church under pastors, teachers, elders, and deacons; John Knox brought this model to Scotland after his exile in Geneva.[1][3]

What happened

  • Presbyterian churches are governed by elders in a series of courts (session, presbytery, synod, general assembly) rather than by bishops.[1][3]
  • The Westminster Confession and Catechisms (1640s) became the doctrinal standards of most English-speaking Presbyterians.[1][3]
  • American Presbyterians repeatedly split and reunited over revivalism (1741, 1810, 1837), slavery and the Civil War (1861), and liberal theology (1936, 1973), and more recently over ordination and sexuality.[1][2]

Key participants

  • John Calvin Reformer whose Geneva church was the model
  • John Knox Leader of the Scottish Reformation
  • Thomas Chalmers Leader of the 1843 Disruption
  • J. Gresham Machen Founder of the Orthodox Presbyterian Church
  • R. C. Sproul Theologian and minister in the PCA

Outcomes and significance

  • Presbyterian and Reformed churches, through the World Communion of Reformed Churches and other bodies, number tens of millions of members worldwide, with large churches in Korea, Africa, and Brazil.[3]

Violence, persecution, and who was responsible

Presbyterians were both persecuted and, where established, persecutors of others.

1660s–1688, especially 1680s

Persecution of the Scottish Covenanters

After the Restoration, Charles II and James VII imposed bishops on the Church of Scotland. Presbyterians who held outdoor services ('conventicles') were fined, imprisoned, transported, or killed; in the 'Killing Time' of the 1680s government troops shot suspects in the field, and in 1685 two women, Margaret McLachlan and Margaret Wilson, were drowned at Wigtown for refusing an oath.[3]
