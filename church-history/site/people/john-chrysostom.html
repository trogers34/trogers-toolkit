John Chrysostom

c. 349–407 · Early Church

Also known as Saint John Chrysostom ("golden-mouthed")

Preacher at Antioch, Archbishop of Constantinople

The greatest preacher of the early Greek church, whose sermons earned him the name "golden-mouthed." As Archbishop of Constantinople, he was deposed and exiled after clashing with the imperial court and a rival archbishop, and died on a forced march into exile.

Born
c. 347–349 · Antioch, Syria (modern Antakya, Turkey)[2][3]

What they did

  • Preached hundreds of sermons, verse by verse through books of the Bible, that are among the most widely read Christian writings of antiquity; the name "Chrysostom" ("golden-mouthed") was given to him after his death.[2][3]
  • Wrote On the Priesthood, a classic treatise on the responsibilities of ministry.[2][3]
  • Preached against the luxury of the rich and for care of the poor, and as archbishop cut his own household's expenses and funded hospitals.[1][2]
  • Reformed the clergy of Constantinople, disciplining corrupt clergy and monks.[1][2]
  • The main eucharistic liturgy of the Eastern Orthodox Church bears his name (the Divine Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom); its core prayer may go back to him, though the full rite developed later.[3]
  • Preached a series of eight sermons Against the Jews (Adversus Judaeos, 386–387) at Antioch, aimed at Christians who attended synagogue. Their harsh language was later used to justify hostility toward Jews.[2][3]
  • Is honored as a saint in all major traditions and as a Doctor of the Church in the Catholic Church.[3]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Chrysostom preached eight sermons at Antioch in 386–387 (Adversus Judaeos) with harsh rhetoric against Jews and Judaism, urging his congregation to restrain fellow Christians who attended the synagogue; they called for no violence against Jews but were later used in Christian anti-Jewish polemic. As bishop of Constantinople he sent monks to destroy pagan temples in Phoenicia and, according to Socrates, took churches from the Novatians and Quartodecimans; he also opposed letting the Arian Gothic general Gainas have a church in the capital. He taught that heretics should not be put to death, though their assemblies could be stopped.

386–387

Sermons against the Jews and Judaizing Christians

Called for it

Against: Jews of Antioch (rhetoric) and Christians who kept Jewish feasts or attended the synagogue

In eight sermons at Antioch, Chrysostom called the synagogue a theatre, a brothel, and a dwelling of demons, and urged his hearers to seize and bring to him Christians who joined Jewish fasts and feasts. He did not call for attacks on Jews; Wilken reads the sermons as rhetoric against Judaizing Christians in a competitive religious setting, but their language was widely quoted in later anti-Jewish writing.[4][5][2]

c. 399–400

Destruction of pagan temples in Phoenicia

Ordered or signed it

Against: Pagan shrines and worshippers in Phoenicia

Theodoret reports that Chrysostom, hearing that Phoenicians still kept pagan rites, gathered monks full of zeal and sent them to destroy the temples, paying the cost with gifts from wealthy Christian women rather than imperial funds. Imperial laws of the period already permitted the closing of temples; the monks carried out the demolitions.[6][2]

c. 401

Churches taken from the Novatians and Quartodecimans

Ordered or signed it

Against: Novatian and Quartodeciman congregations in Constantinople and Asia

The church historian Socrates, who was sympathetic to the Novatians, reports that Chrysostom took many churches away from the Novatians and Quartodecimans during his episcopate, notably during his visit to Asia in 401. Other sources say little, so the scale is uncertain.[7][2]

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Chrysostom was deposed twice and exiled twice by the eastern emperor Arcadius, at the urging of Archbishop Theophilus of Alexandria and with the support of Empress Eudoxia, whom he had offended. His supporters in Constantinople were persecuted after his exile. He died in 407 while soldiers were forcing him on a long march to a more remote place of exile. In 438 his body was brought back to Constantinople, and Emperor Theodosius II publicly asked forgiveness for his parents' treatment of him.

Excommunications

Chrysostom was deposed twice by synods of eastern bishops, in 403 and 404. Rome never accepted the sentences. After his death, his name was restored to the official lists (diptychs) of the churches of Constantinople and Alexandria in the 410s, which amounted to reversing the condemnation.

403

Deposed at the Synod of the Oak

Theophilus, Archbishop of Alexandria, came to Constantinople and held a synod at an estate called the Oak, near Chalcedon, packed with Chrysostom's opponents. It deposed him on a list of charges, and Emperor Arcadius exiled him.[1][2]

June 404 – September 407

Second exile and death on a forced march

After he preached against celebrations at the dedication of a silver statue of the empress near the cathedral, his enemies deposed him again, arguing that he had resumed his see without lawful reinstatement. Arcadius exiled him to Cucusus in Armenia. On the night he left, the cathedral burned, and his supporters (the "Johannites") were arrested and punished. In 407 the government ordered him moved to Pityus on the far shore of the Black Sea; he died on the way at Comana.[1][2][3]
